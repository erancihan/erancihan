## The Adder Is Finished. The Constants Are Not.

Chapter 13 closed with a sentence about horizons, and it was a description of what is left. `fp32_add4` exists: specified in ten numbered clauses, assembled from eleven chapters of proven parts, checked against two independent oracles, corner-tested, covered by a falsifiable bin model, and shipped with a mutation record. Chapter 13 then audited the language it is written in. There is no more hardware to build in this guide and no more language to survey.

What is left is the axis nobody moved. Everything from chapter 6 onward has been priced in one currency: eight exponent bits, a twenty-four-bit significand, bias 127, roundTiesToEven, and a four-input sum computed as three separate binary32 additions. Those are not laws of arithmetic. They are constants, and every one is a choice other people have made differently.

This chapter asks two questions about that axis, and delivers one artifact.

**What the guide declined.** Chapter 12's clause S3 rounds three times; clause S10 puts "the single-rounding four-input sum" explicitly out of scope. Chapter 12's "Seeds for Chapters 13 and 14" prices that exclusion, and chapter 10's "Accuracy Against the Correctly Rounded Sum" and "Rounding Once Instead of Three Times" carry the fuller tables. **Those numbers are not restated here** — they are recorded, and citing them by section title is more robust than copying them. What is not recorded anywhere in the guide is the other side: what the machines that round once are called, who built them, and what they cost.

**What moves if the constants move.** bfloat16 keeps the exponent field and discards sixteen significand bits. FP16 keeps IEEE's shape and shrinks the exponent instead. TF32 is nineteen bits wide with no storage form. FP8 arrives as two encodings, one of which deliberately breaks the all-ones-exponent convention. Posits discard the fixed exponent field entirely.

**The artifact** is the annotated bibliography: eighty-seven sources — sixty-two the guide already cites, twenty-five added here — each with what it is good for and the status of a real fetch attempt on a stated date.

One warning about strength, stated once and honoured in place. **Nothing in this chapter's literature sections was built or run here.** Chapter 11's "Timing, Fmax, and the Epistemic Wall" is the standing rule: no synthesis tool exists in this environment, so no area, latency, power or Fmax figure below is a measurement. Each belongs to the authors of its paper and is attributed on the spot. The guide's own measurements — one of which is new in this chapter — are marked as such and nothing else is.

## The Road Not Taken: Single-Rounding Multi-Operand Addition

Clause S3 fixes rounding as roundTiesToEven applied *per addition* — three roundings for a four-input sum — and clause S10 puts the one-rounding alternative out of scope. Chapter 10's "Rounding Once Instead of Three Times" describes what that alternative computes; chapter 10's "Accuracy Against the Correctly Rounded Sum" and chapter 12's "Seeds for Chapters 13 and 14" price the gap. What no chapter has is the other side of the ledger: **the machines that do round once.**

### The taxonomy, from a source that names the classes

Mikaitis, M., *Monotonicity of Multi-Term Floating-Point Adders* (arXiv:2304.01407, revised 4 December 2023 — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01407>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`; PDF text-extracted here) sorts hardware multi-term adders into four classes. The headings are quoted because the vocabulary is what chapter 10 lacked.

**"Class I: Adders that use long accumulators"**. Keep every bit in a fixed-point register wide enough for the whole format; round once at the end. "This is advocated by Kulisch", citing an implementation that "used 4288 bits internally for multiplying and accumulating binary64 values exactly" — with the warning that "keeping all of the bits can be expensive in circuit area and latency due to carry propagation." ARM's High-Precision Anchored accumulators sit here.

**"Class II: Adders that achieve correct rounding without the use of long accumulators"**. Tenca's fused three-term design is the archetype, and the summary will be instantly familiar: Tenca "proposes a fused design for performing fl(a+b+c) with only one rounding error, which complicates the problem in that bits that are shifted out in the significand alignment step have to be tracked." That is chapter 8's "Compare, Swap, and the Alignment Shift" — guard, round and sticky, and chapter 2's `align_sticky.v` — generalised past two operands.

**"Class III: Adders that replicate software behaviour"**. Units that deliberately keep the intermediate roundings so their bits match a software IEEE 754 sequence. **`fp32_add4` belongs here** — and the paper supplies, from graphics rather than pedagogy, the motivation chapter 12 reached independently, quoting Kim and Kim: "the exact bit-level matching between hardware units and software models is more important in 3D graphics than the rounding errors to the real value." Clause S3's argument in someone else's words, published earlier.

**"Class IV: Adders that use limited precision accumulator"**. Align to the maximum exponent, shift in *limited* precision, add with a couple of carry bits, normalise once — Kaul et al.; Lopes and Constantinides; Hickmann et al. The paper flags the vocabulary trap: such units get called *fused*, but since "only the products are exact, not the accumulation of them, this has a different meaning than the Class I/II designs". This is the class deployed matrix units belong to.

The headline result lands on this guide's operand count: "common techniques for performing multi-term addition with n≥4, without normalization of intermediate quantities, can result in non-monotonicity -- increasing one of the addends x_i decreases the sum s_n." **Four is this guide's n.**

### The price tag, in bits, derived rather than quoted

The literature quotes accumulator widths without deriving them. The derivation is four lines, run in `python3` here on 2026-08-21; it is **arithmetic, not a citation**. A single finite binary32 value spans from 2⁻¹⁴⁹ to just under 2¹²⁸: 128 − (−149) = **277 bits**. Summing *n* of them adds ⌈log₂ n⌉ carry bits and a sign bit, so *n* = 4 needs **280**. The guide's actual significand datapath is **28 bits** (chapter 8, "The Width Budget: 28 Bits"). Products double the span: binary32 **554**, binary64 **4196**, binary16 **80**.

**A fetch-verified primary source has those last three.** Uguen, Y. and de Dinechin, F., "Design-space exploration for the Kulisch accumulator", HAL preprint hal-01488916v2, 20 March 2017 — record page <https://hal.science/hal-01488916>, PDF <https://hal.science/hal-01488916/document>, both `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, PDF text-extracted here. Its **Table 1, "Accumulator sizes for the IEEE-754 formats"**, gives "binary16 (half) … 80 bits / binary32 (single) … 554 bits / binary64 (double) … 4196 bits". Same three numbers from field widths alone with no knowledge of the paper — the check that licenses the 277 and the 280 beside them, since nothing published states those.

The paper also supplies the folklore figure's provenance: "Kulisch even proposed [4] to add even more bits to absorbe [sic] possible temporary overflows, leading to 4288 bits of accumulator for double precision." So 4288 is 4196 plus 92 bits of headroom, *stated* rather than inferred; the analogous construction, with a smaller headroom parameter, gives about 640 for binary32 — the number folklore quotes, though no source fetched here states it, while the paper's own *minimum* is 554. And one cost figure from a real flow, the authors': against accumulating in binary64, "The Proposed 1 accumulator requires 5x the resources, but brings in a 30x latency improvement."

**The honest headline.** Single rounding for four binary32 operands, the Class I way, costs an alignment-and-accumulate datapath of about **280 bits** plus a leading-zero count and normalise over the same width — against **28 bits** shipped. An order of magnitude in the significand path, and a normalise stage whose depth grows with the accumulator rather than the format. Chapter 11's "Cutting at the Priced Seams" located this design's long paths; a 280-bit leading-zero counter lands on exactly those seams.

### What each class buys

**Class I buys exactness and order-independence by construction.** Everything chapter 10 measured about association order — "How Often the Shape Matters", "One Multiset, Three Answers", "'Both Orders Agree' Is Not an Oracle" — becomes vacuous, because there is one rounding and no association to choose. It also dissolves the intermediate-overflow pathology clause S3 documents, in which four finite operands produce a level-one `+inf`: a ±2¹²⁸-scale partial fits inside a 280-bit register.

**Class II buys the same answer at far less width**, by tracking shifted-out bits instead of keeping them — which is why Tenca's "complication" reads as familiar. The sticky bit *is* the two-operand case of that idea. **Class IV buys speed and area and gives up both exactness and monotonicity.** It is what shipped.

**Alignment is the cost centre.** Alexandridis, K. and Dimitrakopoulos, G., *Online Alignment and Addition in Multi-Term Floating-Point Adders* (arXiv:2410.21959, 29 October 2024 — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21959>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, text-extracted here): "Alignment is executed serially by identifying first the maximum of all exponents and then shifting the fraction of each term according to the difference of its exponent from the maximum one." Their fused align-and-add operator reports "area and power savings range between 3%–23% and 4%–26%, respectively" against their baseline, at 16, 32 and 64 inputs. **The authors' synthesis results**, quoted as documentation of what the frontier is working on.

## IEEE 754-2019 Clause 9.5: The Standard's Own Answer

There is a second road out of clause S10, and it does not widen the datapath at all. It adds an output port.

**The status of the standard governs this section.** IEEE Std 754-2019 is paywalled; its record page <https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/754/6210/> was fetched (`[verified but not quotable]`, HTTP 200, 2026-08-21) and serves a purchase page. This project has never read the 2019 clause text and **nothing below quotes it.** Everything is from the revision committee's public page, the proposers' paper, and a follow-up — all fetched and text-extracted here.

### What was added

The committee's background page, <https://754r.ucbtest.org/background/> (`[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`), states verbatim: "754-2019 was approved by IEEE Standards Board on 13 June 2019 and published in July 2019. David Hough was chair, Mike Cowlishaw was editor." Among the principal changes from 754-2008: "9.5 new augmented{Addition,Subtraction,Multiplication} are recommended", because "augmented addition, subtraction, and multiplication operations support building higher precision in software and support reproducible reductions on arrays, and new operations get and set NaN payloads. These recommended operations might be required in a future edition of this standard."

**Clause 9.5, recommended and not required, with the committee flagging a possible promotion.** That is the entire load-bearing claim about the standard, and it comes from the committee rather than the standard. Chapter 10 states the same status on the authority of the Wikipedia IEEE 754 article; **the committee's own page is the better anchor** and this chapter uses it.

### Why, from the proposers

Riedy, E. J. and Demmel, J., "Augmented Arithmetic Operations Proposed for IEEE-754 2018", ARITH-25 (2018), pp. 49-56. <http://www.acsel-lab.com/arithmetic/arith25/pdf/34.pdf> — `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, PDF text-extracted here. From the abstract:

> "These operations were included after three decades of experience because of a motivating new use: bitwise reproducible arithmetic. Standardizing the operations provides a hardware acceleration target that can provide at least a 33% speed improvements [sic] in reproducible dot product, placing reproducible dot product almost within a factor of two of common dot product."

The grammatical slip is in the source. The mechanism, from the introduction:

> "The first new operation is a variation of the well-known twoSum[2] operation, which takes two floating point summands x and y, and returns both their rounded sum h = round(x + y), and the exact error t = x + y − h (exception handling is discussed later). The letters h and t are chosen to stand for head (the leading bits of the sum) and tail (the trailing bits)."

And the precondition that makes the tail exactly representable, which is a statement about *this design's* specification: "For the error t to be exactly representable, the initial rounding must be to-nearest, with any tie-breaking rule and with gradual underflow."

**Gradual underflow is load-bearing, and the guide happens to satisfy it.** Clause S2 commits to exact subnormal handling with no flush-to-zero, and the paper states the converse: "Abrupt underflow, for example, breaks the exact transformation property of augmentedAddition." A flush-to-zero FPU cannot host these operations correctly.

### The rounding direction, and why it is not roundTiesToEven

The detail secondary summaries most often lose, and it matters because this guide's whole rounder is roundTiesToEven: "The operations rely on a new rounding direction, roundTiesToZero, for reasons explained in Section IV-A. The rounding direction is required for these operations and is independent of other rounding attributes." The rationale: three of the five 754-2008 directions break the exact-transformation property; an early draft used roundTiesToAway, but a user survey found cases it could not serve — the paper names the geometric predicates in Shewchuk's *Triangle* — so roundTiesToZero was introduced, "defined only in the recommended augmented arithmetic operations clause".

**The consequence.** Chapter 7's "Rounding: Five Attributes, One Default" enumerates five attributes; augmented operations add a sixth that exists nowhere else. `augmentedAddition` is therefore **not a mode switch on `fp32_add4`**: it is a different rounder plus a second 32-bit output port carrying the tail.

### The exceptional cases, where an implementer bleeds

Clauses S4 and S5 would need counterparts for all of these. Verbatim: "If roundTiesToZero(x + y) is zero, then both the head and tail have the same sign; h = t = roundTiesToZero(x + y)."; "if roundTiesToZero(x + y) overflows, both the head and tail are set to the same infinity, and in this case the operation signals inexact and overflow."; "If either operand is a NaN, augmentedAddition produces the same quiet NaN for both h and t."; and the flag rule, which is the opposite of a reader's guess: "This operation signals inexact only when roundTiesToZero(x + y) overflows; underflows and zeros are exact."

Clause S3 defines `inexact` as the OR across three component additions, and chapter 10's "Flags Under Composition" measures what that costs. The standard's answer for the augmented operation is a *different* answer to the same class of question, reached from the specification side rather than the measurement side. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions about one word.

### Does anything implement them?

**Nothing that could be verified here.** What *can* be cited is how the numerical-analysis community described the situation. Boldo, S., Lauter, C. Q. and Muller, J.-M., "Emulating round-to-nearest-ties-to-zero 'augmented' floating-point operations …", HAL preprint hal-02137968v1, 23 May 2019 — <https://hal.science/hal-02137968v1/file/Emulation-RN0-HalVersion.pdf>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`:

> "Obtaining very fast reproducible summation with that algorithm will certainly require a direct hardware implementation of these operations. However, having these operations available on common processors will certainly take time. The purpose of this paper is to show that, in the meantime, one can emulate these operations with conventional floating-point operations…"

The paper exists *because* the hardware does not, and says so. ### Back to the four-input adder

`augmentedAddition` is the standardised form of what chapter 10's "Rounding Once Instead of Three Times" describes: a two-input adder that also returns its own rounding error. Given it, a correctly rounded four-input sum stops being a datapath problem and becomes a software problem on top of hardware shaped much like this guide's — accumulate the tails, re-inject, round once. **So the two roads out of S10 are not the same size.** One widens the significand path from 28 bits to about 280. The other adds an output port and a sixth rounding direction.

## The Formats, Part One: bfloat16, FP16, TF32

**Method note, governing three sections.** Every number in the census tables below was computed here on 2026-08-21 by a short `python3` model that derives a format's census from its field widths `(ew, mw)` alone, in exact `Fraction` arithmetic. **It is illustrative python, not a shipped target** — no Verilog in it to compile, so `src/ch14/` does not carry it — and it is validated by reproducing three published tables it was never told about: chapter 5's E4M3 census, NVIDIA's two FP8 maxima, and the posit standard's Table 1. The tables are therefore *arithmetic*, and re-runnable in seconds. Its core:

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F

def census(ew, mw, has_inf=True, nans=None):
    bias = 2**(ew-1) - 1
    total, zeros, subs = 2**(1+ew+mw), 2, 2*(2**mw - 1)
    if has_inf:                                   # IEEE-shaped
        infs, nans, emax = 2, 2*(2**mw - 1), bias
        maxf = F(2**(mw+1) - 1, 2**mw) * F(2)**emax
    else:                                         # OCP FP8 E4M3 style
        infs, emax = 0, bias + 1                  # all-ones exponent is a normal binade
        maxf = F(2**(mw+1) - 1 - (1 if nans == 2 else 0), 2**mw) * F(2)**emax
    return dict(total=total, zeros=zeros, subs=subs, infs=infs, nans=nans,
                norms=total - zeros - subs - infs - nans,
                minsub=F(1, 2**mw) * F(2)**(1-bias), minnorm=F(2)**(1-bias), maxf=maxf)
```

Read `bias` and `ew` against `ulp(1)` and the family resolves into one sentence. **binary16 keeps IEEE's shape and shrinks the exponent; bfloat16 and TF32 keep binary32's exponent and shrink the significand.** Loss scaling, the E4M3/E5M2 recipe and the FP32 accumulator inside a tensor core all follow from which choice a format made.

### bfloat16: one decision, stated precisely

The Wikipedia article (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bfloat16_floating-point_format>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`; both strings confirmed by literal substring test against the fetched bytes):

> "This format is a shortened (16-bit) version of the 32-bit IEEE 754 single-precision floating-point format (binary32)"

> "It preserves the approximate dynamic range of 32-bit floating-point numbers by retaining 8 exponent bits, but supports only an 8-bit precision rather than the 24-bit significand of the binary32 format."

Note **"approximate"** — the article is careful and this chapter has to be too, because the census shows where the approximation lives. **Identical:** exponent width, bias, and therefore the minimum *normal* magnitude, 1.17549e−38 for both to every digit, because `emin` is the same number. **Not identical:** the subnormal floor — binary32 reaches 2⁻¹⁴⁹, bfloat16 stops at 2⁻¹³³, **sixteen binades shallower**, the same sixteen bits it gave up. **Also not identical:** the largest finite value, 3.38953e+38 against 3.40282e+38, because bfloat16's significand tops out at 2 − 2⁻⁷.

So the accurate sentence is *"the same exponent range as binary32"*, which is exactly true — not *"the same dynamic range"*, true only to within a rounding of the endpoints. The same species of precision as chapter 7's "Bias 127, and What the Missing One Buys", and worth insisting on, because the loose version is what most secondary sources print.

The empirical case for the trade is Kalamkar, D. et al., *A Study of BFLOAT16 for Deep Learning Training* (arXiv:1905.12322 — <https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12322>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`): bfloat16's range matches FP32's and conversion is trivial, so networks train to comparable quality without retuning where binary16 needs it. **The authors' claim about their own experiments** — a machine-learning result, not an arithmetic one.

Google's Cloud TPU bfloat16 page was fetched (requested <https://cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/bfloat16>, 200, redirecting to <https://docs.cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/bfloat16> — **cite the effective URL**). Its bytes were site navigation and no body text extracted here, so it is `[verified but not quotable]` and **nothing rests on it.**

### FP16 and TF32

binary16 makes the opposite trade: 11 bits of precision, three more than bfloat16, bought with a 5-bit exponent — largest finite value **65,504**, normal floor 6.1e−05. Gradients underflow a 5-bit exponent, which is why loss scaling exists. Chapter 5's "Verifying Floating Point Specifically" names binary16 as the guide's mid-size exhaustive target — "run binary16 overnight" — and the census is where that scheduling arithmetic comes from.

NVIDIA, "Accelerating AI Training with NVIDIA TF32 Tensor Cores" (<https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerating-ai-training-with-tf32-tensor-cores/>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`; all four strings confirmed verbatim):

> "TF32 mode in the Ampere generation of GPUs adopts 8 exponent bits, 10 bits of mantissa, and one sign bit."

> "As a result, it covers the same range of values as FP32. TF32 also maintains more precision than BF16 and the same amount as FP16."

> "TF32 is only exposed as a Tensor Core operation mode, not a type. All storage in memory and other operations remain completely in FP32, only convolutions and matrix-multiplications convert their inputs to TF32 right before multiplication."

> "rounds FP32 inputs to TF32, computes the products without loss of precision, then accumulates those products into an FP32 output"

**Two annotations.** "The same range of values as FP32" is the vendor's phrasing and is approximate exactly as bfloat16's is: computed here, TF32's largest finite value is 3.40116e+38 against 3.40282e+38 and its subnormal floor is 1.148e−41 against 1.401e−45. As a claim about the exponent it is fair; as a claim about the value set it is not. And **TF32 is 19 bits with no storage form**, so the "32" describes the interface rather than the datum — there is no TF32 memory layout for this guide's adder to target. The last quotation is the exact pattern the accelerator section is about: round the inputs down, multiply exactly, accumulate wide.

## FP8: E4M3, E5M2, and the Name That Means Two Things

### The primary source, and its one typo

Micikevicius, P. et al. (fifteen authors), "FP8 Formats for Deep Learning", arXiv:2209.05433, submitted 12 September 2022, last revised 29 September 2022. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05433> — `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, abstract extracted and substring-tested here:

> "In this paper we propose an 8-bit floating point (FP8) binary interchange format consisting of two encodings - E4M3 (4-bit exponent and 3-bit mantissa) and E5M2 (5-bit exponent and 2-bit mantissa). While E5M2 follows IEEE 754 conventions for representatio [sic] of special values, E4M3's dynamic range is extended by not representing infinities and having only one mantissa bit-pattern for NaNs."

**"representatio" is a typo in the arXiv abstract**, not a transcription error here, and it is reproduced deliberately: a citation checker who re-fetches will find it. The fifteen authors span NVIDIA, Arm and Intel — this is a cross-vendor proposal, not one company's format, which is why it went on to be standardised.

### The vendor documentation that is fetchable

NVIDIA, "Using FP8 with Transformer Engine", **Transformer Engine 2.3.0** documentation — <https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/transformer-engine-releases/release-2.3/user-guide/examples/fp8_primer.html>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, text extracted and substring-tested here:

> "The FP8 datatype supported by H100 is actually 2 distinct datatypes, useful in different parts of the training of neural networks: E4M3 - it consists of 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits and 3 bits of mantissa. It can store values up to +/-448 and nan . E5M2 - it consists of 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits and 2 bits of mantissa. It can store values up to +/-57344, +/- inf and nan . The tradeoff of the increased dynamic range is lower precision of the stored values."

The same page gives the hybrid recipe — E4M3 forward, where precision matters; E5M2 for gradients, where range does — and the FP16 background that explains the family: "while the dynamic range of FP16 is enough to store the distribution of the gradient values, this distribution may be centered around values too high or too low for FP16 to handle. Scaling the loss shifts those distributions (without affecting numerics by using only powers of 2) into the range representable in FP16." And the complication FP8 adds: "This makes the single loss scaling factor strategy, which worked for FP16, infeasible for FP8 training and instead requires using distinct scaling factors for each FP8 tensor."

### The collision, computed: this guide's E4M3 is not OCP's E4M3

Chapter 5 flagged this in a parenthesis — its minifloat is "an IEEE-*shaped* E4M3, with infinities; the OCP FP8 format of the same name has none, so its population differs." That was right, and this chapter's job is to cash it out:

| | chapter 5's IEEE-shaped E4M3 | OCP OFP8 E4M3, per the abstract's rule |
|---|---|---|
| exponent field all-ones | reserved for inf/NaN | **a normal binade** |
| zeros / subnormals | 2 / 14 | 2 / 14 |
| **normals** | **224** | **238** |
| infinities | **2** | **0** |
| NaN encodings | **14** | **2** (one mantissa pattern × two signs) |
| **max finite** | **240** | **448** |
| ulp(1) | 2⁻³ | 2⁻³ |

Two independent confirmations that the right-hand column is the real OFP8 rule, despite the specification being unreadable from here. **448 is exactly what NVIDIA's fetched documentation says**, and 448 = 1.75 × 2⁸ is precisely the all-ones exponent with mantissa `110` — the largest pattern below the two reserved for NaN. And **"one mantissa bit-pattern for NaNs" and "two NaN encodings" are the same statement**: one mantissa pattern (`111`) under the all-ones exponent, times two signs.

**E5M2 has no such collision.** It is IEEE-shaped, so chapter 5's model and OCP's E5M2 agree exactly: 2 zeros, 6 subnormals, 240 normals, 2 infinities, 6 NaNs, max finite **57,344** — precisely NVIDIA's published figure, reproduced by a model told only `(ew=5, mw=2)`. That agreement is the check that licenses the E4M3 column beside it.

**The lesson is a citation lesson, which is why it belongs here.** Two formats with the same name and identical field widths differ by **1.87×** in maximum value and by **twelve NaN encodings**, because one spends the all-ones exponent on numbers. A document that says "E4M3" without saying *whose* has said nothing checkable. Chapter 5 got it right in a parenthesis; this chapter promotes it to a rule: **name the standardising body, or name the field-reservation policy, every time.**

From the definitions and formats clauses:

- **The regime is variable-length and signed-unary:** "regime bits A posit bit field following the MSB that uses a form of signed unary encoding (as opposed to positional notation) to represent the regime." and "regime The power-of-16 scaling determined by the regime bits. It is a signed integer."
- **One exception value, not a family:** "NaR Not a real. Umbrella value for anything not mathematically definable as a unique real number." One encoding — against binary32's two infinities and 16,777,214 NaNs.
- **The quire is in the standard, at 16*n* bits:** "There is a quire format of precision 16n that is used to contain exact sums of products of posits of precision n." (the *n* is mathematical italic in the source, so a naive search for `16n` misses it.)
- **Table 1** gives, for posit32: fraction length "0 to 27 bits", minPos 2⁻¹²⁰, maxPos 2¹²⁰, quire precision **512 bits**, quire sum limit 2¹⁵¹.

### A decoder written here, which reproduces that table

This implements the decode from the standard's own field description — sign, two's complement for negatives, signed-unary regime, two exponent bits, the rest fraction. Like the census model it is **illustrative python, not a shipped target**; its point is that it reproduces Table 1 without being told the answers.

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
ES = 2                                             # fixed by the 2022 standard

def decode(bits, n, es=ES):
    """Return (value or None for NaR, fraction_bit_count)."""
    if bits == 0:          return F(0), 0
    if bits == 1 << (n-1): return None, 0          # NaR
    s = bits >> (n-1); x = bits
    if s: x = ((~x) + 1) & ((1 << n) - 1)          # two's complement
    b = format(x, '0%db' % n)[1:]                  # n-1 bits after the sign
    r0 = b[0]; k = 0; i = 0
    while i < len(b) and b[i] == r0: k += 1; i += 1
    if i < len(b): i += 1                          # skip the terminating bit
    regime = -k if r0 == '0' else k - 1
    e = int(b[i:i+es].ljust(es, '0'), 2); i += es
    fb = b[i:]; frac = F(int(fb, 2), 1 << len(fb)) if fb else F(0)
    val = (1 + frac) * F(2)**(regime * (2**es) + e)
    return (-val if s else val), len(fb)
```

Enumerated exhaustively for posit8 and posit16 — 2⁻²⁴/2²⁴ with 3 max fraction bits, and 2⁻⁵⁶/2⁵⁶ with 11 — while posit32's 27 is derived from the field layout (`n − 1 − 2 − es`) rather than enumerated: its 2³² patterns would take about thirteen hours through the decoder printed above. Table 1 says "0 to 3 bits", "0 to 11 bits", "0 to 27 bits" and the same two exponents. Every row agrees.

**The census from the same run** is the structural contrast with the earlier table. posit8 is 256 patterns = 1 zero + 1 NaR + 254 nonzero reals; posit16 is 65,536 = 1 + 1 + 65,534. binary16's 65,536 is 2 zeros + 2,046 subnormals + 61,440 normals + 2 infinities + 2,046 NaNs. Counting patterns that are *not* a nonzero real: **a posit spends 2; binary16 spends 2,050.** That is the "better closure and simpler exception handling" claim in counted encodings rather than adjectives.

**The taper.** For posit32 the fraction count falls by one at each regime step, because the regime field grows by one bit:

| regime *r* | magnitude range | regime bits | fraction bits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | [2⁰, 2⁴) | 2 | **27** |
| 1 | [2⁴, 2⁸) | 3 | 26 |
| 2 | [2⁸, 2¹²) | 4 | 25 |
| 3 | [2¹², 2¹⁶) | 5 | 24 |
| **4** | **[2¹⁶, 2²⁰)** | 6 | **23** ← binary32's precision |
| 5 | [2²⁰, 2²⁴) | 7 | 22 |
| 6 | [2²⁴, 2²⁸) | 8 | 21 |
| 7 | [2²⁸, 2³²) | 9 | 20 |

posit32 carries **four more fraction bits than binary32 near 1.0**, matches it in [2¹⁶, 2²⁰), and falls below above 2²⁰. Not a coincidence — an independent source below lands on the same threshold from the other direction.

### The claim, and the counterweight

**Gustafson, J. L. and Yonemoto, I. T., "Beating Floating Point at its Own Game: Posit Arithmetic", *Supercomputing Frontiers and Innovations* 4(2):71-86, 2017.** <https://superfri.org/index.php/superfri/article/view/137> — `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`. The DOI <https://doi.org/10.14529/jsfi170206> **was resolved on this machine today** and redirects there, so it is safe to print; the ACM DL mirror returned **403** and must not be cited. From the abstract:

> "they provide compelling advantages over floats, including larger dynamic range, higher accuracy, better closure, bitwise identical results across systems, simpler hardware, and simpler exception handling. Posits never overflow to infinity or underflow to zero, and "Not-a-Number" (NaN) indicates an action instead of a bit pattern. A posit processing unit takes less circuitry than an IEEE float FPU."

**Those are the designers' claims about their own format.** The counterweight is **de Dinechin, F., Forget, L., Muller, J.-M. and Uguen, Y., "Posits: the good, the bad and the ugly", CoNGA 2019, ACM, 10 pp.** — <https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~demmel/ma221_Fall20/Dinechin_etal_2019.pdf>, `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`, text-extracted here; HAL record <https://inria.hal.science/hal-01959581> and the CoNGA programme <https://posithub.org/conga/2019/programme> (both 200) corroborate venue and authorship. The author list includes **Jean-Michel Muller**, whose *Handbook of Floating-Point Arithmetic* this guide has cited since chapter 1: the criticism is not from outside the field.

**1. The golden zone.** "for Posit32, the same bound 2−24 is guaranteed for operations whose absolute result lies within[2−20,(2− 2−23)· 219)≈[ 10−6, 106]. This is the "golden zone" (in yellow on Fig. 1) where posits are at least as accurate as floats." (Spacing oddities are the PDF's.) **This independently confirms the crossover the decoder computed above** — one route from field layout run here, one from a published error analysis, both landing on 2²⁰. With the caution attached in the same paragraph: "It is very different to have a property that holds everywhere except in the corners, and to have a property that holds only in a small range."

**2. Arithmetic cost: comparable, not cheaper.** The paper "shows that posit and floats have comparable area and latency", because "Compared to IEEE floats, it trades the overhead of subnormal and other special cases for the overhead of the LDCs (Leading Digit Counters) and shifts in the conversion units." **That directly contradicts** "A posit processing unit takes less circuitry than an IEEE float FPU". This chapter presents both, attributed, and **does not adjudicate**: one is a design claim by the format's authors, the other a synthesis comparison by a different group, and this project has no synthesis tool to settle it with.

**3. Quire cost: large, and quantified.** "In our current implementation targeting FPGAs, summing two products of Posit32 in the hardware quire has more than 4x the area and 8x the latency of summing them using a posit adder and a posit multiplier. Although we do hope to improve these results, such factors should not come as a surprise: the 512 bits of the Posit32 quire are indeed 18x the 27 bits of the Posit32 significand." **The posit world's price tag for exactly what the earlier section priced for binary32, agreeing in shape.** When it is worth paying: "For large dot products, we strongly support Kulisch's claim that the exactness of the result justifies the hardware overhead." When it is not: "this does not extend to the claim that the availability of a hardware quire magically brings in a FMA or a sum of a few products with latencies comparable to those of non-quire operations."

**A four-input sum is "a sum of a few products".** That is the most directly relevant criticism in this chapter to this guide's own design decision, and it points where clause S10 already did.

**4. The quire's specification was incomplete — in 2019.** "The quire is not yet completely specified in the posit standard." The 2022 standard quoted above *does* specify it, so **date that criticism** rather than repeating it as current. The concrete reason for the dating rule.

**5. The alternative the paper actually recommends**, which is not "posits lose": "One viable alternative to posit implementation is therefore to use them only as a memory storage format, and to compute internally on the extended standard format" — with a warning familiar from chapter 8's "Why the Shortcuts Fail": "attempting to use this approach to implement standard posit operations may incur wrong results due to double rounding."

**6. Numerical analysis has to be rebuilt.** Constant-relative-error bounds are a *floating-point* property; posits have deliberately variable accuracy, so "Numerical analysis has to be rebuilt from scratch out of these formula." Chapter 6's "Where Fixed Point Runs Out" is where a reader met constant relative error as the thing floating point buys; posits sell part of it back.

## What Retargeting Would Cost This Design, Format by Format

### The starting point: there are no parameters

Chapter 5's "Verifying Floating Point Specifically" ends its exhaustive-testing argument with the plan chapter 12 was expected to carry out: *"So the plan this argument recommends: parameterise the adder on `EXP_W` and `MANT_W`, verify exhaustively at E4M3 in a second, run binary16 overnight, and only then instantiate at E8M23…"*

**That plan was not carried out.** Measured here on 2026-08-21 with `grep` over `src/ch09/*.v` and `src/ch12/fp32_add4.v`: **zero `parameter` or `localparam` declarations** in any of the eight RTL modules or in `fp32_add4.v` — the only two files under `src/ch09` that declare one at all are testbenches. So the honest answer to "what would this adder have to change" is, for **every** format here: **a rewrite, not a parameter override.** Not a `#(.EXP_W(8), .MANT_W(7))` away from bfloat16 — a pass over nine files.

That is not a defect and this chapter is not reopening it. Chapter 12 shipped a proven binary32 adder and never promised a generic one. Chapter 5 now carries the matching correction in place, dated 2026-08-21, under its own "Verifying Floating Point Specifically": it records what chapter 12 did instead, why (the design descends from chapter 8's algorithm module, whose widths are the 28-bit budget proved for binary32, and whose corner library, coverage bins and golden references are all binary32 artifacts, so parameterising after the fact would have reopened every one), and what it costs — every format retarget here is a rewrite, and the reduced-width verification strategy chapter 5 argues for was never exercised on the guide's own design. Take that argument on its merits, not on this guide's example. It also names an exercise the guide has earned: parameterise it, and use the E4M3 census above as the exhaustive oracle.

### The four modules that carry the format

Chapter 9 built the datapath as seven modules ("Seven Modules and a Top: The Port Map"). Retargeting concentrates in four of them.

**`fp32_unpack.v`** carries the hidden bit and the subnormal rule, and its header states the invariant it establishes: `// Invariant out: every finite operand is now (-1)^s * sig * 2^(e-127-23)`. Both the 127 and the 23 are written into that line and every format changes both. **posit32 deletes the invariant**: a posit has no fixed exponent field to unpack, so the regime must be *counted* — a leading-digit count before alignment, which is a new pipeline stage, not a changed constant.

**`fp32_screen.v`** carries infinities and NaN, and is the module **OCP E4M3 breaks hardest**. Its comment says infinity and NaN "share E = 255"; under OFP8 E4M3 there is no such sharing, the `inf + (-inf) → 7FC00000` case has no counterpart, and clause S5's NaN payload priority — a > b > c > d, measured — has two encodings left to prioritise among.

**`fp32_align.v` and chapter 2's `align_sticky.v`** carry the shift width. Narrower formats shrink it; E4M3's whole significand is four bits, so alignment saturates almost immediately. The single-rounding architectures blow it up to about 280. **The guard/round/sticky discipline itself is unchanged in every case** — the transferable part of chapters 2 and 8.

**`fp32_round_pack.v`** carries `assign ovf = (e_rnd > 9'd254);` and the packed `{sign, 8'd255, 23'd0}`. Both are format constants; **for OCP E4M3 the overflow test has no infinity to produce**, so the format's own saturation rule must be substituted — and that rule lives in a specification this machine could not fetch.

### The subnormal question, where the formats really differ

Clause S2 commits to exact subnormal handling with no flush-to-zero, and chapter 7's "The Flags an Adder Can Actually Raise" measured the consequence that let clause S4 omit the underflow flag *with a proof*.

**That proof is format-specific, and this chapter will not let it travel.** Its premise is a Sterbenz-style relationship between the format's precision and its subnormal ramp depth; it has to be re-derived per format, and this chapter did not re-derive it. **Nothing here asserts the omission carries to bfloat16 or E4M3.**

What does carry: **posits have no subnormals at all**, so the question does not arise — though de Dinechin et al. note the interoperation consequence, "a posit will never be converted to a subnormal, and that subnormals will all be converted to the smallest posit." And **the FP8 ramps are three and two bits deep**, which is why chapter 5's own measurement matters: at E4M3, 10.6 % of operand pairs involve at least one subnormal against 0.78 % for random binary32. Subnormal handling stops being a corner case and becomes the common path, inverting the testing priorities chapters 5 and 12 set for binary32.

### What does not change, which is the pedagogically important half

Everything chapters 8 through 12 established about *structure* survives every format untouched: align → add → normalize → round → pack and chapter 8's "The Algorithm: Ten Steps, Stated Once"; guard, round and sticky, and the rule that sticky excludes the other two; the reference-follows-the-wiring discipline and the `!==`-plus-X-guard equivalence sweep of chapters 9 and 10, neither of which contains a format constant anywhere; and the specification-first habit itself — clause S1's port-order clause exists because chapter 10 measured that the wiring is part of the arithmetic, which is true at every width.

One thing plausibly *does* change and this chapter may not assert it. Composition costs the same *kind* of thing in every format, and it is reasonable to expect a narrower format to pay *more* of it, since each rounding discards a larger relative slice. **That expectation was not measured, here or in anything fetched.** It is written down as an expectation and left as one of the two open experiments named below.

## Accelerators: Systolic Arrays, Tensor Cores, and Why Accumulation Dominates

**Everything here is documentation and other people's measurements**, per chapter 11's "Timing, Fmax, and the Epistemic Wall".

### The famous systolic array is not floating point

Jouppi, N. P. et al., "In-Datacenter Performance Analysis of a Tensor Processing Unit", arXiv:1704.04760, 16 April 2017 (ISCA 2017). <https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04760> — `[verified 2026-08-21, HTTP 200]`:

> "The heart of the TPU is a 65,536 8-bit MAC matrix multiply unit that offers a peak throughput of 92 TeraOps/second (TOPS) and a large (28 MiB) software-managed on-chip memory."

> "Despite low utilization for some applications, the TPU is on average about 15X - 30X faster than its contemporary GPU or CPU, with TOPS/Watt about 30X - 80X higher."

**Two annotations.** The canonical systolic-array accelerator paper is about **8-bit integer** MACs, not floating point: the array question and the format question are orthogonal, and running them together misleads. And the speedup figures are the authors' comparison against *2015-era contemporaries*, a clause they are routinely quoted without.

### The mixed-precision pattern, from a source that measured it

Fasi, M., Higham, N. J., Mikaitis, M. and Pranesh, S., "Numerical Behavior of NVIDIA Tensor Cores", MIMS EPrint 2020.10 — published as *PeerJ Computer Science* 7:e330 (2021). |---|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | Google TPU v2 | 128 × 128 × 128 | **bfloat16** | **binary32** |
| 2017 | Google TPU v3 | 128 × 128 × 128 | bfloat16 | binary32 |
| 2017 | NVIDIA V100 | 4 × 4 × 4 | **binary16** | **binary32** |
| 2018 | NVIDIA T4 | 4 × 4 × 4 | binary16 | binary32 |

And the sentence the paper quotes from NVIDIA's Volta white paper: "Tensor Cores operate on FP16 input data with FP32 accumulation. The FP16 multiply results in a full precision product that is then accumulated using FP32 addition with the other intermediate products for a 4× 4× 4 matrix multiply."

### Why accumulation dominates, and why the standard permits the looseness

The **multiply** is the cheap place to lose bits — each product rounds once — while the **accumulate** is expensive, since an *n*-term sum accumulates error across *n* roundings. That is chapter 10's subject at a different *n*.

The paper then makes the standards point this chapter most wants carried away. IEEE 754-2019's clause 9.4 covers *reduction* operations and — as the paper describes it, with the clause number as printed there — deliberately does not prescribe the order of partial sums, and permits a higher-precision internal format. It enumerates what is left open — "1) whether this internal format should be normalized … 2) which rounding mode should be used, and 3) when the rounding should happen" — and states the consequence:

> "These loose requirements can potentially cause the results computed with a given multi-operand addition unit to be significantly different from those obtained using other hardware implementations or a software implementation based on IEEE 754-compliant elementary arithmetic operations."

NVIDIA's own PTX documentation, quoted in the same paper, confirms the gap from the vendor side: "The accumulation order, rounding and handling of subnormal inputs is unspecified."

**That is the standards-level answer to a question chapter 12 answered by fiat.** Clause S3 defines the correct result *as* the three-rounding composition, in a specified association order, through specified ports. It had to, because the standard does not define it for reductions. The guide reached the right conclusion for the right reason; clause 9.4 explains why the conclusion had to be a decision.

### What a real accumulator turned out to be

Fasi et al.'s conclusions for the V100, verbatim — their "(new)" and "(confirmed)" markers say which findings were already known:

> "• Subnormal numbers in binary16 and binary32 are supported (new).
> • The binary16 products in (2) are computed exactly, and the results are kept in full precision and not rounded to binary16 after the multiplication (confirmed).
> • The five summands in (2) are accumulated starting with the largest in absolute value (new).
> • The additions in (2) are performed using binary32 arithmetic (confirmed) with round-toward-zero (new).
> • Only the final result of (2) is normalized; the partial sums are not, but the accumulator uses two extra bits for carries (new)."

with the internal width given explicitly: "the V100 has a format{3.23} (or{4.23} if 3 extra bits for carries are present as discussed in Section 3.1.4) and the T4 has a format {3.24} (or{4.24}) for computing the significands before the final normalization and rounding to{1.23} (the format of the significand for binary32)." (The missing spaces around the brace notation are in the extraction.)

**Read that against this guide's own numbers.** The V100's five-operand adder is a fixed-point accumulator roughly `{3.23}` to `{4.24}` wide — 26 to 28 bits — normalised **once**, rounded **round-toward-zero**, operands presented largest-first. Chapter 8's "The Width Budget: 28 Bits" derived 28 bits for a *two*-input binary32 adder. **A commercial five-operand unit's internal significand path is the same order of magnitude as this guide's two-operand one** — exactly what the taxonomy predicts, because the V100's unit is Class IV, not Class I. The 280-bit figure is what Class I would have cost, and nobody shipped Class I.

### The line back to the four-input composition problem

Fasi et al. close with a result that is structurally chapter 10's subject:

> "We can show that the lack of normalization causes the dot product in tensor cores—and most likely in any other similar architectures in which partial sums are not normalized …—to behave non-monotonically."

Their counterexample is five binary32 terms: c₁₁ plus four copies of 2⁻²⁴, where c₁₁ = 1 − 2⁻²⁴ gives 1 + 2⁻²³ and c₁₁ = 1 gives 1. **Increasing an input decreased the sum.** Mikaitis generalises it to any *n* ≥ 4 in units that skip intermediate normalisation.

`fp32_add4` is *n* = 4 and normalises every intermediate, because it is three real binary32 additions with a finished value on every wire between them. So it *should* be monotonic — and until this chapter that was an inference resting on monotonicity of correctly rounded addition, which nothing in this project had verified. The next section stops inferring.

And the honest symmetry. **The guide's four-input adder** rounds three times, is bit-reproducible against a software model, and misses the correctly rounded sum at the rates clause S3 records. **A tensor core's five-input adder** rounds once, uses a wide fixed-point accumulator, round-toward-zero and largest-first ordering — and is non-monotonic, and its behaviour was undocumented until four numerical analysts reverse-engineered it. Neither is "the right answer": they are different points on one trade, and this guide's contribution is that it **wrote its point down as a specification with measured prices** rather than leaving it, as the PTX manual does, "unspecified".

One further fetchable source: Markidis, S., Chien, S. W. D., Laure, E., Peng, I. B. and Vetter, J. ## Monotonicity: The One Thing This Chapter Measured for Itself

### The code decision

This chapter ships **one** Verilog target and **no new RTL**: `src/ch14/tb_mono4.v`, one `run` row in `src/ch14/targets.txt`, and a README carrying the mutation record.

"Should this chapter ship code at all?" was a live question. A literature chapter with a bibliography as its deliverable needs no listing to justify itself, and a decorative one would have been the first unearned artifact in fourteen chapters — which is why the three `python3` models above stay in the text labelled illustrative rather than being dressed up as targets. But one thing here is not literature: the fetched papers pose a specific question about *n* = 4 multi-term adders, and this guide owns an *n* = 4 multi-term adder with a property the literature says such units often lack. Answering it costs one testbench over existing modules and turns an inference into a measurement. Everything shipped is manifest-covered, compiles with zero `iverilog` output, self-checks, has been mutation-tested, and runs green under `bash run_all.sh ch14` and the full repository regression.

### The property, precisely

For non-NaN operands and a non-NaN result, replacing any one addend by the next binary32 value strictly above it must not decrease the result in the real-number ordering. Three details make that testable rather than merely sayable.

**"The next value above" has to be defined at the awkward places.** The shipped helper is a bit-pattern walk:

```verilog
  // next binary32 value strictly above w (finite or -inf input).
  // -0 -> smallest positive subnormal; negatives walk toward zero;
  // non-negatives walk up, with maxnormal + 1 ulp landing on +inf.
  function [31:0] nextup(input [31:0] w);
    begin
      if (w == 32'h8000_0000)      nextup = 32'h0000_0001;
      else if (w[31])              nextup = w - 32'd1;
      else                         nextup = w + 32'd1;
    end
  endfunction
```

`-0` steps to the smallest positive subnormal, not to `+0`, which is the same number; negative patterns step *down*, because sign-magnitude counts the wrong way; and `7F7FFFFF + 1` is `7F800000`, so one ulp off the largest finite value lands on `+inf` — correct, and deliberately allowed.

**"Does not decrease" needs a total order on bit patterns, and `-0` must not break it.**

```verilog
  // total order on non-NaN binary32, with -0 == +0
  function [31:0] ordkey(input [31:0] w);
    reg [31:0] v;
    begin
      v = (w == 32'h8000_0000) ? 32'h0000_0000 : w;
      ordkey = v[31] ? ~v : (v | 32'h8000_0000);
    end
  endfunction
```

Without the first line, `-0` sorts one unit below `+0` and a result that flipped the sign of zero would read as a violation when the two are numerically equal. Chapter 12's signed-zero clause — the result is `-0` only when all four operands are — is exactly the rule that makes this matter.

**NaN is unordered, and the bench says so rather than guessing.** A trial whose base result is NaN is skipped and counted. A trial whose base is *not* NaN but whose bumped result *is* gets its own counter and is **not** scored as a violation. That case is real and a directed vector pins it: with `a = 7F7FFFFF`, `b = +0`, `c = d = FF7FFFFF`, the pair `c + d` overflows to `-inf` while `a + b` is finite, so the sum is `-inf`; bump `a` by one ulp and `a + b` overflows to `+inf`, so the sum is qNaN. **One ulp on one addend took the result from `-inf` to NaN.** That is clause S3's intermediate-overflow pathology seen from the order side — and exactly the discontinuity a Class I accumulator does not have, because a ±2¹²⁸-scale partial fits inside a 280-bit register instead of becoming an infinity.

### The DUT, and the checker's own checker

The bench drives chapter 10's combinational `fp32_add4_tree`, not the pipelined `fp32_add4` — convenience, and free in strength, because chapter 12's `tb_add4_stream` proves the two bit-identical over tens of thousands of quadruples. **The result transfers to the shipped adder along a proven equivalence, not an assumption.**

Before any DUT vector runs, `task keyladder` walks twelve pinned patterns from `-inf` to `+inf`, requires each key to exceed the last **except** at the `-0`/`+0` pair where it requires equality, and requires `nextup` to move up the ladder at its three awkward points. A monotonicity bench with a broken comparator certifies anything: breaking `ordkey`'s negative branch — one character, `~v` to `v` — produces five ladder failures immediately, which was verified.

### Results

Shipped size, `NT=2500` per regime — 10,000 trials over four operand regimes (full-range normals, exponents clustered within ±10, within ±2, and a NaN-free special mix including both infinities and both zeros), about 7 seconds:

```
tb_mono4 census: 39605 ordered comparisons (11737 strictly up, 27868 unchanged),
23 NaN-born, 26 NaN-base trials skipped, 268 ports unbumpable, 6 directed
PASS tb_mono4 (39605 one-ulp increases, 0 non-monotonic)
```

Headline run, same binary at `-DNT=25000` — 100,000 trials, 68 seconds:

```
tb_mono4 census: 396225 ordered comparisons (117382 strictly up, 278843 unchanged),
203 NaN-born, 255 NaN-base trials skipped, 2552 ports unbumpable, 6 directed
PASS tb_mono4 (396225 one-ulp increases, 0 non-monotonic)
```

**396,225 one-ulp increases; zero decreases.** Plus six directed vectors pinning both results by exact bits — including D1, Fasi et al.'s own counterexample shape reduced to four terms: `(1 − 2⁻²⁴) + 2⁻²⁴ + 2⁻²⁴ + 2⁻²⁴` gives `1 + 2⁻²³`, and raising the first addend to exactly 1 gives `1 + 2⁻²³` again, because the level-one tie rounds to even. The shape that made a five-term tensor-core accumulator go backwards leaves this one flat. The census shape matters as much as the headline: **70.4 % of one-ulp increases move the four-input sum not at all** — the composition absorbing the perturbation, and the first hint that the property is weak.

### What the mutation record showed, which is not what was expected

Nine mutants, applied to scratchpad copies and never to `guides/four-input-fp-adder/src/`. All nine killed; the full table is in `src/ch14/README.md`, and the column that matters is *which mechanism* killed each.

**Six of nine were caught only by the six pinned directed vectors, with zero order violations** — sticky forced to 0 and to 1; `round_up` forced always-on and always-off; the level-two adder fed `sum_ab` twice so `c` and `d` are dropped entirely; mantissa bit 22 inverted. Every one is a badly wrong adder and every one is **perfectly monotonic**: truncation is monotone, always-round-up is monotone, and a function ignoring two of its arguments is monotone in those two by definition.

The other three are the honest counterweight, and an earlier draft of this section got them wrong — it claimed all eight non-M9 mutants were monotone, which the chapter 14 review disproved by re-running them. Re-measured here: inverting the result's sign bit reverses the order wherever the sum strictly increases, so it produces **11,744 errors** and the testbench's own inert-stimulus guard fires as well (`0 strictly up`); swapping result mantissa bits 22 and 23 produces **29 errors** including genuine non-monotonic pairs; and M9 — bit 1 inverted only inside a narrow exponent band — produces **358**. So the order property does catch a third of this set. The conclusion it cannot support is the stronger one: six badly wrong adders sail through it, which is the point.

**One mutant was caught by the monotonicity property alone**, and had to be built for it (M1 and M2 are caught by it too, but they are caught by the directed vectors as well) — result bit 1 inverted, but only when the result exponent lies in [200, 220], so the directed vectors, all at small exponents, never touch it. It produced 358 order violations in 39,605 comparisons while every directed vector passed. The order check is live and independently load-bearing; it is simply not sensitive.

Three required failures confirm the bench cannot pass vacuously. `-DNT=0` fires three guards, including an absolute floor that had to be added *in front of* the relative one — because `n_cmp < NT*4` is satisfied by `NT = 0`, the exact vacuity trap chapter 12's `targets.txt` records for `tb_equiv4`'s `NQ`. Deleting one directed vector fires the count guard; breaking `ordkey` fires the ladder. Two behavioural guards also stand: the NaN-born path must have fired, and at least one comparison must have strictly increased, or the bench reports an inert generator rather than a clean result.

### What may be claimed, and at what strength

**Measured:** over 396,225 one-ulp increases across four regimes, plus six pinned directed vectors including the literature's own counterexample shape, chapter 10's four-input tree — bit-identical to the shipped `fp32_add4` — never decreased its result when an addend increased.

**Not claimed:** that it is monotonic everywhere. This is a sweep, not a proof; the argument that would make it one (correctly rounded addition is monotone, composition of monotone functions is monotone, and every intermediate here is a correctly rounded binary32 value per chapters 9 and 10) is written down as an argument and was not formally checked. **Also not claimed:** that monotonicity is worth much on its own. The mutation record says the opposite as clearly as possible. It does not begin to replace chapters 9-12's equivalence sweeps, and `src/ch14/README.md` says so in the same words.

### First, the three ways an HTTP 200 lies

**97 URLs were requested from this machine on 2026-08-21** for this chapter's verification pass: **79 → 200, 9 → 403, 4 → 202, one → 418, one → 429, one → 404, and two failed at the TLS layer with no status at all.**

The interesting part is that **at least four of the seventy-nine 200s are failures.** A checker that reads status codes and stops passes every one. Three distinct mechanisms produce them.

**One — the off-host redirect.** `http://www.sunburst-design.com/papers/CummingsSNUG2000SJ_NBA.pdf` returns **200**. What it returns is `https://www.paradigm-works.com/technical-library?term=Nonblocking+Assignments+in+Verilog+Synthesis%2C+Coding+Styles+That+Kill%21` — a *search page* on another company's site. The whole domain does it. This is chapter 3's hazard, reproducing exactly, on the URL of the most-cited SNUG paper in this guide. **The 200 is a false green and the paper is not there.**

**Two — the consent or cookie wall.** `https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00607-010-0131-y` — Kulisch and Snyder, *Very fast and exact accumulation of products* — returns **200** at an effective URL that has grown `?error=cookies_not_supported&code=…`. The bytes are a cookie-error landing page.

**Three — the bot interstitial**, met while writing rather than while researching. A cold `curl` for `https://posithub.org/docs/posit_standard-2.pdf` returned **200 with `content-type: text/html`**: a seven-kilobyte "One moment, please…" page with a JavaScript reload. The real 138 KB PDF — the source half this chapter's posit section rests on — arrived only on a retry carrying the challenge cookie. **A checker that requests each URL once, cold, records this standard as fine, and would do so even if it had gone.**

The test that matters is not *what status came back* but *did the expected content arrive*. Every quotation in this chapter was confirmed by a literal substring test against fetched bytes for exactly that reason.

## Traps Beginners Fall Into

Every chapter has closed with the ways its own subject goes wrong. This chapter's subject is reading and citing other people's work.

**Treating an HTTP 200 as a verification.** It produced four false greens in this chapter's own bibliography, by three mechanisms — an off-host redirect to a search page, a cookie-consent landing page, and a bot interstitial served as `text/html` in place of a PDF. Check what arrived, not what the status line said: substring-test quotations against the fetched bytes, and confirm a PDF's first four bytes are `%PDF`.

**Treating a 403 as a dead link.** Six hosts refused this machine while being healthy on the open web, and one answered a *different* fetch path on the same machine in the same minute with a 200. The two paths disagree in both directions — `github.com` one way, `hal.science` the other — so a one-tool re-verification produces false failures whichever tool you picked.

**Quoting a format name without a standardising body.** "E4M3" names two formats that share field widths and differ by 1.87× in maximum value and by twelve NaN encodings. The same trap waits under "FP8", "TF32", and every posit paper published before March 2022, when the exponent field stopped being variable.

**Repeating a vendor's approximate phrasing as an exact one.** "The same dynamic range as FP32" is what a reader remembers; "the same *exponent range*" is what is true. Vendors are usually careful — the bfloat16 article says "approximate" — and the looseness gets added downstream, by people summarising.

**Citing the DOI you constructed rather than the one you resolved.** An unfollowed DOI is a guess with a respectable prefix. Two DOIs here resolve to publisher landing pages returning **202** and no document, which is why neither is asserted as the source's URL — both appear in their entries only as the record of what was tried. One resolves cleanly and is cited as such. The difference is entirely observational.

**Letting a format-specific proof travel.** Chapter 7 proved a binary32 adder can never raise the underflow flag and chapter 12 omitted the flag on that authority. The premise is a relationship between precision and subnormal ramp depth, and both move with the format.

**Measuring a weak property and reporting it as a strong one.** This chapter's own measurement is the example: monotonicity over 396,225 one-ulp increases sounds like a correctness certificate and certifies almost nothing, since six of nine deliberately wrong adders passed it. The number is worth having *with* the mutation table beside it, or it will be misread — including by whoever wrote it.

**Assuming a documentation URL is stable.** The Transformer Engine FP8 primer was reorganised and retitled between one reference to it and the next. Pin the version when the vendor offers one and quote from the pinned copy — or accept that the quotation has an expiry date you will not be told about.

## The Arc, and Where to Go Next

This is the end of the book, so it is worth saying what the book was.

Fourteen chapters ago it started with gates and truth tables and one full adder drawn badly enough that a mechanical column audit had to be invented to catch it. It ends with a pipelined, specified, proven four-input IEEE 754 binary32 adder, a verification kit whose every component has been seen to fail, and a bibliography whose every link carries a fetch status and a date. Between those two points, almost nothing was taken on authority.

That is the actual shape of the arc, and it is not "we built an adder". It is: **at every step, the guide asked what would falsify the claim, and then went and tried it.**

Chapter 2 compiled forty throwaway modules and found the folklore about `1 << 40` is wrong under context-determined widths. Chapter 3 asked how many of the fourteen classic beginner bugs the simulator reports and found the answer was one — and that races here are perfectly reproducible, so the standard advice to re-run and see never reveals one. Chapter 4 found a shipped testbench printing PASS against a design rebuilt with a missing flip-flop, and the project acquired the rule that has governed every chapter since: **no check ships until it has been seen to fail.** Chapter 5 turned that into machinery and discovered, in its own headline measurement, that re-seeding per trial had corrupted the number. Chapter 7 measured that a binary32 adder can never raise the underflow flag, so chapter 12 could omit it with a proof instead of a shrug. Chapter 8 wrote an invariant, ran a million pairs, and watched the sweep falsify it. Chapter 9 found that `!=` silently passes an undriven design, which would have made the central equivalence sweep vacuous. Chapter 10 killed the folklore that adder trees are more accurate, and showed that two structures agreeing proves nothing about either. Chapter 11 quantified how often an injected X quietly disappears. Chapter 12 retired the project's one outstanding debt with a proof, an attack, and a witness on a wire no output could reveal — then found its own coverage-bin guard had let two mutations through until the *library* was fixed. Chapter 13 audited a language and found much of what the textbooks teach is a parse error here, and a smaller, meaner fraction is accepted and silently does nothing.

This chapter did the same to the literature. Ninety-seven fetches, three mechanisms by which a 200 lies, two fetch paths disagreeing in opposite directions, one arithmetic standard readable for free and one not readable at all — and one measurement, because the fetched papers asked a question this guide's artifact was the right shape to answer.

**What a reader can do now that they could not at chapter 1.** Read an IEEE 754 bit pattern by hand and say what it is. Write a Verilog module that means what it says, and know which traps the compiler will not warn about. Build a testbench that can fail, and prove it can by breaking the design. Compose two correct units and know that composition is a specification decision, not a wiring convenience. Pipeline something without inventing an arithmetic bug in the reset. Write a specification down before verifying against it, and notice when a disagreement is really a specification dispute. And — this chapter's addition — read a paper, a standard and a vendor manual while keeping straight which of the three you are quoting.

**Where to go next**, in rough order of how well the guide has prepared you:

- **Parameterise the adder.** The guide argued for reduced-width exhaustive verification in chapter 5 and then did not do it, and chapter 5 now says so in its own text. `EXP_W` and `MANT_W` through nine files, an exhaustive E4M3 sweep as the oracle with the census above as the pinned expectation, then binary16 overnight. The exercise the guide most clearly earned and most clearly owes.
- **Run the two experiments named above** and not run here.
- **Build a Class II three-term adder.** Tenca's problem — tracking bits shifted out during alignment — is chapter 8's sticky bit with one more operand. The smallest real step toward single rounding, and you now have all the parts.
- **Get a simulator this environment does not have.** Chapter 13's Tier-3 layer is unlocked by tooling, not by knowledge.
- **Get a synthesis tool.** Every sentence in this guide about area, Fmax and timing is flagged as documentation, in eleven chapters, on purpose. That wall is a tooling wall. Knock it down and re-run chapter 11's claims.
- **Read entries 62, 65, 78, 80 and 81, in that order.** They are the five sources that would most change how you think about what chapters 8 through 12 built, and all five were fetchable from an ordinary machine on an ordinary day.

The adder is finished. The bibliography is checked. The last thing this guide has to say is what it has been saying since chapter 4, and it applies to citations exactly as it applies to testbenches: **what would have to be true for this to be wrong, and have you gone and looked?**
