## The Design Is Done. What Would the Other Language Have Bought?

Chapter 12 shipped the thing. `fp32_add4` is specified in ten numbered clauses, assembled from eleven chapters of proven parts, streamed against two independent oracles at the hundred-thousand-quadruple level, corner-tested from a python-pinned library, covered by a 105-bin model whose bin definitions are themselves verified, and closed with the project's one long-standing debt retired in writing. It is written in Verilog-2001 style throughout, compiled at `-g2012` because chapter 2 measured that Icarus gates syntax rather than system tasks and there is no reason to use a lower level.

Chapter 12's "Seeds for Chapters 13 and 14" then made a promise on this chapter's behalf: "Nearly everything in this chapter's kit is a hand-rolled version of something SystemVerilog names." That is a claim, and this guide does not ship claims. So this chapter is not a SystemVerilog tutorial. It is an **audit**, and it has one question:

> Take a file this guide actually shipped. Rewrite it in SystemVerilog. Compile both on the same simulator, drive both from the same stimulus, and measure what changed.

Four shipped artifacts were rewritten that way this session, and all four rewrites are in `src/ch13/` as regression targets, each compiled *beside* the original it replaces:

- Chapter 7's `fp32_fields.v` became a **packed struct with a typedef in a package** — 1,003,072 vectors, bit-identical.
- Chapter 9's `fp32_normalize.v` became **`logic` ports, one `always_comb`, and an `automatic function`** — 250,420 vectors, bit-identical.
- Chapter 11's valid pipe became **two `interface` bundles** wiring the real pipelined adder — 60,000 streamed cycles, 52,541 retired results, identical every cycle to a conventionally wired twin.
- Chapter 11's latency-deep circular scoreboard became a **queue** — 52,570 pairs against the real DUT, both DUT mutants killed.

The rest of the chapter is what those four rewrites, plus a matrix of about fifty compiled probes, actually measured. Some of it is a pleasant surprise: `always_comb` fixes a trap chapter 3 taught, on this very simulator, in five lines. Some of it is worse than the folklore: the flag that quiets the compiler's complaints about assertions deletes the assertions that *work*. And the chapter's climax is a piece of this guide's own methodology that survives the transition untouched, because clause 19 does not offer it.

Two ground rules. **The epistemic wall:** every "Icarus does X" sentence below is a compile-and-run result from this session with the diagnostic quoted verbatim; every "the standard says" or "other simulators would" sentence is documentation, flagged in place. `which verilator` returns nothing on this machine — **Verilator is not installed here** — and neither Vivado, Questa nor VCS exists in this environment, so chapter 3's enforcement story carries forward as documentation and does not get upgraded. **The harness:** chapter 13 adds fourteen targets and the full-repo regression stands at **122/122**, under the unchanged method — Icarus Verilog 13.0, `iverilog -g2012 -Wall`, zero-output compiles on every `run` target, every transcript produced this session, every number verified in `python3`.

## The Subset, Measured: One Matrix, Three Ledgers

Chapter 5's "Assertions, and What You Can Actually Run Here" established the method: take the constructs you want, compile each one at every language level the tool offers, and write down what happened. It did that for ten assertion-related constructs. This chapter extends the same discipline to the whole transition list — about fifty constructs, each a compiled-and-run probe at every level where the construct exists — `-g2005`, `-g2005-sv`, `-g2009` and `-g2012`.

One fact underwrites everything that follows, and every target in this chapter's manifest demonstrates it: a Verilog-2001 module and a SystemVerilog package compile in the **same** `iverilog` invocation. Icarus gates the language on `-g`, not on the file suffix, so `../ch07/fp32_fields.v` and `fp32_pkg.sv` sit side by side on one command line. That is what makes incremental migration possible at all — you are never forced to convert a design in one commit.

The first result collapses the table by half. **In every probe, `-g2005-sv`, `-g2009` and `-g2012` behaved identically** — same exit status, byte-identical diagnostics. Chapter 5's local finding — "Nothing changes between `-g2005-sv`, `-g2009` and `-g2012` for any construct tested" — is a property of the whole language subset, not just of assertions. The guide's standing advice — pick `-g2012` and stop thinking about it — survives the transition unchanged.

The second result is that the answers fall into three ledgers, and the middle one is the dangerous one.

**Ledger 1 — supported.** Compiles silently, runs with the semantics the standard describes. `logic`; the two-state types `bit`/`byte`/`shortint`/`int`/`longint`; all three `always_*` forms; packed structs and typedefs, including through ports; enums with `.name()`; packages with `import`; queues; dynamic arrays; `foreach`; the `string` type; size, type and sign casts; `.*` and `.name` port connections; `++`, `+=`, `do…while`; `void` functions with `return` and default arguments; `$urandom_range`; immediate `assert` and `assert … else`; interfaces as instantiated bundles. That is a genuinely useful language.

**Ledger 2 — rejected at compile time, loudly.** Loud, immediate, unmistakable. Concurrent assertions with a temporal operator; covergroups; unpacked structs; associative arrays; `inside`; `unique if`; interfaces as module *ports*; named assignment patterns; a queue of a packed struct. Every one of these fails at compile time and tells you so. This ledger costs you features; it does not cost you correctness.

**Ledger 3 — accepted and silently gutted.** This is the one to memorise, because nothing warns you:

- **Blocking `=` inside `always_ff`**, and **two `always_ff` processes writing one variable** — both compile clean at `-g2012 -Wall`, both run, zero diagnostics. The standard forbids the second outright. Chapter 3's verdict re-confirmed on 13.0.
- **An incomplete `if` inside `always_comb`** — no latch warning, and the value really latches. Under `always_latch` the behaviour and the silence are byte-for-byte identical, so the two keywords do not differ in the one respect they exist to distinguish. (An `always_comb` variable also written by an `initial` block is equally forbidden and equally silent.)
- **`unique case`'s overlap check** — the compiler prints `sorry: Case unique/unique0 qualities are ignored.` and then implements *half* of them: a no-match at run time really does warn; two items matching at once never does.
- **Immediate `cover`** — accepted, and inert. Measured side by side in one probe: `cover pass-statement ran 0 time(s); assert pass-statement ran 1 time(s)`. Chapter 5's matrix ticks immediate `cover` and its text already says it "produces nothing at all"; what this probe adds is that the *pass statement* specifically never executes, while `assert`'s does. The tick means "accepted", not "functional".

The full ~50-row matrix, with every verdict and diagnostic, is in `src/ch13/README.md`. What matters here is its shape: **Icarus 13.0 gives you almost all of SystemVerilog's *description* layer and almost none of its *verification-methodology* layer.** The things it rejects or guts — SVA, covergroups, associative arrays, `inside`, the strictness promises around `always_comb` — are precisely the things this guide hand-rolled in chapters 5, 9, 11 and 12. Those mechanisms were never a stylistic preference; they were the only option, and the matrix is the receipt.

One asymmetry deserves stating early, because it is the price tag on everything that follows. The shipped `src/ch07/fp32_fields.v` compiles silently at every level from `-g1995` to `-g2012` (measured). Its SystemVerilog rewrite dies below `-g2005-sv`, and this is what it says:

```
$ iverilog -g2005 -o /tmp/x.vvp fp32_pkg.sv
fp32_pkg.sv:25: syntax error
I give up.
```

Line 25 is the `package` keyword. The parser does not recover, the message names neither the construct nor the flag that would fix it, and the failure is per-file and total. Migration on this simulator is a one-way door: 2001-style sources run everywhere; SystemVerilog sources run on `-g2005-sv` and above or not at all. And the file extension does not help — the identical text in a `.v` file behaves identically at both levels, measured. Icarus keys the language off `-g` and nothing else.

## `logic` and `always_comb`: A Chapter 9 Stage, Rewritten and Swept

Start with the description layer, because that is where the transition is real and cheap.

Chapter 2's "Wires, Variables, and the Lie in the Name `reg`" spent several pages on a distinction that exists for historical reasons and costs beginners hours: `wire` versus `reg`, which one a continuous assignment may drive, which one a procedural block may drive, and why a `reg` is not a register. SystemVerilog's `logic` deletes the question. It is a four-state variable that either kind of driver may write — one continuous driver or one procedural process, not both — and the compiler now says so in the friendliest error Icarus owns. Compile a `logic` with a continuous driver at `-g2005`:

```
error: Variable 'a' cannot be driven by a continuous assignment/module.
     : This is allowed when SystemVerilog is enabled.
```

The diagnostic names the fix. That is the whole `logic` story: one keyword instead of two, and nothing else changes.

`always_comb`, `always_ff` and `always_latch` are the same idea applied to processes. Chapter 3's "Describing Combinational Logic Three Ways" taught `always @(*)` for combinational logic and `always @(posedge clk)` for sequential, and warned that the sensitivity list is a promise the author makes and the tool does not check. The SystemVerilog forms move the promise into the keyword: `always_comb` declares "this block is combinational", `always_ff` declares "this block is a register bank", `always_latch` declares "yes, I meant a latch".

Here is chapter 9's normalize stage — golden steps 6 and 7, the right-1 / none / left-N shifter with the subnormal stop — rewritten in that style. This is `src/ch13/fp32_normalize_sv.sv`, complete from the module header to the end of the `always_comb`, with only the file's header comment and the closing `endmodule` omitted:

```systemverilog
module fp32_normalize_sv
  (input  logic        eff_sub,
   input  logic [26:0] sum27,
   input  logic        s_in,       // alignment sticky, passing through
   input  logic [7:0]  e_big,
   output logic [23:0] nsig,
   output logic        ng,
   output logic        nr,
   output logic        ns,
   output logic [8:0]  e_norm);

  // Everything the function reads still arrives as an ARGUMENT. Chapter 9
  // made that a rule; chapter 13 measures the second reason for it, since
  // always_comb -- unlike @* -- follows dependencies INTO function bodies.
  function automatic logic [4:0] lzc26(input logic [25:0] v);
    lzc26 = 5'd26;
    for (int i = 0; i <= 25; i++)
      if (v[25 - i] == 1'b1 && lzc26 == 5'd26)
        lzc26 = 5'(i);
    return lzc26;
  endfunction

  always_comb begin
    logic        carry, right1;
    logic [25:0] frame, framel;
    logic [4:0]  lz, shl;
    logic [7:0]  e_lim, shl8;

    carry  = sum27[26];
    right1 = ~eff_sub & carry;
    frame  = sum27[25:0];
    lz     = lzc26(frame);
    e_lim  = e_big - 8'd1;
    shl8   = ({3'b000, lz} > e_lim) ? e_lim : {3'b000, lz};
    shl    = shl8[4:0];
    framel = frame << shl;

    nsig   = right1 ? sum27[26:3]       : framel[25:2];
    ng     = right1 ? sum27[2]          : framel[1];
    nr     = right1 ? sum27[1]          : framel[0];
    ns     = right1 ? (s_in | sum27[0]) : s_in;
    e_norm = right1 ? ({1'b0, e_big} + 9'd1)
                    : ({1'b0, e_big} - {4'b0000, shl});
  end

```

Read it against `src/ch09/fp32_normalize.v` and the differences are all syntax. Ports are `logic`. Every intermediate that was a module-scope `wire` is now a **block-local variable inside the `always_comb`** — a real readability gain, since `carry`, `frame`, `lz` and `shl8` are visibly scratch values of one computation rather than nets some other block might read. The leading-zero count is a `function automatic` with `for (int i ...)`, an explicit `return`, and a `5'(i)` size cast where the original wrote `i[4:0]`: chapter 2's "Width and Signedness: The Rules That Will Break Your Adder" argued that width conversions should be deliberate, and the cast says out loud what the part-select said by implication.

What did not change is the arithmetic. `e_norm` still carries its ninth bit for the reason chapter 9's header gives. And the measurement says so:

```
PASS tb_norm_equiv (250420 vectors, bit-identical)
```

The sweep instantiates the shipped module and the rewrite side by side from one stimulus and compares `{nsig, ng, nr, ns, e_norm}` with `!==` — chapter 9's "Composition, and the Golden-Equivalence Sweep" discipline, because `!=` would pass an undriven design. Directed stimulus covers all 26 single-bit frames crossed with carry, `eff_sub`, the sticky input and the exponent corners, plus the all-zero frame; 250,000 random vectors follow, seeded once. The rewrite computes the same bits.

**And it does not compile silently.** Ten diagnostics, all of one kind:

```
fp32_normalize_sv.sv:57: sorry: constant selects in always_* processes are not fully
supported (the process will be sensitive to all bits in 'sum27[26:0]').
```

One per constant bit- or part-select read inside the block: six naming `sum27`, three naming `framel`, one naming `shl8`. Two isolating probes locate the cause exactly. The **same** select inside a plain `always @*` compiles with zero output. The **same** select inside an `always_ff` compiles with zero output. The message belongs to `always_comb`'s own sensitivity-inference engine and to nothing else, and its parenthesis states the fallback: the process becomes sensitive to the whole vector instead of to the selected bits. For complete combinational logic that costs extra evaluations and changes no settled value — which is not an argument, it is a hypothesis, and the 250,420-vector sweep is what turns it into a result.

The practical consequence is a project-level one, and it is why this guide's shipped style stays where it is. **This project fails any `run` target whose compile log is not empty**, and a non-trivial `always_comb` datapath block cannot meet that rule on this simulator. So `fp32_normalize_sv.sv` ships as a `warn` target — a row whose contract is "must compile, and must produce output" — where the manifest pins the *presence* of the diagnostic. If a future Icarus stops emitting it, the target goes red and this chapter learns its transcript went stale. That is chapters 2 and 3's deliberate-warning mechanism, applied to a construct that is genuinely useful and genuinely noisy at once.

## Two Real Differences, and the Warning That Still Does Not Come

The standard reason given for `always_comb` over `always @(*)` is documentation and synthesis-tool checking. Chapter 3's "What the Simulator Will and Will Not Tell You" measured that Icarus performs none of that checking, and chapter 9 found one exception. This session found a second, and re-verified that the headline absence is still absent. Three measurements, in order of how much they should change your habits.

### Difference one: `always_comb` runs at time zero

Chapter 3's "How a Simulator Actually Runs Your Code" taught a trap that costs an evening the first time: `always @(*)` **does not self-start**. If its only input was initialised at its declaration, no event ever fires, the block never executes, and the output sits at `x` through the entire first time step. Here is that trap and its SystemVerilog twin in one module:

```systemverilog
module tb_selfstart;

  reg a = 1'b1;           // initialised AT DECLARATION: no event ever fires
  reg y_star, y_comb;

  always @(*)  y_star = ~a;
  always_comb  y_comb = ~a;
```

```
y_star=x y_comb=0
PASS tb_selfstart (@(*) stayed x, always_comb computed at time zero)
```

The `@(*)` block never ran; the `always_comb` block computed at time zero, exactly as the standard requires. That is `src/ch13/tb_selfstart.sv`, and it is a `run` target rather than a quoted anecdote — the trap is pinned by a self-checking testbench, so if a future Icarus makes `@(*)` self-start, the regression goes red. **A trap this guide taught in chapter 3 has a measured, in-simulator fix, and the fix is one keyword.** That is not a documentation claim; it is a two-value transcript from this session.

### Difference two: sensitivity follows dependencies into function bodies

Chapter 9's "Normalize, Round, and the Latch Simulation Cannot See" measured the other difference and made a coding rule out of it: everything a function reads must arrive as an argument, because `always @*` builds its sensitivity from expressions it can see in the block and cannot see into a function body. Re-run this session in the normalize stage's own shape — a leading-zero counter reading its frame through a global instead of its argument, called from both process styles, with only the global then changed:

```
t1: star=0 comb=0 (both 0)
t2: star=0 comb=25 (comb 25, star STALE if 0)
t3: star=25 comb=25 (star catches up)
```

At `t2` only the hidden global moved. `always_comb` re-evaluated; `@*` sat stale until the argument finally changed at `t3`. Icarus really does implement the standard's "sensitivity includes the contents of any function called" rule.

Notice what the two differences have in common: both are cases where `always_comb` runs *more often* than `@(*)`. And notice the cost that comes with them — the constant-select `sorry` of "`logic` and `always_comb`: A Chapter 9 Stage, Rewritten and Swept" is the same inference engine reporting the one case it cannot analyse precisely. The engine that fixes the time-zero trap is the engine that floods your compile log.

### The absence: no latch warning, and `always_latch` does not help

Now the thing that did **not** change. Take the shape of chapter 3's "The Accidental Latch", as chapter 9 met it in real arithmetic — the normalize fragment whose right-1 branch forgets to assign `ns` — and put it inside an `always_comb`. Compile at `-g2012 -Wall`:

```
p58b_norm_latch.v:9: sorry: constant selects in always_* processes are not fully
supported (the process will be sensitive to all bits in 'sum27[26:0]').
```

That is the entire compile log. **Zero latch-related diagnostics.** And the latch is real: after the else path evaluates with `s_in = 0`, entering the right-1 path leaves `ns` holding the stale `0` where the correct value is `1`. Rewrite the same fragment with `always_latch` and the behaviour and the silence are byte-for-byte identical. So on Icarus:

- `always_comb` does not warn that a block is incomplete;
- `always_latch` does not warn that a block is *complete* (that is, that the latch you promised is not there);
- nothing distinguishes an intended latch from an accidental one.

The same result appeared again from the other direction while mutation-testing this chapter's own code. Mutation C2 broke the packed-struct decode by making one assignment conditional — `if (|w.e_raw) hidden = 1'b1;` with no `else` — inside an `always_comb`. The compile log contained six "constant selects" sorries and nothing else; what caught the bug was the equivalence sweep, which reported `comb={0 00 000000 x X00000 0X}` on the very first vector. Chapter 3's verdict stands unmodified, now measured a third time and in this chapter's own files: **the discipline (complete `if`/`else`, default assignments first) is the defence, and the simulator is not.**

One rule *is* enforced, and it is worth knowing because it is the only one. A timing control inside any of the three forms is a hard error:

```
error: a blocking delay is not allowed in an always_comb, always_ff or always_latch process.
error: there must be no event controls or blocking delays in an always_comb process.
```

That ships as `src/ch13/bad_comb_delay.sv`, an `xfail` target, so that "unenforced" is never read as "wholly unenforced". Two smaller checks exist nearby: a non-edge sensitivity list on an `always_ff` produces a genuine warning, and a variable with a continuous driver is a hard elaboration error if a process also writes it.

The classic bug the keyword is most often advertised against — blocking assignment inside `always_ff`, chapter 3's "Blocking and Non-Blocking, Derived From the Queue" and chapter 11's "The Stage Bank: Chapter 3's Rule, Finally Measured" — compiles clean and says nothing. The keywords are intent documentation on this toolchain. Write them anyway, for the two measured behavioural improvements and because the day the code meets a tool that *does* check, the intent is already stated. Do not write them believing you have bought enforcement here.

## The Format as a Type: Chapter 7's Decode as a Packed Struct

Chapter 6 drew binary32 as a picture: one sign bit, eight exponent bits, twenty-three fraction bits. Chapter 7 turned the picture into `src/ch07/fp32_fields.v` — six continuous assignments slicing `w[31]`, `w[30:23]` and `w[22:0]`. The picture and the code are the same information written twice, and only one of them is checkable.

A **packed struct** is what makes them one thing. It is a bit vector with named fields, laid out most-significant-first in declaration order:

```systemverilog
package fp32_pkg;

  typedef struct packed {
    logic        sign;   // bit 31
    logic [7:0]  e_raw;  // bits 30:23
    logic [22:0] frac;   // bits 22:0
  } fp32_t;
```

`$bits(fp32_t)` measures **32**. Assign `32'h40490fdb` to a variable of this type and `x.sign`, `x.e_raw`, `x.frac` read back `0`, `80`, `490fdb` — the encoding of π to single precision, decomposed by name. The typedef **is** chapter 6's field diagram, in a form the compiler reads.

The gain is not brevity; it is that the magic numbers stop repeating. In the shipped design `31`, `30:23` and `22:0` appear in four separate modules — `fp32_fields`, `fp32_unpack`, `fp32_screen` and `fp32_round_pack` all re-slice them. In the SystemVerilog build they appear once, inside a `package`, and every module that needs them writes `import fp32_pkg::*;` — which is also the natural home for the constants chapter 12 scatters as `localparam`s.

The rewritten decode, `src/ch13/fp32_fields_sv.sv`, is the shipped module with `w[30:23]` replaced by `w.e_raw`:

```systemverilog
module fp32_fields_sv
  import fp32_pkg::*;
  (input  fp32_t       w,       // packed binary32 pattern, as a record
   output logic        sign,    // w.sign
   output logic [7:0]  e_raw,   // w.e_raw
   output logic [22:0] frac,    // w.frac
   output logic        hidden,  // the implicit bit the format does not store
   output logic [23:0] sig,     // {hidden, frac}
   output logic [7:0]  e_eff);  // max(e_raw, 1)

  assign sign   = w.sign;
  assign e_raw  = w.e_raw;
  assign frac   = w.frac;
  assign hidden = |w.e_raw;
  assign sig    = {hidden, w.frac};
  assign e_eff  = hidden ? w.e_raw : 8'd1;

```

A plain 32-bit vector drives that `fp32_t` port with no ceremony — packed structs and equal-width vectors assign freely in both directions — so the rewrite drops into chapter 7's testbench unchanged. Swept against the shipped module over every stored exponent crossed with six boundary fractions and both signs (3,072 directed), then a million random patterns:

```
PASS tb_fields_equiv (1003072 vectors, bit-identical)
```

One million and three thousand vectors to establish that a renaming is a renaming. That is the point of doing it this way: the rewrite carries **zero arithmetic risk** and it is now *proven* to carry zero arithmetic risk, which is a different and much better thing than being obviously safe.

### The form that ships, and the form that does not

The first draft of this decode was the one a textbook writes — a single `always_comb` block:

```systemverilog
  always_comb begin
    sign   = w.sign;
    e_raw  = w.e_raw;
    frac   = w.frac;
    hidden = |w.e_raw;
    sig    = {hidden, w.frac};
    e_eff  = hidden ? w.e_raw : 8'd1;
```

It is semantically identical, and `src/ch13/tb_fields_comb.sv` proves it over 203,072 vectors. It also compiles like this:

```
:0: sorry: constant selects in always_* processes are not fully supported (the process
will be sensitive to all bits in 'w[31:0]').
```

Six of them, one per member read, and five of the six arrive with the file position mangled to `:0:`. It is the same diagnostic as the normalize stage's, from the same engine, with the same benign meaning — reading `w.e_raw` makes the process sensitive to all of `w`, which for a decode is what you wanted anyway. But under this project's zero-output rule the `always_comb` form cannot be a `run` target, and the continuous-assign form compiles in total silence. **So the shippable SystemVerilog here is the one that looks most like the Verilog it replaces**, and the textbook form ships beside it as a `warn` row that quotes the diagnostic.

That is a small, concrete instance of a pattern worth naming. The transition is not "SystemVerilog is better, use SystemVerilog". It is a per-construct trade with a per-toolchain price, and the price is sometimes paid in a place — the compile log, the regression policy — that has nothing to do with the language.

### What the struct does not buy

Three honest limits. It **does not validate anything**: an `fp32_t` will happily hold `e_raw = 255` with a non-zero `frac`, and deciding that this is a NaN is still `fp32_class`'s job. It **does not remove chapter 7's actual content** — the `hidden`, `sig` and `e_eff` derivations, which are where the subnormal story lives; those six assignments are still six assignments. And Icarus will not take the next step. The natural SystemVerilog model for "a binary32 is a normal, or a subnormal, or a special" is a tagged union, which is unpacked-struct territory, which is `sorry: Unpacked structs not supported.` at every language level.

## Interfaces: A Namespace, Not a Contract

Chapter 11's `fp32_add2_p2` carries the pattern SystemVerilog interfaces exist to name. Three signals travel into the pipe together — `{in_valid, a, b}` — and five travel out together — `{out_valid, result, invalid, overflow, inexact}`. Every testbench in "The Streaming-Equivalence Harness" declares both bundles by hand, connects them port by port, and re-declares them in the next file. An interface is a named, instantiable collection of exactly that, with `modport`s naming the two views:

```systemverilog
interface fp_in_if;
  logic        valid;
  logic [31:0] a, b;
  modport drv (output valid, a, b);   // the testbench's view
  modport dut (input  valid, a, b);   // the DUT's view
endinterface

interface fp_out_if;
  logic        valid;
  logic [31:0] result;
  logic        invalid, overflow, inexact;
  modport dut (output valid, result, invalid, overflow, inexact);
  modport mon (input  valid, result, invalid, overflow, inexact);
endinterface
```

The boundary is sharp. **Interfaces work as instantiated bundles on Icarus 13.0. They do not work as module ports.** All three port forms — modport-typed, plain, and generic `interface` — fail identically at every SystemVerilog level:

```
$ iverilog -g2012 -Wall -o /tmp/x.vvp bad_if_port.sv
bad_if_port.sv:35: syntax error
bad_if_port.sv:1: Errors in port declarations.
```

Line 35 is this, and it is the whole reason people want interfaces:

```systemverilog
module bad_if_port (input wire clk, stream_if.dut s, output wire [31:0] y);
```

That file ships as `src/ch13/bad_if_port.sv`, an `xfail` target — one of the SystemVerilog parse walls this guide pins, alongside chapter 5's `bad_sva.v` and `bad_covergroup.v` — which stay where they are and are cross-referenced rather than duplicated — and this chapter's own `cg_cov4.sv`.

What *does* work is instantiation plus hierarchical member access, and it works on the real design. `src/ch13/tb_if_stream.sv` declares the two interfaces above, instantiates them, and wires chapter 11's `fp32_add2_p2` — with its complete chapter 7 and chapter 9 module tree underneath — entirely through their members. A second, conventionally wired copy runs beside it from the same stimulus:

```systemverilog
  fp_in_if  bi ();
  fp_out_if bo ();

  // DUT A: every port reached through an interface member.
  fp32_add2_p2 duta (.clk(clk), .rst_n(rst_n),
                     .in_valid(bi.valid), .a(bi.a), .b(bi.b),
                     .result(bo.result), .invalid(bo.invalid),
                     .overflow(bo.overflow), .inexact(bo.inexact),
                     .out_valid(bo.valid));

  // DUT B: plain wires, chapter 11 style.
  wire [31:0] r_b;
  wire        iv_b, ov_b, ix_b, val_b;
  fp32_add2_p2 dutb (.clk(clk), .rst_n(rst_n),
                     .in_valid(bi.valid), .a(bi.a), .b(bi.b),
                     .result(r_b), .invalid(iv_b), .overflow(ov_b),
                     .inexact(ix_b), .out_valid(val_b));
```

Notice that the DUT's **outputs** drive interface members. Icarus accepts that: the output port becomes the single continuous driver of `bo.result` and the rest. Sixty thousand streamed cycles, a new random pair every non-bubble cycle, one-in-eight X-data bubbles (chapter 11's discipline), and a cycle-by-cycle `!==` comparison of `{out_valid, result, flags}` against the wire-wired twin:

```
PASS tb_if_stream (52541 retired results, interface-wired == wire-wired)
```

Zero-output compile, 5.2 seconds. The bundle is wiring-transparent on the real pipelined adder.

### What it does not buy, and the one protection that exists

Direction checking is the entire point of `modport`, and with modport ports unparseable there is none. Nothing in the file above distinguishes the driver's view of `bi` from the DUT's. A testbench that drives `bi.valid` from two `initial` blocks, or reads a member it was supposed only to write, compiles and runs.

One protection does fire, and it is worth knowing because it is real. SystemVerilog's single-driver rule for *continuous* drivers is implemented: once a DUT output port drives an interface member, a procedural write to that same member is a hard elaboration error.

```
error: Cannot perform procedural assignment to variable 'b.r' because it is also
continuously assigned.
```

So you get accidental-double-drive protection on the output bundle, and nothing on the input bundle.

The honest summary, and the sentence to remember: **on Icarus today an interface is a namespace, not a contract.** It is a typed, instantiable wiring harness that keeps chapter 11's two bundles together and makes a testbench read like the protocol it is exercising — a real gain, worth 52,541 results of evidence. The contract half — modport enforcement at the port boundary, `clocking` blocks, virtual interfaces feeding class-based drivers — is documentation here, and it is flagged as documentation because there is no simulator in this environment on which to measure it.

The mutation record makes one more limitation explicit. Mutating the shared `fp32_add2_p2` module **survives** this testbench: both DUTs are the same module, so an arithmetic bug changes both sides of the comparison and cancels. That is not a hole; it is the claim. This target proves interface wiring is transparent, and arithmetic is proven by chapters 9 through 12 — and, in this chapter, by the queue scoreboard next door, where the wrapper mutants die.

## The Testbench Layer, Where the Wins Are Real

Everything so far has been a renaming with a price tag. The testbench layer is different: here SystemVerilog deletes code, deletes a bug class, and deletes a constant that should never have been a constant.

### The queue scoreboard, and the latency that stops being typed

Chapter 11's "The Streaming-Equivalence Harness" is the guide's most careful testbench. Its scoreboard is a `LAT`-deep circular buffer: parallel arrays `eq`, `vq`, `aq`, `bq`, an index `cyc % LAT`, and the one-index check-before-overwrite trick that makes the off-by-one "structurally impossible". It is good code. It also mentions the pipeline latency in six places, and every one of them is a place to get it wrong.

The SystemVerilog version is a queue. Push an expectation when a pair is driven; pop one when `out_valid` retires:

```systemverilog
  logic [98:0] q[$];
  exp_t        e;
```

```systemverilog
      if (in_valid) begin
        q.push_back({r_c, inv_c, ovf_c, inx_c, a, b});
        n_driven = n_driven + 1;
      end
      if (q.size() > maxq) maxq = q.size();
      if (ov === 1'b1) begin
        if (q.size() == 0) begin
          errors = errors + 1;
          $display("FAIL cyc %0d: out_valid with an EMPTY queue (spurious retire)", i);
        end else begin
          e = q.pop_front();
          if ((^{r_p, inv_p, ovf_p, inx_p}) === 1'bx) begin
            errors = errors + 1;
            if (errors <= 10)
              $display("FAIL cyc %0d: %h+%h X in outputs", i, e.a, e.b);
          end else if ({r_p, inv_p, ovf_p, inx_p} !== {e.r, e.inv, e.ovf, e.inx}) begin
            errors = errors + 1;
            if (errors <= 10)
              $display("FAIL cyc %0d: %h+%h got %h/%b%b%b exp %h/%b%b%b",
                       i, e.a, e.b, r_p, inv_p, ovf_p, inx_p,
                       e.r, e.inv, e.ovf, e.inx);
          end
          n_retired = n_retired + 1;
        end
      end
    end
```

The load-bearing observation is not the line count. It is that **the queue version never mentions the latency.** No `LAT` parameter, no modulo, no depth. And so the latency stops being an input to the testbench and becomes an *output* of it:

```
PASS tb_q_stream (52570 pairs, max queue depth 3 = measured latency + 1)
```

Depth 3 is the pipeline's latency of 2 plus the just-pushed expectation, because the sample point pushes before it pops. Nobody typed 3. Deepen the pipe and the number moves. A testbench that measures a property instead of asserting it cannot disagree with the design about that property — which is exactly the class of bug chapter 11's "Skew, and the Other Four: Bugs That Pass a Weaker Harness" spends its length on.

Two composition walls turned up while building it, and both are visible in the listing. The natural record type — `exp_t q[$];` — does not elaborate:

```
Sorry: Queue of type `11netstruct_t` is not yet supported.
```

Queues take plain vectors. So the queue is `logic [98:0] q[$]` and the packed struct is kept as the **unpacking** type: push a concatenation, pop into `e`, then read `e.r`, `e.inv`, `e.a` by name. And the concatenation is a concatenation rather than a named assignment pattern because `'{r: ..., inv: ...}` is a syntax error in any context here, while the positional form `'{8'h12, 8'h34}` works. Both walls ship as teaching targets (`bad_qstruct.sv` is the `xfail`), and together they make the chapter's most transferable rule: **Icarus's SystemVerilog features frequently work alone and fail combined.** A support matrix built from single-feature probes will not predict this. Compile the composite.

### It can fail, four ways, and each was measured

Chapter 4's standing practice asks what would have to break for a testbench to print `FAIL`. For this one, measured against scratch copies of the DUT:

| Mutation | Result |
|---|---|
| the pipelined sticky bit dropped (`p1_s_al <= 1'b0`) | **24,246 errors** |
| retire one cycle early (`out_valid = vpipe[0]`) | **52,570 errors** — every retire skewed, the first at cycle 1 with an X |
| never retire (`out_valid = 1'b0`) | `52570 expectation(s) never retired` / `driven 52570, retired 0` |
| spurious retires during bubbles (`out_valid = vpipe[1] \| vpipe[0]`) | **59,133 errors**, the empty-queue guard firing from cycle 17 |
| a shared *stage* module mutated | **SURVIVED** — see below |

Rows three and four are the interesting ones. A scoreboard with only a data comparison passes the "never retire" mutant — it never gets a transaction to compare — and mostly passes the "spurious retire" mutant, because a spurious retire has nothing to compare against. What kills both is the bookkeeping: `q.size() == 0` on a retire, the drain loop, the empty-at-end check, and `driven == retired`. Those four are the queue's replacement for the structural guarantee chapter 11 got from its one-index trick.

The survivor is honest. Chapter 11's oracle for the pipelined `fp32_add2_p2` is the combinational `fp32_add2`, and both are built from the *same* seven stage modules, so mutating a stage changes DUT and oracle together and cancels — chapter 9's "Masking, Measured in Both Directions", reproduced in this chapter's own code. This target checks the pipeline wrapper; the stage arithmetic is checked by chapter 9's unit suites. A testbench's claim should be stated at exactly the strength of its evidence.

One incident from the build is worth a sentence, because it is a free advertisement for those guards. The first draft deasserted `in_valid` mid-cycle after the last sample; the final pair was withdrawn before any posedge captured it, and the result was exactly one unretired expectation and zero data mismatches. A scoreboard without the count guards would have printed PASS.

### `foreach`, `string`, enums, casts, and the operators

The small conveniences are all real at `-g2012` and all cost nothing, and each has a shipped file it would improve:

- **`foreach`** over an array replaces the three `for (i = 0; i < NBINS; ...)` loops in chapter 12's `tb_cov4.v`.
- **`string`** with `len`, concatenation, `substr`, `==` and `itoa` replaces the `bname()` machinery in `cov_names.vh` — though not `toupper()`, which is `error: Method toupper is not a string method.`
- **Enum `.name()`** turns chapter 12's bare `fclass()` codes 0-4 into `FC_ZERO`...`FC_NAN` in failure messages. One caution measured here and not in the research notes: a cast's result cannot take a method. `fclass_e'(code).name()` is a syntax error; assign to a typed variable first.
- **Size casts** such as `24'(expr)` make chapter 9's width conversions self-documenting where the shipped code writes `shl8[4:0]`.
- **`++`, `+=`, `do...while`** shrink loop boilerplate.
- **Dynamic arrays** (`new[N]`, resize-copy `new[2N](d)`) work, and would suit a runtime-sized corner library — a marginal gain given chapter 12's generated `.vh` libraries already ship.

### `$urandom_range`, and the seed rule that actually protects

`$urandom_range(lo, hi)` works at **every** language level, including `-g2005` — consistent with chapter 2's "The Toolchain: Directives, System Tasks, and What Icarus Really Supports", which found that Icarus gates syntax, not system functions. Bounds are respected over 1,000 draws, the one-argument form works, and reversed arguments swap per the standard: `$urandom_range(20, 10)` lands in [10, 20]. It draws from the same per-thread generator `$urandom(seed)` seeds, so chapter 5's discipline transfers verbatim.

Except that chapter 5's rule needs sharpening, and this is the sharpening. Chapter 5 measured that `$urandom(seed)`'s first draw is near-linear in the seed and prescribed seeding once per run, printing the seed, and throwing the first draw away. Measured this session, the discard does not help nearly as much as it sounds:

```
seed 1: first=00010e00  second=1c598438
seed 2: first=00021c00  second=38b1fc71
seed 3: first=00032a00  second=550a72aa
seed 4: first=00043800  second=7162e8e2
```

The *second* draw's ratios against seed 1 are 1.000, 2.000, 3.000, 4.000 to three decimal places. And `$urandom_range` inherits the correlation straight through: after one discard, `$urandom_range(0, 9999)` returns 1107, 2214, 3321, 4429 for seeds 1 to 4. **One discarded draw does not decorrelate nearby seeds.** The half of the rule that carries the weight is the first half: *seed once per simulation, never per trial*. Every testbench in chapters 9 through 13 already complies, so no shipped result is affected — but the wording matters for anyone reaching for a per-trial reseed.

### The two-state trap

`bit`, `byte`, `int` and friends are genuinely two-state on Icarus, and that is exactly the problem. Measured: assign `32'hxxxx_xxxx` into a `bit [31:0]` and read back `32'h00000000`. Silently.

Now recall what this guide's verification rests on. Chapter 9's central move is `!==` plus X-guards, because an `!=` comparison passes an undriven design. Chapter 11's "X in the Pipe, Quantified" measured that 59 per cent of single-bit X injections are absorbed into bit-correct outputs, which is why X-discipline has to be enforced at injection points. A scoreboard or expected-value store declared `bit` or `int` **cannot hold an X to guard against**: the X becomes a plausible zero at the moment of storage, which is precisely the "defined-looking wrong answer" failure shape chapter 9's "X-Propagation: The Wrong Answer That Looks Right" is about.

The rule that follows is simple and absolute for this project's style: **two-state types are for counters and loop indices. Anything that touches DUT data stays four-state `logic`, and the X-guards stay.**

## Assertions: What Runs, What Is Deleted, and by Which Flag

Chapter 5's "Assertions, and What You Can Actually Run Here" drew this boundary from a ten-construct matrix, and the boundary has not moved: **immediate assertions work from `-g2005-sv` up; concurrent assertions — SVA, the temporal half, the interesting half — do not exist at any level.** What this session adds is precision about the flags, and one finding that is strictly worse than the chapter 5 record.

### Immediate assertions are worth using, and here is the evidence

Chapter 5's argument for `assert … else` over a plain `if` is that the failure message writes itself. Measured again this session, in this chapter's own target:

```
ERROR: tb_assert_live.sv:55: deliberate: q=9 exceeds 3
       Time: 5000  Scope: tb_assert_live
```

File, line, simulation time and scope, none of which the author typed. And the *pass* statement executes, which matters more than it sounds — it is the measurable difference between immediate `assert` and immediate `cover`. `src/ch13/tb_assert_live.sv` uses both halves:

```systemverilog
      // Holds for every q driven here. The PASS statement executes -- which
      // is the measured difference between immediate `assert` and immediate
      // `cover`, whose pass statement never runs at all.
      assert (q <= 4'd3) passes = passes + 1;
        else begin fired = fired + 1; $error("q=%0d exceeds 3", q); end
```

```systemverilog
    // Deliberately false: the check that proves assertions are still here.
    #1;
    q = 4'd9;
    assert (q <= 4'd3)
      else begin fired = fired + 1; $error("deliberate: q=%0d exceeds 3", q); end
```

That file is a `run` target, and it does not *describe* the flag behaviour below — it *depends* on it. Under the default flags the pass statement runs four times and the deliberate failure reports once, so the run is green. Change one thing about the build and it goes red.

### `-gno-assertions` deletes the assertions that work

Chapter 5 measured that `-gno-assertions` silently discards a *concurrent* assertion it can parse, and called it "a flag that turns a failing check into a silent pass". Measured this session, it is worse than that:

```
$ iverilog -g2012 -Wall -o /tmp/a.vvp tb_assert_live.sv && vvp /tmp/a.vvp
ERROR: tb_assert_live.sv:55: deliberate: q=9 exceeds 3
       Time: 5000  Scope: tb_assert_live
PASS tb_assert_live (4 pass statements, 1 deliberate failure reported)

$ iverilog -g2012 -gno-assertions -Wall -o /tmp/a.vvp tb_assert_live.sv && vvp /tmp/a.vvp
FAIL tb_assert_live: pass statement ran 0 times, expected 4 -- assertions were compiled out
```

**`-gno-assertions` deletes IMMEDIATE assertions too** — the ones Icarus fully supports, the ones chapter 5 recommends, the ones this guide's `tb_assert.v` is built from. The whole statement goes: pass branch and `else` branch alike. Both compile logs are zero bytes; nothing anywhere says a check was removed. A file full of working assertions becomes a file full of nothing, and the run is green.

And the diagnostic Icarus prints when it meets a concurrent assertion is this:

```
sorry: concurrent_assertion_item not supported. Try -gno-assertions or
-gsupported-assertions to turn this message off.
```

The compiler recommends, by name, the flag that deletes every working check in the design. A reader who follows that advice to "turn off the noise" gets a build in which no assertion in any file will ever fire again.

### The four-way grid, and the only safe flag

Four builds, four constructs, all at `-g2012`:

| | default | `-gassertions` | `-gsupported-assertions` | `-gno-assertions` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| immediate `assert` | fires | fires | **fires** | **gone; clean run** |
| `assert property (@(posedge clk) req);` | compile FAIL, the `sorry` above | same | compiles; property **silently discarded** | compiles; **silently discarded** |
| `assert property (... \|-> ...)` | `syntax error` + `Error in property_spec of concurrent assertion item.` | same | same | same |
| immediate `cover (expr) stmt` | compiles; inert | same | same | same |

Three consequences, and chapter 5's wording needs sharpening on two of them.

**First**, chapter 5 already measured that these flags "silence the diagnostic and discard the property" for concurrent assertions, and that they "enable nothing" — no flag makes SVA work. What chapter 5 did not test is what they do to the assertions Icarus **does** support, and there the three flags differ: `-gsupported-assertions` keeps immediate assertions **alive** while letting a file containing parseable concurrent text compile, by discarding those items in silence. It is the only setting under which chapter 5-style immediate assertions and an SVA-bearing file coexist — the right flag for a mixed codebase, provided everyone knows the concurrent properties are decoration.

**Second**, the two failure *classes* of concurrent assertions are different and worth keeping apart. The non-temporal form `assert property (@(posedge clk) expr);` is a `sorry`-type compile error by default and compilable-but-discarded under the two permissive flags — which is how chapter 5's discard finding was possible at all. Anything with a real temporal operator (`|->`, `##n`, `throughout`, `disable iff`) is a plain **syntax error under every flag**. No flag rescues real SVA; that is chapter 5's `bad_sva.v`, still an `xfail` target, still reproducing its two-line diagnostic exactly.

**Third**, chapter 5's matrix ticks immediate `cover`, and its text already says the construct "produces nothing at all" — what this probe adds is which half is inert. Measured side by side in one program, with the covered expression true:

```
cover pass-statement ran 0 time(s); assert pass-statement ran 1 time(s)
```

No count is reported anywhere at end of simulation either — consistent with the finding that Icarus has zero coverage instrumentation. The tick means "accepted", not "functional", and the practical consequence is that this guide's hand-rolled coverage counters are not only the covergroup replacement; they are the immediate-`cover` replacement too.

### `unique` and `priority`: half a feature, honestly priced

One more construct belongs here, because it is the only place where a compile-time message *understates* what the runtime does. Write `unique case` and the compile says:

```
fp32_class_flags_sv.sv:39: vvp.tgt sorry: Case unique/unique0 qualities are ignored.
```

They are not entirely ignored. `src/ch13/fp32_class_flags_sv.sv` decodes chapter 12's five operand classes from a typed enum with a `unique case`, and `tb_unique_case.sv` pins three properties. Drive an unlabelled code and the simulator prints, at run time:

```
WARNING: fp32_class_flags_sv.sv:39: value is unhandled for priority or unique case statement
         Time: 11000  Scope: tb_unique_case.dut
```

That is a real check that plain `case` does not give you, and `priority case` behaves the same way. `unique0 case` is correctly *silent* on a no-match, so the runtime genuinely distinguishes the two qualities despite the message. What is missing is the other half — the one `unique` is famous for. Two simultaneously matching items are never reported: the testbench drives `3'b110` into a `unique casez` whose first two items both match, and asserts that `hit` comes out `1`, first-match-wins, in silence. **The testbench passes on the absence of a feature**, which is the only kind of test that can pin one: state what is missing, and go red if it ever appears.

Two costs come with adopting it. Every `unique`/`unique0 case` adds a `sorry` to the compile log, which makes any target containing one a `warn` row under this project's rules; `priority case` does not, though it produces the same runtime warning. And the mutation record exposes a limit worth knowing: delete `unique` from both case statements and the testbench still passes — everything it can observe from inside a simulation is behaviour plain `case` shares. What goes red is the *manifest*, because the compile log drops from 165 bytes to zero. The classification is the check — with a caveat the mutation record makes explicit: the `warn` contract pins only that the log is **non-empty**, and this target's 165 bytes are two sorries from two files. Delete `unique` from the module alone and the testbench's own sorry keeps the row green at 80 bytes — measured. The classification catches wholesale removal, not per-site removal. The runtime WARNING itself is written to the simulator's output where no Verilog code can see it, so it is pinned only by being quoted — here, and in the file's header.

## Coverage: The Parse Error, and the Guard No Language Has

Chapter 12's "The Coverage Model: 105 Bins on the DUT's Own Wires" is 105 integer counters, five `for` loops, a stage-qualified `sample_all` task and an include file of bin names. Chapter 5 built the first such model by hand and explained why: **Icarus has zero coverage instrumentation.** No coverage target exists among `blif`, `null`, `pcb`, `sizer`, `stub`, `vhdl`, `vlog95` and `vvp`, and nothing in `iverilog -h` or `vvp -h` offers one.

So what would a `covergroup` replace? Here are three of chapter 12's 105 dimensions written as IEEE 1800 clause 19 says to write them — the operand-class bins, the exponent-distance buckets, and the rounding-action family:

```systemverilog
  covergroup cg @(posedge clk);
    cp_class_a: coverpoint fclass(a) {
      bins zero = {0}; bins subn = {1}; bins norm = {2};
      bins inf  = {3}; bins nan  = {4};
    }
    cp_expd: coverpoint (a[30:23] > b[30:23] ? a[30:23]-b[30:23]
                                             : b[30:23]-a[30:23]) {
      bins d0 = {0}; bins d_near = {[1:2]};
      bins d_mid = {[3:24]}; bins d_far = {[25:255]};
    }
    cp_round: coverpoint {ng, nr, ns, round_up} {
      bins tie_up   = {4'b1001};
      bins tie_down = {4'b1000};
      wildcard bins up = {4'b???1};
    }
    x_class_expd: cross cp_class_a, cp_expd;
  endgroup
```

The comparison is not close. Three coverpoints and one `cross` replace a 105-element array, five loops and the arithmetic that maps a pair of class codes onto an index; `bins`, ranges, `wildcard bins` and `cross` are exactly the vocabulary chapter 12's model had to spell out; the `@(posedge clk)` clause replaces a stage-qualified task call, and `get_coverage()` replaces the closure report.

And here is what happens:

```

cg_cov4.sv:16: syntax error
cg_cov4.sv:16: error: Invalid module item.
...                       (26 further lines)
cg_cov4.sv:32: syntax error

```

line by line before giving up. The first pair and the last two are shown.)* Line 16 is the `covergroup` keyword. The first pair is byte-identical

### The climax: what a covergroup would not replace

Now the part of chapter 12 that survives the language transition untouched, and the reason it survives.

Chapter 12's "Pinning the Bins: The Last Unpaid Debt" solved a problem chapter 5 recorded and could not fix: **the bin definitions themselves were unverified.** Moving a bucket boundary from 25 to 60 still closed 34 of 34 bins, silently, because a coverage model is a program that *claims* to describe your specification and nothing was checking the claim. The fix is a two-key lock: the directed phase is deterministic, so every bin's count over it is a predictable number, and `cov_gen.py` implements all 105 bin definitions a second time, independently, in python, emitting the expected counts as a pinned table the bench compares against with `!==`. A definition edited in the bench alone breaks against python; edited in python alone, against the bench; edited in both, it is a visible, reviewable specification change — the only kind the guard permits.

Read IEEE 1800 clause 19 with that mechanism in mind. This next paragraph is **documentation, not measurement** — no simulator in this environment runs a covergroup, so nothing here was executed — but the reading is checkable by anyone with the standard.

A covergroup gives you `bins`, ranges, `cross`, `illegal_bins`, `ignore_bins`, `option.at_least`, `get_coverage()` and a coverage database. Every one of those is machinery for asking **"did each interesting thing happen at least N times?"** Not one of them verifies that a bin's *definition* means what the verification plan meant. `option.at_least` is a floor on a count, not a pin on it. `illegal_bins` is the single definitional check the language offers — a bin that must stay empty, which is exactly chapter 12's D9 never-bin and would map onto it directly, paired with an arming `cover` the same way chapter 12 pairs its witness assertion with an armed-count guard. But "this bin must stay empty" is a much weaker statement than "this bin must contain exactly four entries under this stimulus, computed by an independent implementation".

Could you build chapter 12's guard around a covergroup? In a tool with a coverage database, yes: run the directed phase, read every bin's count back through the coverage API, compare against the independent model. That is a **methodology you build around the covergroup**, not a feature in it — the second implementation and the discipline of keeping the two apart are unchanged in any language.

And chapter 12 proved that guard is load-bearing, not decorative — twice over, in the least flattering way possible. Two bin mutations initially **survived** it: a mistimed stage qualification hidden by back-to-back traffic, and a boundary move the directed library did not straddle. Both were killed only after the *library* was fixed. A third survived the shipped guard until chapter 12's own reviewer found it. A coverage model is a testbench and gets mutation discipline; a coverage *guard* is a testbench too, and gets it as well.

So the honest summary, and it is this chapter's central claim about the guide's own contribution. Migrating `tb_cov4` to a covergroup would shrink the **counting** substantially — the 105-element array and the index arithmetic come free as bins and crosses. It would not touch the **pinning**, and it would not touch the partition invariants either, which are count identities *across* coverpoints rather than bins. Verifying bin definitions against an independent model is not a feature of clause 19, and this chapter has not found it offered as one anywhere it has looked, which is why the one piece of this guide's methodology that no SystemVerilog construct replaces is the piece this guide had to build for itself. That is a modest claim stated at full strength: it is documentation about what clause 19 offers, plus measurement about what Icarus runs, and neither half would change if a simulator here started running covergroups tomorrow.

One thing carries over unchanged in a different sense. Chapter 12's samples come from the DUT's own wires with stage-correct qualification (`vpipe[0]`, `p1_screen`). In SystemVerilog that becomes `sample()` on demand, or a clocking expression with `iff` guards. Either way the stage-timing analysis chapter 12 did by hand still has to be done — by the person writing the `iff`.

## The Honest Recommendation, in Three Tiers

Everything above is a measurement. This section is a judgement, and it is grounded in the matrix rather than in taste. Three tiers, each anchored to something that was actually run.

### Tier 1 — real on Icarus 13.0 today; adopt, with measured benefit

**In the testbench layer**, this is where the transition pays for itself and the payment is large:

- **Queues** for scoreboards. The rewrite deleted the latency constant and the whole off-by-one class with it, and it is mutation-proven: 24,246 errors on a dropped sticky pipe, 52,570 on a one-cycle skew, and its bookkeeping guards kill two mutants that a data-comparison-only scoreboard passes.
- **Immediate `assert … else`** for every invariant — file, line, time and scope for free.
- **`$urandom_range`**, under the sharpened rule: seed once per simulation.
- **`foreach`**, **`string`**, enum **`.name()`**, **`++`/`+=`**, casts, `void` functions with `return`. All free at `-g2012`.

**In the design layer**, adopt but expect renaming rather than revolution: **`logic`** wherever a `wire`/`reg` decision used to need thinking about; **packed structs and typedefs in packages** for the binary32 fields (bit-identical over 1,003,072 vectors, zero-output compile in the continuous-assign form, format constants no longer repeated in four modules); **packages** for the constants chapter 12 scatters as `localparam`s; **`.name` and `.*` port connections**, which would shorten every instantiation in chapters 9 through 12; and the three **`always_*` forms** as intent markers — with two measured behavioural improvements (time-zero execution, function-body sensitivity), one measured hard check (no timing controls), and one measured cost, since the constant-select `sorry` floods the compile log of any non-trivial `always_comb` datapath block and this project's zero-output rule then keeps the shipped style at continuous assignments.

### Tier 2 — partially real; adopt with your eyes open

- **`unique` / `priority case`.** The runtime no-match check is real and free; the overlap check does not exist; each `unique` case statement costs a `sorry` in the compile log, and `priority` is free of it.
- **Interfaces.** Real as instantiated bundles — proven streaming-transparent on the actual pipelined adder over 52,541 results. Absent as ports and modports. A namespace, not a contract.
- **Two-state types.** Real, and they dissolve this guide's X-discipline at the moment of storage. Confine them to counters and loop indices.
- **`-gsupported-assertions`.** The right flag if concurrent-assertion text ever enters the codebase — as long as everyone knows it discards those items in silence.

### Tier 3 — aspirational on this toolchain; teach as documentation, and flag it

SVA (`|->`, sequences, `disable iff`) — parse errors under every flag. Covergroups — parse errors whose diagnostics never name the construct. Associative arrays — an *internal error*, not even a `sorry`. `inside`; unpacked structs; named assignment patterns; queues of structs; `unique if`; class-based testbenches and UVM, which are unmeasurable here and pointless without the rest. And the four-simulator enforcement story chapter 3 told: **`which verilator` returns nothing on this machine, and Vivado, Questa and VCS are not installed either.** That story is documentation in chapter 3 and it is still documentation here. This chapter did not upgrade it and will not pretend to have.

For every Tier-3 feature this guide already ships a hand-rolled equivalent, measured and mutation-tested: chapter 5's `if`/`$fatal` assertion pattern, chapter 11's streaming scoreboard, chapter 12's python-pinned 105-bin model and its armed white-box witness. They exist because they are the only versions that run here — and, having been written, they turned out to be the versions whose failure modes are known.

### What to adopt first, and the migration cost curve

If you take one thing from this chapter into your next project: **start in the testbench, start with queues and immediate assertions, and leave the RTL alone until you have a reason.** The testbench gains are large, immediate and provable at the scale of a single file. The RTL gains are real but they are readability gains with a compile-log tax, and — this is the part that decides it — they are a one-way door.

Measured once and stated once: the shipped 2001-style sources compile from `-g1995` through `-g2012`. Every SystemVerilog rewrite in `src/ch13/` dies below `-g2005-sv`, usually with `syntax error` / `I give up.` at a `package` keyword, naming neither the construct nor the flag. The `.sv` extension does not help — Icarus keys the language off `-g` alone, measured. On this simulator the transition is all-or-nothing per file. That is not a reason to refuse it; it is a reason to convert files you are already touching, keep the equivalence sweep next to each one, and know which language level your regression is pinned to.

One last piece of good news, and it is the reason the recommendation is as short as it is: `-g2005-sv`, `-g2009` and `-g2012` were indistinguishable across every probe in the matrix. There is no ladder to climb and no per-feature flag research to do. Pick `-g2012`, and the only remaining question is per-construct, which is what the matrix answers.

## Traps Beginners Fall Into

**Believing the keyword bought you enforcement.** `always_comb` reads like a promise the tool will keep. On this simulator it keeps exactly one part of it — no timing controls — and none of the rest. An incomplete `if` inside `always_comb` compiles clean, really latches, and looks identical to an `always_latch` that meant it. Write the keyword; keep the discipline; do not retire the equivalence sweep because the block is now labelled.

**Reading a support matrix as a support guarantee.** Queues work. Packed structs work. A queue of a packed struct does not. `.name()` works. Casts work. `fclass_e'(code).name()` is a syntax error. Every composite construct in this chapter's code had to be compiled before it could be believed, and two of them failed. A single-feature probe tells you about a single feature.

**Turning off the noise.** The compiler meets a concurrent assertion, prints a `sorry`, and recommends `-gno-assertions` by name. That flag deletes every immediate assertion in the design — pass branch, `else` branch, all of it — and the build stays green and silent. If you need any of the assertion flags, `-gsupported-assertions` is the only one that leaves working checks alive.

**Declaring a scoreboard `int` or `bit` because it holds a number.** Two-state types absorb X at the moment of storage: `32'hxxxx_xxxx` into a `bit [31:0]` reads back zero, silently. The X-guard you carefully wrote then guards nothing, and chapter 9's "defined-looking wrong answer" failure shape is back. DUT data stays `logic`.

**Re-seeding to "get more randomness".** Chapter 5 measured that `$urandom(seed)`'s first draw is near-linear in the seed; this chapter measured that the second draw is too, and that `$urandom_range` inherits it. Seeds 1 to 4 produce 1107, 2214, 3321, 4429 from the first post-discard range call. Seed once per simulation. A per-trial reseed corrupted chapter 5's own headline number before it was caught.

**Migrating a file because it would look nicer.** Every SystemVerilog file in `src/ch13/` is dead below `-g2005-sv` with a diagnostic that names neither the problem nor the fix, while its 2001-style original runs on every Icarus since 1995 syntax. Convert files you are already working on, keep the original next to the rewrite until the equivalence sweep is green, and know which `-g` level your regression is pinned to.

**Assuming the noisy diagnostic means the code is wrong.** The constant-select `sorry` says the process will be sensitive to more bits than you selected. For complete combinational logic that is harmless — and "harmless" was established by sweeping 250,420 vectors, not by reading the message. When a tool tells you it has fallen back to something conservative, the right response is a measurement, not a shrug in either direction.

## Bridge to Chapter 14

The audit is done, and the answer is narrower than the folklore on both sides. SystemVerilog is not a different language you must learn before you can write real hardware — four of this guide's shipped files were rewritten in it this session and every one came out bit-identical, because the arithmetic was never the part a language was going to change. Nor is it a set of features you can adopt by reading a book: on this toolchain, a substantial fraction of what a verification textbook teaches is a parse error, and a smaller, meaner fraction is accepted and quietly does nothing.

What survives the transition is what this guide has been building all along. Chapter 12's specification is still a specification. Chapter 9's equivalence sweep is still an equivalence sweep — and it is what caught every mutant in this chapter, including two that no compiler diagnostic mentioned. Chapter 11's X-discipline still has to be enforced at injection points, harder in a two-state testbench rather than less. And chapter 12's bin-pinning guard is still the one mechanism no language feature replaces.

Chapter 14 changes the axis. Everything from chapter 6 onward has been priced in binary32's currency: 24-bit significands, 8-bit exponents, and the three-rounding composition that chapter 12's clause S3 specified and clause S10 explicitly declined to improve. The last chapter asks what the design would look like if those constants moved — the single-rounding multi-operand architectures that keep four addends unrounded until one final rounding, the formats that re-price every trade-off measured here (bfloat16's shorter significand, posits' variable-length exponent), and the annotated bibliography for a reader who wants the literature behind what this guide measured for itself. One honest caveat carries forward with it: the Tier-3 layer this chapter could only describe — covergroups with a coverage database, SVA, the enforcement a commercial elaborator performs — is unlocked by a simulator this environment does not have, and a reader with access to one can measure what this chapter had to cite. The adder is finished, the languages are surveyed, and what is left is the horizon.
