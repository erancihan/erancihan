## One Contract, Three Fields

Chapter 6 closed by handing you a number, `0x3dcccccd`, and a sentence: floating point is fixed point plus a hardware-managed exponent. This chapter is that sentence made exact. IEEE 754 single precision — **binary32**, the standard's own name for it — is three of chapter 6's number systems packed into one 32-bit word under a single contract:

- a **sign-magnitude** sign bit (the "obsolete" system of "One Pattern, Five Numbers", returned to duty),
- an 8-bit exponent in **excess-127** (the biased encoding of "Biased Encoding: The Exponent's Format", with the off-by-one that section flagged as a seed),
- and a 23-bit fraction that is a **UQ1.23 fixed-point value with its integer bit not stored** — an unsigned quantity in [1, 2) whose leading 1 the format refuses to spend a wire on.

The contract is everything and the bits are nothing: "The Bits Never Know" applies with full force, because `0x3F800000` is 1,065,353,216, or +1.0, or garbage, depending entirely on who is reading. What is new in this chapter is that the contract has *corners written into it*. Two exponent patterns out of 256 are reserved, and those two reservations generate every special value floating point has: signed zeros, subnormals, infinities, NaNs. Chapter 5's section "Verifying Floating Point Specifically" already named these as the operand classes its taxonomy crosses; this chapter defines them from the format side, so that by the end you can take *any* 32-bit pattern and, by hand, decode it, classify it, and say why the standard put it there.

The working method is the same as the last six chapters. Every numeric claim below was verified this session with `python3` (`struct` is ground truth for encodings, `fractions.Fraction` for exact arithmetic), every simulator claim measured against **Icarus Verilog 13.0** (`iverilog -g2012 -Wall`) on this Linux x86-64 machine, and every listing is a file in `src/ch07/` — all four build targets green through `run_all.sh ch07`, with zero compiler output. Where a statement comes from the standard's text rather than a measurement, it is flagged in place; IEEE 754-2019 clause numbers are cited by title only.

One terminology decision, fixed now and used for the rest of the book. The full 24-bit quantity `1.F` (or `0.F`) is the **significand** — the standard's word. The stored 23 bits are the **fraction** (the standard says *trailing significand field*). "Mantissa" is the folk word for either; fine in speech, ambiguous in a spec, and this guide stops using it here.

## Decoding by Hand: Fields, Classes, and the Hex Ranges

The layout, MSB first:

```
bit:   31 | 30 ......... 23 | 22 ................. 0
       S  | E (8 bits)      | F (23 bits)
```

Field extraction is pure wiring — `s = w[31]; e = w[30:23]; f = w[22:0];` in Verilog, `s = (b>>31)&1; e = (b>>23)&0xFF; f = b&0x7FFFFF` in python. Anchors, read from real encodings with `struct` this session (the first three rows also appeared in chapter 6's bias table; they are the patterns worth memorizing):

```
     1.0 bits 3f800000  sign 0  exp 127 (0b01111111)  frac 0x000000
    -2.0 bits c0000000  sign 1  exp 128 (0b10000000)  frac 0x000000
     0.5 bits 3f000000  sign 0  exp 126 (0b01111110)  frac 0x000000
    6.25 bits 40c80000  sign 0  exp 129 (0b10000001)  frac 0x480000
```

Note `2.0 = 0x40000000` and `-2.0 = 0xC0000000`: negation is one bit, no arithmetic — sign-magnitude delivering exactly what chapter 6 said it would. Then the whole format is two lookups on the exponent field:

| E field | F field | class | value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | signed zero | ±0 |
| 0 | ≠0 | subnormal | ±(0.F)₂ × 2⁻¹²⁶ |
| 1…254 | any | normal | ±(1.F)₂ × 2^(E−127) |
| 255 | 0 | infinity | ±∞ |
| 255 | ≠0 | NaN | no value; F bit 22 = quiet bit, rest = payload |

The five classes partition all 2³² patterns exactly — the census, re-derived this session and checked against 2³²:

| class | count | formula | share |
|---|---|---|---|
| zeros | 2 | 2 | 0.00000005% |
| subnormals | 16,777,214 | 2·(2²³−1) | 0.390625% |
| normals | 4,261,412,864 | 2·254·2²³ | 99.218750% |
| infinities | 2 | 2 | 0.00000005% |
| NaNs | 16,777,214 | 2·(2²³−1) | 0.390625% |

Two readings of that table matter for verification. First, subnormals and NaNs have *identical* populations — same count, opposite ends of the exponent scale. Second, a uniform random pattern is a normal 99.22% of the time, which is chapter 5's argument (in "Choosing Stimulus: Directed, Exhaustive, Random" terms) that FP corner cases must be directed: random stimulus alone finds a subnormal in a pair of operands only 0.78% of the time.

**The hand procedure** — decode any pattern in four questions, no arithmetic until the last:

1. Split 1/8/23. Hex habit: the sign is bit 31, but E straddles the first two hex digits, so read it as `(b >> 23) & 0xFF` — do not eyeball hex digits.
2. E = 0? Then F = 0 means signed zero, F ≠ 0 means subnormal.
3. E = 255? Then F = 0 means infinity, F ≠ 0 means NaN — and bit 22 splits quiet from signalling.
4. Otherwise normal: value = (1 + F/2²³) × 2^(E−127), sign from bit 31.

Worked, on the number chapter 6 told you to keep in your pocket. `0x3DCCCCCD`: sign 0. E = `0x3D_C…` → (0x3DCCCCCD >> 23) & 0xFF = 123, not 0 and not 255, so a normal with actual exponent 123 − 127 = −4. F = 0x4CCCCD, so the significand is 1 + 0x4CCCCD/2²³ = 1.60000002384… and the value is that times 2⁻⁴ = **0.100000001490116119384765625** exactly (the full decimal, from `Fraction` this session). There is the repeating pattern chapter 6 promised — `CCCC` is `1100 1100…`₂ — and the final `D` is the round-up. "Encoding by Hand: The Algorithm and Its Escapes" derives both.

A second drill, all the way through: `0xC0490FDB`. Sign 1. E = 128 → 2¹. F = 0x490FDB → significand 1.5707963705… → value **−3.1415927410125732421875**. That is −π rounded to binary32; π itself, like 0.1, does not exist here.

And the specials, faster than the formula: `0x7F800000` is +∞ (E = 255, F = 0). `0xFF800000` is −∞. `0x7FC00055` has E = 255, F = 0x400055 ≠ 0: a NaN, bit 22 set so *quiet*, payload bits 21:0 = 0x55 = 85. `0x7F800055` is the *signalling* NaN with the same payload. `0x00000200` has E = 0, F = 0x200 ≠ 0: a subnormal — its value waits for "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp". `0x80000000` is −0.

**The hex-range table.** Because the encoding is monotone in the magnitude bits (verified in "Bias 127, and What the Missing One Buys"), the five classes are *contiguous unsigned ranges* of `w[30:0]` — so classification is also a plain range lookup, which is both the fastest hand method and literally the comparator chain a Verilog classifier compiles to. Sign-0 half; mirror with bit 31 set for negatives:

```
0x00000000               +0
0x00000001 .. 0x007FFFFF +subnormal
0x00800000 .. 0x7F7FFFFF +normal
0x7F800000               +inf
0x7F800001 .. 0x7FBFFFFF sNaN   (quiet bit clear)
0x7FC00000 .. 0x7FFFFFFF qNaN
```

The reading habit to build: anything strictly between `0x00800000` and `0x7F800000` is an ordinary positive number; every interesting pattern clusters at the two ends of each half. This table is not a mnemonic — it is the *second, independent formulation* of the classification, and "The Classifier in Verilog" below uses it as the reference model against the field-equality formulation, precisely because a misreading of the format would have to be made twice to pass.

## Encoding by Hand: The Algorithm and Its Escapes

Decoding is a lookup; encoding is an algorithm, and it is the algorithm your adder's normalize-and-round stage will run in hardware. By hand:

1. Strip the sign; work with |x|.
2. **Normalize**: find e with 2^e ≤ |x| < 2^(e+1). If e < −126, clamp e = −126 — the subnormal path, with no hidden 1.
3. Compute the 23 fraction bits of |x|/2^e − 1 (normal) or of |x|/2⁻¹²⁶ (subnormal), keeping the bits below position 23 as guard, round and sticky.
4. **Round** — ties-to-even unless stated otherwise.
5. Assemble sign, E = e + 127, fraction — with two escapes: a fraction carry out of rounding bumps the exponent, and e + 127 > 254 means overflow to infinity.

Every example below was verified with `struct` this session, and all of them run as known-answer checks in `src/ch07/tb_encode.v` — the transcript closes this section.

**1.0, the anchor.** 1.0 = 1.0 × 2⁰. Sign 0, E = 0 + 127 = 127 = `0b01111111`, fraction 0. Assembled: `0 01111111 000…0` = **0x3F800000**.

**6.25, a terminating fraction — no rounding decision.** 6.25 = 110.01₂ = 1.1001₂ × 2². Sign 0; E = 2 + 127 = 129; the fraction is the bits after the point, left-aligned into 23 bits: 0.5625 × 2²³ = 4,718,592 = 0x480000 exactly. Assembled **0x40C80000**. Terminating binary fractions are the only encodes with no rounding step — which is why this example comes before 0.1: the next one adds exactly one new ingredient.

**0.1, a repeating fraction that must round.** 0.1 = 1.6 × 2⁻⁴, so E = −4 + 127 = 123. Chapter 6's section "Radix, and the Fraction That Never Terminates" showed 0.1's bits repeat with period 4; the fraction bits of 1.6, computed exactly this session:

```
first 28 fraction bits of 1.6: 1001100110011001100110011001...
kept 23: 10011001100110011001100   G=1 R=1 S=1 (the tail repeats forever)
```

G = 1 with (R|S) = 1 means strictly past halfway: round up. 0x4CCCCC + 1 = 0x4CCCCD. Assembled: **0x3DCCCCCD** — chapter 6's seed, now derived rather than asserted. The stored value is 0.100000001490116119384765625, which is 1.49×10⁻⁹ *above* one tenth: 0.2 ulp of error, comfortably inside round-to-nearest's half-ulp guarantee. Print that decimal in full once and never forget it: "0.1" does not exist in binary32; that number is what does.

**2²⁴ + 1 = 16,777,217, the first integer that does not fit.** It needs 25 bits (`1000000000000000000000001₂`); normalized it is 1.000…001 × 2²⁴ with the trailing 1 landing in the guard position. G = 1, R = 0, S = 0: an *exact tie* between 16,777,216 (F = 0x000000, LSB even) and 16,777,218 (F = 0x000001, LSB odd). Ties-to-even keeps the even neighbour: `float(16777217)` = 16,777,216.0 = **0x4B800000** — the tie broke *down*. Now 16,777,219, which ties between 16,777,218 (odd) and 16,777,220 (even): ties-to-even goes *up*, to **0x4B800002**. One tie each way — chapter 5's row G said a testbench needs both, and now you know why one direction alone would pass a rounder that always rounds up.

**2⁻¹⁴⁰, a subnormal.** 2⁻¹⁴⁰ < 2⁻¹²⁶, so step 2 clamps: E = 0 and the value must be expressed as F × 2⁻¹⁴⁹. F = 2⁻¹⁴⁰/2⁻¹⁴⁹ = 2⁹ = 512 = 0x000200. Assembled: **0x00000200**. Cross-check in the other convention: (0.F)₂ × 2⁻¹²⁶ with 0.F = 512/2²³ = 2⁻¹⁴, and 2⁻¹⁴ × 2⁻¹²⁶ = 2⁻¹⁴⁰. ✓ Exact value 7.174648137343063…×10⁻⁴³.

**The endpoints of the normal range.**

```
min normal  0x00800000 = 0 00000001 000...0  = 2^-126
            = 1.17549435082228750796873653722224567781866555677208752150875e-38
max normal  0x7F7FFFFF = 0 11111110 111...1  = (2 - 2^-23) * 2^127
            = 340282346638528859811704183484516925440  (~3.4028235e38)
```

Both decimals are exact (`Fraction`, 60-digit `Decimal`). And one adjacency worth staring at: the largest subnormal `0x007FFFFF` and the smallest normal `0x00800000` are one pattern apart, and their *values* differ by exactly 2⁻¹⁴⁹ — the encoding crosses the class boundary without a seam. "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp" explains why that is not luck.

The escapes: rounding at the top of a binade can carry the fraction out (0.111…1 rounds to 1.000…0), which increments E — usually harmless, but from E = 254 it lands on 255 and the result is infinity. Overflow is a property of the *rounded* result, not the exact one; "Rounding: Five Attributes, One Default" pins the exact threshold.

The whole set, proved in the simulator (`tb_encode.v`, all 17 known-answer checks against `$shortrealtobits`, which "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory" establishes as a correctly rounding binary32 encoder; transcript abridged to this section's examples):

```
  3f800000  1.0
  40c80000  6.25 (no rounding)
  3dcccccd  0.1 (G=1, R|S=1: up)
  4b800000  2^24+1 (tie -> even, down)
  4b800002  2^24+3 (tie -> even, up)
  00000200  2^-140 (subnormal)
  00800000  min normal
  7f7fffff  max normal
  7f800000  overflow -> +inf
```

## Bias 127, and What the Missing One Buys

Chapter 6's excess-8 toy used the "natural" bias 2^(N−1) = 8. IEEE's is 2^(N−1) − 1 = **127**, one less, fixed by rule in the standard: emax = 2^(w−1) − 1 and emin = 1 − emax (754-2019, the binary-format parameters table; title-only citation). With stored 0 and 255 reserved, the normal exponents are:

```
stored 1..254  ->  actual -126..+127
```

**Where the asymmetry lands.** |emin| = 126 < emax = 127: the spare binade sits at the *top* of the range. The consequences, all `struct`-verified this session:

```
max normal      = (2-2^-23)*2^127 = 3.4028234663852886e+38   bits 7f7fffff
min normal      = 2^-126          = 1.1754943508222875e-38   bits 00800000
min subnormal   = 2^-149          = 1.401298464324817e-45    bits 00000001
1/min_normal    = 2^126  -> bits 7e800000   (exact, a normal -- no overflow)
1/max_normal    = 2.938736e-39 -> bits 00200000  (subnormal, NOT zero)
```

So the reciprocal of every *normal* is representable and nonzero: 1/minnormal lands inside the normals, and 1/maxnormal lands in the subnormals thanks to gradual underflow. A defensible way to state the design logic — rationale, not standard text: underflow has a cushion (subnormals extend reach to 2⁻¹⁴⁹) while overflow is a cliff, so the spare binade goes to the cliff side. Under the bias-128 alternative the top of the range would halve to (2−2⁻²³)·2¹²⁶ while the bottom gained a binade it barely needed. A tidy corollary, verified exactly: minnormal × maxnormal = 4 − 2⁻²² ≈ 4, so the normal range is geometrically centred near 2, not 1.

One identity chapter 6 taught does *not* carry over. Excess-2^(N−1) is two's complement with the MSB flipped — but 127 is 2^(N−1) − 1, and the off-by-one that buys the reserved endpoints also breaks that tidy equivalence. IEEE chose its bias for two other properties, and both are load-bearing for your adder.

**The encoded-order property.** With the fields packed exponent-above-fraction and the exponent biased, the 31 magnitude bits of a float compare like an unsigned integer. Verified two ways this session:

```
all 255 exponent-boundary crossings monotone: True
10^6 random adjacent sign-0 pairs monotone: True
```

The map from bits to value is strictly increasing across the *entire* sign-0, non-NaN range — including the subnormal/normal boundary and the normal/infinity boundary, with no special cases. That is why the hex-range table in "Decoding by Hand: Fields, Classes, and the Hex Ranges" works at all, and it is why chapter 8's compare-and-swap step — which operand is bigger — will be answered by one 31-bit unsigned comparator on `w[30:0]`.

The caveat from chapter 6's corrected claim carries forward unchanged: this is a **magnitude** property. The sign bit must be handled aside, because whole floats do not compare lexically across signs:

```
-1.0 bits bf800000   2.0 bits 40000000   lexical order says -1.0 > 2.0: True
```

Negative floats sort in *reversed* bit order — sign-magnitude, not two's complement — and −0/+0 compare equal numerically while differing in bit 31.

**The reserved-endpoints property.** Stored 0 and stored 255 are not wasted patterns; they are where the format keeps everything that is not an ordinary number. Because they sit at the *ends* of the biased scale, the specials land at the ends of the unsigned magnitude order — zeros and subnormals at the bottom, infinity and NaN at the top — which is exactly what keeps the monotonicity seamless and the classifier a chain of range compares.

**Dynamic range, closing chapter 6's account.** "Where Fixed Point Runs Out" measured Q16.16 at 2⁴⁷ ≈ 14.15 decades. Binary32, verified this session: maxnormal/minnormal = 2²⁵⁴ ≈ **76.46 decades**, and with the subnormal ramp maxnormal/minsubnormal = 2²⁷⁷ ≈ **83.39 decades** — nearly six times the decades of Q16.16 in the same 32 bits, bought by spending 8 bits on a scale field and paying with 24-bit rather than 31-bit precision. That is the fixed-versus-float trade in one line.

> **Seed for chapters 8 and 9.** Biases cancel in a difference: the alignment question "whose exponent is bigger, and by how much?" is one 8-bit unsigned subtract of raw exponent fields, and the operand-magnitude question is one 31-bit unsigned compare. No un-biasing hardware exists anywhere in an FP adder. (Multiplication is the operation that pays bias arithmetic — raw fields add to E_A + E_B, twice-biased, and the correct stored result is E_A + E_B − 127, so one bias must be re-subtracted. Addition's freedom from this is an underappreciated simplification.)

## The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp

Normals get 24 significand bits out of a 23-bit field because normalization *forces* the leading bit: in base 2, the leading digit of a normalized number can only be 1, so storing it would waste a wire. The decoder prepends it: significand = `{1'b1, F}` when E ≠ 0.

But the free bit has a precondition. The exponent field bottoms out at stored 1 (actual −126), and a value below 2⁻¹²⁶ cannot slide its leading 1 up to the point — the exponent has nowhere left to go. Its significand must start `0.`, and those leading zeros must be *stored*, eating precision. That is a **subnormal**: fixed scale, variable precision, from 23 significant bits at `0x007FFFFF` down to a single bit at `0x00000001`. (C's names preserve the classic confusion: `FLT_MIN` is the min *normal* 2⁻¹²⁶; the min subnormal 2⁻¹⁴⁹ is `FLT_TRUE_MIN`.)

**The −126/−149 reconciliation.** Both numbers appear in the literature for "the subnormal exponent", and confusing them is a classic error. For E = 0:

```
value = (0.F) * 2^-126      -- fractional significand, effective exponent -126
      = F     * 2^-149      -- integer significand, scale of the fraction LSB
```

Verified identical this session on `0x00000200`: both give 7.174648e-43. −126 is the *exponent*; −149 = −126 − 23 is the *weight of the fraction's LSB*. And there is a wrong third reading, measured so you recognize its fingerprint: plugging E = 0 into the normal formula's 2^(E−127) decodes `0x00000200` to 3.587324e-43 — **off by exactly 2×**. Stored exponents 0 and 1 share the scale 2⁻¹²⁶; the step from E = 0 to E = 1 is compensated by the hidden bit *appearing*, not by the scale changing. Chapter 5's row B calls the resulting datapath mistake the commonest subnormal bug; a factor-of-two error on subnormals is its signature.

**What gradual underflow buys.** Delete the subnormals and the number line develops a cliff, measured:

```
gap from 0 to min normal (if subnormals removed):  2^-126 = 1.175494e-38
gap from min normal to the next normal:            2^-149 = 1.401298e-45
ratio: 2^23 = 8,388,608
```

Without subnormals, the first gap above zero is over eight million times wider than the gaps just above it. With them, 2²³ − 1 values fill (0, 2⁻¹²⁶) at uniform spacing 2⁻¹⁴⁹, and the spacing is monotone all the way down: zero is approached by a ramp, not a cliff.

The property that pays the rent (Goldberg's property (10), verified here): **x = y if and only if x − y = 0**. Take the two adjacent normals `0x00800001` and `0x00800000`: their difference is 2⁻¹⁴⁹ = bits `0x00000001` — *nonzero*, because the difference of two normals closer than 2⁻¹²⁶ is subnormal and representable. Goldberg's motivating fragment is `if (x != y) then z = 1/(x-y)`: without subnormals it dies by spurious division by zero on exactly such pairs. Related, and load-bearing for chapter 8's error analysis: **Sterbenz's lemma** — if y/2 ≤ x ≤ 2y then x − y is exact. Spot-checked this session over 200,000 conforming random pairs: zero inexact differences.

**Exponent promotion comes free.** Chapter 5's row B vectors, re-verified this session:

```
00400000 + 00400000 = 00800000     (0.5 + 0.5) * 2^-126 -> min normal
007fffff + 00000001 = 00800000     largest subnormal + one ulp
00200000 + 00600000 = 00800000     (0.25 + 0.75) * 2^-126
```

The mechanism, exactly: subnormals and the bottom binade of normals share the per-LSB scale 2⁻¹⁴⁹ (verified: 1.0 × 2⁻¹²⁶ ≡ 2²³ × 2⁻¹⁴⁹), so a carry out of fraction bit 22 lands in bit 23 — which *is* the hidden-1 position of stored exponent 1 at the same scale. The adder's raw sum `{carry, fraction}` under E = 0 is already the correct `{E = 1, fraction}` encoding: exponent 0→1 with no shift and no special case. This is the single prettiest hardware consequence of the encoding, it is why the largest-subnormal/smallest-normal adjacency in "Encoding by Hand: The Algorithm and Its Escapes" is seamless, and chapter 9's datapath gets it for free — the one-line decode that unlocks it is in "The Classifier in Verilog" below.

**Flush-to-zero, the cop-out.** FTZ replaces subnormal results with zero (its sibling DAZ zeroes subnormal *inputs* too); GPUs, DSPs and `-ffast-math` do it because a denormalized significand drags a leading-zero counter and shifter into paths that otherwise skip them. The cost, stated precisely: every x in (0, 2⁻¹²⁶) maps to 0 — 100% relative error across the whole band, where gradual underflow keeps the absolute error of any rounding at most 2⁻¹⁵⁰ and loses relative accuracy only gradually. And the x − y property dies: `0x00800001 − 0x00800000` flushes to 0 while x ≠ y. Chapter 5 already said the verification half of this: FTZ is a *specification*, not an optimization, and if chapter 12's adder flushed, its reference model would have to flush identically. This guide's adder will not flush; that decision is now written down.

## Zeros, Infinities, and the Anatomy of NaN

**Signed zeros.** Two zeros exist because the format is sign-magnitude — 2³¹ patterns on each side of the sign bit — and rather than paper over the duplicate, the standard makes it useful. Where the sign of zero is *observable*, measured this session (exact double computations; every one is also a binary32 fact):

```
bits(+0.0) = 0x00000000    bits(-0.0) = 0x80000000    +0 == -0 numerically: True
1/+0 = +inf     1/-0 = -inf
(-0.0)*5.0   = -0.0        (+0.0)*(-5.0) = -0.0       sign = XOR of signs, even at zero
sqrt(-0.0)   = -0.0
atan2(+0,-1) = +pi         atan2(-0,-1) = -pi         the branch-cut use case
copysign(1, -0.0) = -1.0                              the sign is really there
```

Note what is *absent* from that list: `==`. Numeric comparison cannot see the sign of zero; only bit inspection, copysign/signbit, or division can. For the adder, the sign of a zero *result* is a rule, not a computation — magnitude subtraction produces zero with no sign of its own, so the hardware must hard-code the standard's choice (chapter 5's row A). The rule itself is in "Rounding: Five Attributes, One Default", because it is attribute-dependent.

**Infinities.** `0x7F800000` and `0xFF800000` — E = 255 with a zero fraction. They are deliberate, ordered values: bigger than every finite number, symmetric under negation, and the default result of overflow. For addition, measured this session:

```
inf + inf = inf     inf + finite = inf     -inf + finite = -inf     inf + 0 = inf
inf + (-inf) = qNaN, and the invalid exception   <- the one addition that invents a NaN
```

**NaN anatomy.** E = 255, F ≠ 0. Fraction bit 22 — the MSB of the trailing significand field — is the **quiet bit**: 1 means quiet NaN, 0 means signalling. Bits 21:0 are the **payload**: 22 bits of freight the format carries and no arithmetic reads. Populations: 8,388,608 qNaNs and 8,388,606 sNaNs per the census — the two missing sNaN patterns are ±infinity, which is exactly why a signalling NaN's payload can never be all zeros: that pattern is taken.

What 754-2019 §6.2 *requires* versus *recommends* matters more here than anywhere else in the standard (title-only citations, clause numbers as in the 2019 text):

- **Shall**: an operation that signals invalid and delivers a floating-point result delivers a **quiet** NaN (§6.2). Quiet-NaN inputs (with no sNaN present) produce a quiet NaN and signal nothing.
- **Should**: the quiet-bit-is-MSB *encoding itself is only a recommendation* (§6.2.1) — universal today on x86, ARM and RISC-V, but historically inverted on PA-RISC and pre-2008 MIPS. Cross-platform NaN bit-compares are a portability bug even between "IEEE machines".
- **Should**: payload *propagation* (§6.2.3) — the result of an operation with NaN inputs should be one of those NaNs, quieted if it was signalling. Hardware is free to return a canonical qNaN instead, and some ARM configurations do ("default NaN" mode).

On this machine, propagation and quieting behave as recommended through the conversion-plus-add path (Icarus 13.0, `tb_lab.v`, transcript in "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory"): a qNaN with payload 85 survives `+ 1.0` intact, and an sNaN comes out with the quiet bit *set* and the payload preserved — though Trap 2 of "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory" shows the conversion alone already quiets, so this is a measurement of the path, not of the add operation's §6.2.3 behaviour in isolation.

**The sign of a generated NaN is unspecified** — §6.3 pins a NaN's sign only through copy, negate, abs and copySign. This is not a paper subtlety; it is a measured portability lesson in three acts. Act one: this x86-64 host produces `ffc00000` — a *negative* qNaN, the x86 "QNaN floating-point indefinite" — for runtime `inf + (-inf)`, `0/0` and `inf*0`. Act two: the arm64 machine that ran earlier chapters of this guide produced `7fc00000` for the same expression. Act three, sharpest of all: on *this single machine*, Icarus's compile-time constant folder gives `0.0/0.0` a **positive** sign while the runtime FPU gives the same expression a **negative** one — both signs from one simulator, measured this session. The rule for every testbench from chapter 8 onward, stated once and never relaxed: **never compare a generated NaN's full bit pattern. Classify — E = 255, F ≠ 0 — then check the quiet bit.** Payload and sign comparison is legitimate only for NaNs the testbench itself injected.

Behaviour worth one line each: any comparison with a NaN is unordered, so `x != x` is the standard self-test (verified: true for `0x7FC00055`); NaN propagates through all four arithmetic operations; and NaN beats infinity in the decode priority — chapter 5's row C — because `inf + (-inf)` must produce NaN, not infinity. One line of colour: 2²² payload bits per sign is real estate, and dynamic-language runtimes famously "NaN-box" entire pointers into binary64 payloads (folklore, title-only); for this guide's hardware, the payload is freight to preserve, never to invent.

## The Classifier in Verilog

Everything so far compiles to remarkably little hardware, and it is time to build it. `src/ch07/fp32_class.v` is the five-class decode table as combinational logic — the core of the file, verbatim:

```verilog
  wire [7:0]  e = w[30:23];     // stored exponent field
  wire [22:0] f = w[22:0];      // trailing significand field

  wire e_zero = (e == 8'd0);    // bottom reserved exponent
  wire e_ones = (e == 8'd255);  // top reserved exponent
  wire f_zero = (f == 23'd0);

  assign sign    = w[31];
  assign is_zero =  e_zero &  f_zero;
  assign is_sub  =  e_zero & ~f_zero;
  assign is_norm = ~e_zero & ~e_ones;
  assign is_inf  =  e_ones &  f_zero;
  assign is_nan  =  e_ones & ~f_zero;
  assign is_qnan = is_nan  &  f[22];
  assign is_snan = is_nan  & ~f[22];
```

Two 8-bit equality checks, one 23-bit zero check, one bit read, a handful of gates. The port contract is one-hot by construction — exactly one of the five class outputs is high for every one of the 2³² patterns, `is_qnan | is_snan == is_nan` — and the sign is reported for every class, NaN included, because what a NaN's sign *means* is the caller's problem.

Its companion `fp32_fields.v` is the unified decode that "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp" promised — the whole subnormal story in three assignments:

```verilog
  assign hidden = |e_raw;
  assign sig    = {hidden, frac};
  assign e_eff  = hidden ? e_raw : 8'd1;
```

`e_eff = max(E, 1)`: treat E = 0 as exponent field 1 with a zero hidden bit, and *no other subnormal special case exists in the align/add datapath*. This line is the vaccine for the halve-every-subnormal bug.

> **Seed for chapters 9 and 12.** These two modules are deliverables, not demos. Chapter 9 instantiates both on the adder's operand path (NaN must be screened before the magnitude compare; `sig` and `e_eff` feed the aligner) and chapter 12 instantiates `fp32_class` again on the *result* path for its flag unit, which needs `is_snan` and `is_inf`. Their ports are shaped for that: one-hot classes plus the quiet split, not an encoded enum a consumer would have to re-decode.

The verification is this chapter's own medicine. `tb_class.v` drives a 3,072-pattern boundary sweep — both signs × all 256 exponent fields × six fraction values chosen to force every class transition the format has (zero, minimal nonzero, largest quiet-bit-clear, smallest quiet-bit-set, a payload carrier, all ones) — against **three independent references**: the hex-range model from "Decoding by Hand: Fields, Classes, and the Hex Ranges" (unsigned range compares on `w[30:0]` — a different formulation from the DUT's field equalities), a one-hot invariant, and the simulator's own value-domain decoder (`$bitstoshortreal`: NaN by `v != v`, zero by `v == 0`, infinity and subnormals by magnitude against the class endpoints). The value-domain reference deliberately skips the quiet/signalling split, because conversion *quiets* an sNaN on this host — "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory" measures that — so the split is pinned instead by twenty directed vectors whose expected outputs were computed with an independent `python3` script this session. Sweep-count and directed-count guards make a sweep that silently didn't run a failure rather than a pass. `tb_fields.v` proves the unified decode over 3,060 finite patterns with *exact equality* against `$bitstoshortreal` — no tolerance, since both sides are exact doubles — and pins the factor-of-two fingerprint directly. ## ULP, Epsilon, and Where the Integers End

The **ulp** — unit in the last place — is the value of one step of the fraction's LSB, and it is the yardstick every FP error claim is measured in. For x in the binade [2^e, 2^(e+1)): **ulp(x) = 2^(e−23)**. Two definitions circulate (Goldberg's is per-representation; Harrison's is the gap between the floats straddling x — cf. Muller, "On the definition of ulp(x)", title-only) and they differ only at binade edges: at 2.0 the gap below is 2⁻²³ and the gap above is 2⁻²², both measured. This guide uses the binade convention above and, for adder error, always says "ulp *of the result*".

The spacing table, each row the measured gap to the adjacent representable value — upward, as value(bits+1) − value(bits), for every row except max normal, whose upward neighbour is +∞; that row's gap is measured downward, via `7F7FFFFE`..`7F7FFFFF`:

```
     x        bits         ulp(x)      as 2^k    ulp/x
   1.0    3F800000   1.192093e-07     2^-23    1.19e-07
   2.0    40000000   2.384186e-07     2^-22    1.19e-07
  10.0    41200000   9.536743e-07     2^-20    9.54e-08
  1000    447A0000   6.103516e-05     2^-14    6.10e-08
  10^6    49742400   6.250000e-02     2^-4     6.25e-08
  2^23    4B000000   1.000000e+00     2^0      1.19e-07
  2^24    4B800000   2.000000e+00     2^1      1.19e-07
  maxnorm 7F7FFFFF   2.028241e+31     2^104    ~6e-08
  2^-126  00800000   1.401298e-45     2^-149   1.19e-07
  2^-140  00000200   1.401298e-45     2^-149   1.95e-03   <- subnormal: relative gap blows up
```

Read the last column: *relative* spacing is constant within a factor of two across 76 orders of magnitude — the defining property of floating point, and the exact opposite of fixed point's constant *absolute* spacing — until the subnormal band trades it away, which is the gradual part of gradual underflow.

Read the middle rows: at 2²³ the ulp reaches 1, and above 2²⁴ it exceeds 2. Hence **every integer with |n| ≤ 2²⁴ = 16,777,216 is exactly representable, and 16,777,217 is the first that is not** — verified by an exhaustive sweep of every integer up to the boundary in this chapter's research pass, and re-pinned at the boundary this session (16,777,215 ✓, 16,777,216 ✓, 16,777,217 ✗ → 16,777,216). Quote the sharp boundary, not "about 16 million": an accumulator, a frame counter, a sample index in binary32 is exact until precisely there and silently wrong after.

**Machine epsilon has two conventions, one factor of two apart — always say which.** The C/`FLT_EPSILON`/numpy convention: ε = spacing of the [1, 2) binade = **2⁻²³** ≈ 1.1921×10⁻⁷ (verified: nextafter(1.0) − 1.0). Goldberg's paper *defines* machine epsilon as the maximum relative rounding error, (β/2)β^(−p) = **2⁻²⁴** — what modern usage calls the **unit roundoff u**. Reconciliation: u = ε/2. The bound that matters for chapter 8: round-to-nearest guarantees relative error ≤ u = 2⁻²⁴ ≈ 5.9605×10⁻⁸ for any normal-range result; measured over 10⁶ random conversions this session, the worst case observed was 5.9504×10⁻⁸ — under the bound, as it must be.

The folk definition "epsilon is the smallest x with 1 + x ≠ 1" needs care under ties-to-even. Measured (and pinned in `tb_encode.v`):

```
1.0 + 2^-23           -> 3f800001   (eps survives the add)
1.0 + 2^-24           -> 3f800000   (exact tie; the even side is 1.0)
1.0 + (2^-24 + 2^-30) -> 3f800001   (a hair past the tie: up)
```

So values *below* ε already bump 1.0 — anything strictly past the tie point does — and ε = 2⁻²³ is precisely the smallest *power of two* that does. The folk definition is off unless you add that qualifier.

And the payoff chapter 6 promised when it first showed you 0.1: accumulate the stored 0.1 ten times in true binary32 — rounding after every add, which in this toolchain means round-tripping through the bits functions, as "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory" shows — and the sum is not 1.0:

```
  0.1 x 10 in binary32 = 3f800001 = 1.0000001192
```

One ulp *above* 1.0 (each stored 0.1 is 0.2 ulp high, and eight of the ten adds are themselves inexact — measured: only the first two are exact). The same sum in double lands just *below* 1.0 (0.9999999999999999): same phenomenon, different accumulation path, and neither equals 1.0. `while (sum != 1.0)` on a 0.1 accumulator is the canonical resulting bug, in any language and any width. This check runs green in `tb_encode.v` on every regression.

## Rounding: Five Attributes, One Default

The standard defines five **rounding-direction attributes** (754-2019 §4.3, title-only): **roundTiesToEven** (RNE — the binary default), **roundTiesToAway** (RNA — required only for decimal formats), **roundTowardZero** (RTZ), **roundTowardPositive** (RTP) and **roundTowardNegative** (RTN). This guide implements RNE and treats the rest as things you must be able to *recognize*, because a wrong tie-break is precisely the kind of bug that passes every casual test.

The behaviour table, produced by a from-scratch five-attribute rounder over exact rationals built for this chapter's research and cross-checked against `struct`'s RNE on 100,000 random doubles with zero mismatches:

```
           value      RNE      RNA      RTZ      RTP      RTN
          2^24+1 4B800000 4B800001 4B800000 4B800001 4B800000
          2^24+3 4B800002 4B800002 4B800001 4B800002 4B800001
         1+2^-24 3F800000 3F800001 3F800000 3F800001 3F800000
       1+3*2^-24 3F800002 3F800002 3F800001 3F800002 3F800001
      -(1+2^-24) BF800000 BF800001 BF800000 BF800000 BF800001
             0.1 3DCCCCCD 3DCCCCCD 3DCCCCCC 3DCCCCCD 3DCCCCCC
  maxn*(1+2^-25) 7F7FFFFF 7F7FFFFF 7F7FFFFF 7F800000 7F7FFFFF
           2^128 7F800000 7F800000 7F7FFFFF 7F800000 7F7FFFFF
```

Read down the columns: RNE alone rounds ties by LSB parity (down at 2²⁴+1, up at 2²⁴+3 — the two directions "Encoding by Hand: The Algorithm and Its Escapes" worked); RNA always breaks away from zero; RTZ truncates; and the directed modes are asymmetric in sign — see the −(1+2⁻²⁴) row, where RTP truncates the *negative* value. RNE's statistical virtue is chapter 6's half-even lesson at a new width: ties split by parity instead of always up, so long accumulations do not acquire a systematic drift from the tie cases.

The last two rows are the overflow story. Overflow is decided on the value *rounded as if the exponent were unbounded*, and the exact threshold is **(2 − 2⁻²⁴)·2¹²⁷** = maxnormal + half an ulp: just below it → `7F7FFFFF`; at it — an exact tie whose "even" neighbour is 2¹²⁸ — RNE gives `7F800000`. The §7.4 defaults at overflow differ by attribute: RNE/RNA → ±∞; RTZ → ±maxfinite; RTP/RTN → ∞ only in their own direction (that is the `maxn*(1+2^-25)` row: RTP overflows where everyone else stays finite). Both threshold facts run as known-answer checks in `tb_encode.v`.

What each attribute does to (+0) + (−0) is a *separate rule, not a rounding* — §6.3, standard text: an exact zero sum of operands with unlike signs is **+0 under every attribute except roundTowardNegative**, where it is −0; a zero sum of like-signed zeros keeps that sign in all modes. Measured under RNE this session and pinned in `tb_lab.v`:

```
(+0)+(+0) = +0     (-0)+(-0) = -0      <- like signs: that sign
(+0)+(-0) = +0     (-0)+(+0) = +0      <- unlike signs, exact zero
x + (-x)  = +0     (both orders, x = 1.5)
```

This is the hard-coded rule chapter 5's row A demanded, now with its justification: no datapath produces it, so the adder must state it.

**The decision logic itself** is one gate expression away from hardware you already own. Chapter 2's "Reduction Operators and the Sticky Bit" built `align_sticky.v` with guard, round and sticky as three separate ports; with L the LSB of the kept significand, RNE is:

```
round_up = G & (R | S | L)
```

verified exhaustively this session over all 1,024 six-bit-significand × dropped-bit combinations against arithmetic rounding, zero mismatches. Reading: G=1 with (R|S)=1 is strictly past halfway — up; G=1 with R=S=0 is an exact tie — up only if L is odd; G=0 — down, regardless. The other four attributes replace only this equation (RTZ: never up; RNA: up iff G; RTP/RTN: up iff any of G, R, S is set and the sign agrees). The full align→add→normalize→round pipeline — including why normalization can promote G to a real significand bit before the decision — is chapter 8's business; this chapter stops at the decision table.

## The Flags an Adder Can Actually Raise

The standard defines five exceptions (754-2019 clause 7, title-only), each with a status flag and a default result. Specialized to *addition* — because that is the only operation this guide builds — the table collapses dramatically, and the collapse is provable:

| flag | raised by addition when | default result |
|---|---|---|
| invalid (§7.2) | an operand is sNaN; or inf + (−inf) — magnitude subtraction of infinities | qNaN |
| divideByZero (§7.3) | **never** | — |
| overflow (§7.4) | \|result rounded as if the exponent were unbounded\| > maxnormal | ±∞ or ±maxfinite by attribute |
| underflow (§7.5) | tiny AND inexact — **never for addition**, see below | the subnormal or zero result |
| inexact (§7.6) | rounded result ≠ exact result | the rounded result |

**divideByZero never fires** by definition: it is specified for an exact infinite result from *finite* operands (1/0 is the archetype), and the exact sum of two finite numbers is always finite. An infinite addition result comes either from an infinite operand (no exception) or from overflow (a different flag).

**Underflow never fires either, and this one deserves its proof.** Every binary32 value is an integer multiple of 2⁻¹⁴⁹ — normals and subnormals alike (spot-verified across classes this session). So the *exact* sum of two of them is an integer multiple of 2⁻¹⁴⁹. If that sum is tiny — |sum| < 2⁻¹²⁶ = 2²³·2⁻¹⁴⁹ — then it is k·2⁻¹⁴⁹ with |k| < 2²³, which is *exactly* a subnormal or zero: representable, no rounding needed. A tiny addition result is therefore always exact, and default underflow requires tiny **and** inexact. The empirical hammer, re-run this session: 400,000 random cancelling pairs produced 29,971 tiny sums, and the number of inexact results among them was **zero**. (The research pass's generator produced 15,550 tiny sums from the same pair count — the tiny *rate* is a property of the generator's exponent distribution, and the zero-inexact result held under both.) This corrects chapter 5's cross-cutting flags note, which listed underflow among the flags binary32 addition raises per Clause 7; that enumeration is right for operations in general and provably wrong for addition, and chapter 5 now carries a dated correction saying so. (The standard offers two tininess-detection choices, before or after rounding; exactness kills the flag under both, so the conclusion does not depend on that choice.)

**Overflow, by contrast, has two distinct hardware paths**, and chapter 5's row H already demanded a test for each. Magnitude overflow: maxnormal + maxnormal = 6.8×10³⁸, over the threshold, → +∞. Rounding-induced overflow: maxnormal + 2¹⁰³ has an exact sum of maxnormal + ulp/2 — *below* 2¹²⁸ — but the tie against the all-ones (odd) significand rounds *up*, carrying out and pushing the exponent past 254: infinity, raised by the *rounder*, not the adder. Both paths measured this session and pinned in `tb_lab.v`:

```
7f7fffff + 7f7fffff -> 7f800000     magnitude overflow
7f7fffff + 2^103    -> 7f800000     rounding-induced overflow
```

**Inexact is the everyday flag** — it fires on most real additions. Exactness examples, checked with `Fraction`: 1.0 + 2⁻²⁴ is inexact (the tie rounds the addend away entirely); 2²⁴ + 1.0 is inexact (the ulp is 2 there); 1.0 + 1.0 and 0.5 + 0.25 are exact.

> **Seed for chapter 12.** The four-input adder's flag specification can now be written honestly: outputs for **invalid, overflow and inexact**, and either no underflow wire at all — with the proof above cited in the spec — or one tied low with a comment. Never an underflow wire that pretends to compute something. Chapter 5's taxonomy demanded this decision be made in writing; this section is that writing, and divideByZero is likewise out of scope by definition, not omission. Note the proof is for a single two-input addition: it applies to each node of chapter 10's adder tree individually, since each node adds two binary32 values.

## shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory

Chapter 2 found that Icarus 13.0 supports `shortreal` with `$shortrealtobits` / `$bitstoshortreal`, and chapter 5 sharpened it: a `shortreal` is stored as a *double*. This chapter's research sharpened it once more, and the result changes how every later testbench must be written. All of the following is measured on this machine, asserted as 36 known-answer checks in `src/ch07/tb_lab.v`, and quoted from its transcript.

**Trap 1: assignment does not round.** The natural belief is that `shortreal s = r;` rounds r to binary32. It does not — the variable keeps the full double, and the *only* place binary32 rounding happens is inside `$shortrealtobits`, at the call:

```
(1) s = 1 + 2^-24: $shortrealtobits(s) = 3f800000, yet s - 1.0 = 5.960464e-08
```

The conversion *looks* like it rounded — the bits come out as 1.0 — but `s > 1.0` still evaluates true and the whole 2⁻²⁴ is still in the variable. A `shortreal` in Icarus is a double wearing a costume. Consequence: there is no way to "keep a shortreal rounded"; a binary32 golden model must round-trip **every intermediate** through `$shortrealtobits`/`$bitstoshortreal`, exactly as chapter 5's most-dangerous-trap warning said, and as `tb_encode.v`'s accumulation loop does. One add followed by one `$shortrealtobits` is still trustworthy: rounding through binary64 then binary32 provably equals rounding once when the wide format has at least 2p+2 digits (Figueroa, title-only; 53 ≥ 2·24 + 2), re-verified empirically twice: ~992,000 random pairs in the research pass and 200,000 more in the writing session, zero mismatches in both. That one-operation guarantee is what makes `$shortrealtobits` a legitimate in-simulator reference for chapter 9 — and the chain rule is what keeps it one.

**Trap 2: a pure round-trip quiets an sNaN.** No arithmetic at all — convert to the value domain and back, and the quiet bit comes back set:

```
(2) pure $bitstoshortreal -> $shortrealtobits round-trips:
  7fa00000 -> 7fe00000   CHANGED
  7f800001 -> 7fc00001   CHANGED
  7fc00055 -> 7fc00055
  ffc00055 -> ffc00055
  00000001 -> 00000001
  80000000 -> 80000000
  7f800000 -> 7f800000
```

Both conversions pass through the host's float32↔float64 instructions, and x86's conversion quiets an sNaN (payload kept, bit 22 set). Python's `struct` does exactly the same — this is a *host-FPU* behaviour, not an Icarus quirk. Quiet NaNs, payloads and all, and every finite boundary value round-trip bit-exactly. Consequence for every later chapter: **an sNaN bit pattern cannot survive any trip through `shortreal`, `real`, or python `float`.** Testbenches that need an sNaN operand must build its bits directly as a `reg [31:0]`, and reference models must handle sNaN at the bit level before touching the value domain. Arithmetic behaves the same way, with the payload riding through: `qNaN(payload 55) + 1.0 = 7fc00055` intact; `sNaN 7fa00000 + 1.0 = 7fe00000`, quieted with the payload preserved — the *should*-propagation of §6.2.3, honoured on this host.

**Trap 3: both NaN signs from one simulator.** The transcript that makes the portability lesson concrete:

```
(3) runtime 0/0 = ffc00000, constant-folded 0.0/0.0 = 7fc00000
    inf + (-inf)  = ffc00000  (the one addition that invents a NaN)
```

Same expression, opposite signs: the compile-time constant folder produces the positive qNaN, the runtime FPU produces x86's negative one. `tb_lab.v` therefore *asserts the class and the quiet bit* of these results and only prints the bits; the sign assertions that would pass today would be wrong on the arm64 machine this guide's earlier chapters ran on, and possibly wrong under a future Icarus that folds more (or less) aggressively. This transcript is architecture-dependent by design and flagged as such. Meanwhile `1.0/0.0 = 7f800000` and `-1.0/0.0 = ff800000` with *no diagnostic whatsoever*: real division by zero yields infinities silently, so an accidental `/ 0.0` in a reference model surfaces as an infinity comparison failure three testbenches later, not as a warning.

**Trap 4: printing lies near the edges.** All asserted via `$sformatf` string compares:

```
(4) %f of 2^-149 prints 0.000000; %e prints 1.401298e-45; %h of 2.5 is 3
```

`%f` prints every subnormal — and even the minimum *normal*, 1.18×10⁻³⁸ — as `0.000000`, so below about 10⁻⁶ real signal activity is invisible under `%f`; use `%e` or `%g`. `-0` does print as `-0.000000`, so signed zero *is* visible in logs (and `%f`/`%g` of NaN can print `-nan`, leaking the very sign nondeterminism trap 3 warns about into transcript diffs). And `%h` of a `shortreal` does not print the encoding: it converts real→integer first, *rounding ties away from zero* — 2.5 prints `3`, 2.49 prints `2`, −1.5 prints `fffffffffffffffe` (−2 in 64 bits) — while `$rtoi(2.5)` *truncates* to 2. Three different real→integer behaviours in one testbench line; the only way to see encoding bits is `$shortrealtobits`.

One last trap sits in the language, not the library. **`-2.0**128` is +2¹²⁸ in Verilog.** Unary minus binds *tighter* than `**` (IEEE 1800 precedence, title-only; behaviour measured this session), so `-2.0**128` = (−2.0)¹²⁸ = +2¹²⁸ → `7f800000` — while python parses the same spelling as −(2¹²⁸). A reference model or worked example transliterated between the two silently negates. Write `-(2.0**128)` (measured: `ff800000`). Both spellings run as checks in `tb_encode.v`, so the regression itself now documents the precedence.

## A Short History of the Format

The format you can now decode by hand was designed in the late 1970s, mostly by William Kahan, and the primary source for this section is Charles Severance's 1998 interview with him (fetched and read in this chapter's research pass; link in the sources). The design grew out of the **K-C-S draft** — Kahan, his student Jerome Coonen, and visiting professor Harold Stone — written, in Kahan's words, for "a mass market" of non-specialist programmers. Intel's John Palmer authorized Kahan to disclose the planned i8087's "precisions, exponent ranges, special values" to the standards effort; the 8087 itself was Intel's bid to put, again per the interview, one chip with most of the essentials of a math library into every PC — at 40,000 transistors, which is why not everything fit.

**Gradual underflow was the fight.** The interview's figure — quote it as Kahan's anecdote, not as a measurement — is "at least a victim per month per machine" falling into the underflow gap during the 1970s: programs dividing by a tiny difference that had flushed to zero, exactly the failure "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp" demonstrated. DEC opposed K-C-S, and commissioned G. W. Stewart III — a respected error-analysis authority — to make the case against gradual underflow. At the 1981 Boston p754 meeting, Stewart reported that he had concluded gradual underflow was the right thing to do — in the interview's words, a "substantial setback" for DEC "on their home turf". The standard was ratified as **IEEE 754-1985**, having already become a de facto standard: the 8087 shipped in 1980, and its clones carried the format into the world before the committee finished canonizing it. Revisions: 754-2008 (FMA, decimal formats, the formalized *should* on the quiet-bit encoding), 754-2019 (the clause numbers used in this chapter; title-only).

Two honesty notes. First, DEC's position was not stupid: VAX hardware was fast and clean without subnormals, and flush-to-zero remains the shipping behaviour of many accelerators *today* — teach the fight as an engineering trade that IEEE decided in favour of the naive programmer, not as good versus evil. Second, the fact that the "wrong" side's own expert witness switched sides is the best one-sentence summary of how strong the technical case for gradual underflow actually is.

## Bridge to Chapter 8

You now hold the format the way a designer must: not as a diagram but as a set of mechanical consequences. The magnitude bits order like an unsigned integer, so operand comparison is one comparator. Biases cancel in differences, so alignment is one raw-field subtract. `max(E, 1)` and a hidden bit make subnormals ordinary. A carry across the bottom binade boundary re-encodes itself. The classifier is a handful of gates, and you shipped it.

Chapter 8 assembles these into the floating point addition algorithm: unpack (this chapter's `fp32_fields`), screen the specials (this chapter's `fp32_class`, with NaN beating infinity), compare and swap on magnitude, align the smaller significand rightward while `align_sticky` from chapter 2's "Reduction Operators and the Sticky Bit" catches what falls off, add or subtract the 24-bit significands as plain integers — chapter 6's odometer, one last time — then normalize, round with `G & (R | S | L)`, and re-assemble through the escapes you already know: promotion at the bottom, infinity past the top. Every one of those steps is a section of this chapter or an earlier one wearing datapath clothes. What chapter 8 adds is the *order* they run in, the width every intermediate needs so nothing is lost before the rounder decides, and the two places the naive pipeline loses a bit anyway. The first number it will add is one you can now take apart blindfolded: `0x3DCCCCCD`.
