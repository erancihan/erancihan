## Three Adders, Two Shapes, One Specification Problem

Chapter 9 closed with a design, a question, and a debt. The design is `fp32_add2`: seven contracts, a top that is wiring, proven bit-identical to chapter 8's golden algorithm over 200,092 checks. The debt — the latent `exact_zero` bug its bridge told this chapter to watch under reuse — is settled near the end, in the direction nobody wants. The question is the one its bridge could only pose: when you compose adders, each interior edge packs to binary32 and unpacks again, rounding at every node — and *whose job is it to say in what order?* This chapter answers it with measurements, and the answer turns out to be stranger and more useful than "pick a tree."

The guide's goal is a four-input adder, and four operands admit more than one association. This chapter builds both natural ones — `fp32_add4_tree` computing `(a+b)+(c+d)` and `fp32_add4_seq` computing `((a+b)+c)+d` — as pure compositions of chapter 9's module, and then asks the three questions composition raises, in order of how badly folklore answers them. **Correctness:** each structure is proven bit-identical, flags included, to an order-faithful reference — 240,000 quadruples × 2 structures × 2 independent reference styles this session, zero mismatches. **Accuracy:** against the exact single-rounding four-operand sum, a 1.2-million-quadruple sweep finds *no systematic winner* between tree and sequential — the folklore that trees are more accurate is not a measurable n = 4 effect, and this chapter kills it with numbers. **Specification:** one multiset of four operands, `{max, max, −max, −max}`, is shown returning **qNaN, +inf, or +0** depending on which structure computes it *and in what order the values arrive at the ports* — so chapter 12's design document must pin not just a tree shape but a port-to-operand mapping, or its corner suite tests an under-specified design.

Composition is a new design decision, not more of the same. Nothing in this chapter touches the arithmetic inside `fp32_add2`; every result below is a property of *wiring three verified adders together* — which is exactly why the chapter's verification discipline (the reference must replicate the composition, add by add, round-trip by round-trip) and its two headline traps (an order-unfaithful reference, and the false oracle "both orders agreed") live at the interfaces, where chapter 9 taught you the new bugs live. The working method is unchanged: Icarus Verilog 13.0, `iverilog -g2012 -Wall` with zero-output compiles on every target, python3 exact-arithmetic models validated before use (620,400 cross-checks this session, zero mismatches), every number below re-measured this session. The chapter's three build targets are green through `run_all.sh ch10`; the full-repo regression stands at 95/95.

## The Two Structures: Composition as Wiring

Both four-input adders are three `fp32_add2` instances and three OR gates; neither contains one line of new datapath logic. The tree, from `src/ch10/fp32_add4_tree.v`:

```verilog
  fp32_add2 u_add_ab (.a(a), .b(b), .result(sum_ab),
                      .invalid(inv_ab), .overflow(ovf_ab), .inexact(inx_ab));
  fp32_add2 u_add_cd (.a(c), .b(d), .result(sum_cd),
                      .invalid(inv_cd), .overflow(ovf_cd), .inexact(inx_cd));
  fp32_add2 u_add_r  (.a(sum_ab), .b(sum_cd), .result(result),
                      .invalid(inv_r), .overflow(ovf_r), .inexact(inx_r));

  assign invalid  = inv_ab | inv_cd | inv_r;
  assign overflow = ovf_ab | ovf_cd | ovf_r;
  assign inexact  = inx_ab | inx_cd | inx_r;
```

and the sequential chain, from `src/ch10/fp32_add4_seq.v`:

```verilog
  fp32_add2 u_add1 (.a(a), .b(b), .result(sum_ab),
                    .invalid(inv1), .overflow(ovf1), .inexact(inx1));
  fp32_add2 u_add2 (.a(sum_ab), .b(c), .result(sum_abc),
                    .invalid(inv2), .overflow(ovf2), .inexact(inx2));
  fp32_add2 u_add3 (.a(sum_abc), .b(d), .result(result),
                    .invalid(inv3), .overflow(ovf3), .inexact(inx3));
```

This is chapter 9's port contract cashed a second time: the four-reason case for decomposition ("Cutting Along the Seams") promised that chapter 10 would instantiate these exact modules, and here they are, unmodified. One build detail matters enough for the manifest to encode it: `fp32_add2` alone does not elaborate. The chapter's targets compile the full cross-chapter source set — chapter 7's `fp32_fields`/`fp32_class`, chapter 2's `align_sticky`, all seven chapter 9 modules — and every target compiles with **zero `iverilog -g2012 -Wall` output**, the warning-gated rule the harness has enforced since chapter 6.

**The flag OR is a design decision, made where you can see it.** Each wrapper ORs `invalid`/`overflow`/`inexact` across its three instances. That is the IEEE 754 reading of this hardware: the four-input sum is *computed as three separate additions*, each operation raises its own exceptions, and status flags accumulate as a union. The alternative — report only the final operation's flags — is not a stylistic variant but a different (and wrong) claim about what happened: the corner suite below has a vector (Q1) where the tree's overflow is raised at level 1, its invalid at level 2, and the final stage's own flags alone would report neither the overflow (sequential) nor anything but the invalid (tree). The mutation record makes this mechanical: dropping the OR fails 12 directed checks, flag-only, while every result-bits-only comparison in the equivalence sweep passes the mutant untouched. Chapter 12's design document must state the OR rule as the flag specification, not leave it implicit in a wrapper.

**What these structures are not.** Neither rounds fewer than three times. Every intermediate edge — `sum_ab`, `sum_cd`, `sum_abc` — is a finished binary32 value, so the composition inherits `fp32_add2`'s per-operation correctness and therefore *cannot* be more accurate than three IEEE additions in its declared order. Architectures that keep intermediates wide and round once are real hardware, but different hardware; they get their own section near the end, flagged as documentation. Everything measured in between is about what rounding three times does — and does not — cost.

## Depth, Registers, and the Third Option

The first structural surprise at n = 4: **the tree does not cost more adders.** Any association of n operands uses exactly n − 1 two-input adds; tree and chain are both three instances. What the shape buys is depth, and what it costs is nothing at all in adder count:

| | adders | combinational depth (adder-delays) | balancing registers when pipelined |
|---|---|---|---|
| tree `(a+b)+(c+d)` | 3 | **2** | none — all four operands enter at t₀, both level-1 results arrive together |
| seq `((a+b)+c)+d` | 3 | **3** | `c` must wait one adder-latency, `d` two |

These are counts read off the structures, not timing measurements — no synthesizer exists in this environment, and chapter 9's "What Cannot Be Known Without a Synthesizer" wall stands unmoved (its `-t sizer` inventory of one `fp32_add2`, 1,761 elaborated gates with the shifters and LZC unaccounted, triples with the instance count but remains an elaboration inventory, not area). Within that limit, the pipelining consequence is countable now and chapter 11 inherits it. Cut each `fp32_add2` into S stages at chapter 9's declared boundaries (100/95/73/72 bits of state per cut) and the tree pipelines with latency 2S, one result per cycle, and no input-side balancing — its two level-1 adders are the same depth by construction. The chain pipelines with latency 3S, *plus* delay-matching pipes for the late operands: `c` needs S cycles × 32 bits and `d` needs 2S × 32 — 3S × 32 bits of registers doing nothing but waiting. The structural asymmetry favors the tree twice over, and neither advantage has anything to do with accuracy.

**The third option chapter 11 must name:** one `fp32_add2`, an accumulator register, and 4-beat operand multiplexing — one adder, four-plus cycles per result, a quarter of the throughput. Its association order is sequential *by construction*: everything this chapter measures about `((a+b)+c)+d`, including the accumulator pathology worked in the inter-pair-cancellation section (S2, where the chain re-loses a sub-ulp addend three separate times), is that design's arithmetic too. The three designs are three points on a cost/latency curve — and when the same four operands arrive, they compute **different specifications**. That sentence is the chapter's thesis, and the next several sections put numbers on it; the cost table above should never be quoted without its arithmetic column attached.

## The Reference Follows the Wiring

Chapter 8's "Order Is Part of the Answer" ended with the testbench consequence: *the reference must replicate the DUT's association order add by add, round-tripping between every one.* This chapter teaches that discipline in full, as four rules, each carrying a measurement — and then proves both structures against **two** order-faithful references at once, because chapter 12 will want both and their agreement had to be established before either was trusted.

**Style A — the chained golden algorithm.** `src/ch10/ref_add4.v` wires three chapter 8 `fp32_add_alg` instances in the DUT's own order (`ref_add4_tree`, `ref_add4_seq`), flags ORed the same way. This reference is *bit-exact including flags and NaN bit patterns*: chapter 9 proved `fp32_add2` bit-identical to `fp32_add_alg`, and identical modules see identical stage inputs, so the composition must preserve the identity exactly.

**Style B — the chained `shortreal` round-trip.** In-testbench, each add is computed in the simulator's double arithmetic and forced through the only rounding point that exists — chapter 7's "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory" proved `shortreal` *assignment* does not round; `$shortrealtobits` does — with every intermediate re-expanded before the next add. From `src/ch10/tb_equiv4.v`:

```verilog
  function [31:0] rt_add(input [31:0] x, input [31:0] y);
    shortreal sx, sy;
    begin
      sx = $bitstoshortreal(x);
      sy = $bitstoshortreal(y);
      rt_add = $shortrealtobits(sx + sy);
    end
  endfunction
```

Each `rt_add` call is a true binary32 one-add oracle because the innocuous-double-rounding condition holds (binary64's p = 53 ≥ 2·24 + 2, chapter 8's "Why the Shortcuts Fail" safe harbor): rounding the exact two-operand sum to binary64 and then to binary32 equals rounding it once. Chaining does not stretch the argument — each call is one add, rounded once. The tree reference is `rt_add(rt_add(a,b), rt_add(c,d))`; the sequential reference is `rt_add(rt_add(rt_add(a,b),c),d)`; and nothing else.

**The four rules, with their measurements.** (1) *Round-trip every intermediate* — "What Skipping the Round-Trip Costs, by Regime" prices the violation: ~1.3 % of full-range quadruples, a quarter to a third of clustered ones. (2) *Sum in exactly the hardware's order* — the mutation record prices this one to the vector: an order-mismatched *result-bits* reference fails a correct DUT on precisely the quadruples where the two associations differ, no more, no fewer (a flags-carrying reference fails on more: flags diverge on some bits-agreeing quadruples) (1,369 of 10,000 in the probe — exactly the probe's tree≠seq census of 17+607+731+14). (3) *Compare NaN by class plus quiet bit, never by bits, and deliver sNaN operands as bits* — a generated NaN's sign is architecture-dependent (this project's two hosts have disagreed; STATE.md), and a `shortreal` round-trip quiets an sNaN with no arithmetic at all, so only style A may assert NaN bits, and the stimulus generator builds `7FA00000` directly. (4) *Use `!==` behind an explicit X-guard* — chapter 9's measured lesson ("Composition, and the Golden-Equivalence Sweep"); `tb_equiv4.v` fails on `^result === 1'bx` before comparing anything.

**The verification, run.** `tb_equiv4.v` instantiates both DUTs and both style-A references, computes both style-B references per vector, and drives four regimes of random quadruples: exponents uniform on [1, 254]; within ±10 of a per-quadruple base; within ±2; and a special-mix regime in which each operand is, with probability 1/4, drawn from a 14-entry table (±0, ±inf, qNaN, sNaN by bits, ±maxnormal, subnormal boundaries, ±1.0). `$urandom` is seeded once through an integer variable with the first draw discarded — chapter 5's rule, and chapter 4's: a literal seed is a `vvp` load-time error. The shipped target runs 6,000 quadruples per regime in 34.5 s on this container (the harness's 60 s timeout sizes it); the headline run is the same source at `-DNQ=60000`, re-run this session in 5 m 47 s:

```
tree!=seq per regime (of 60000 each): full=402 win10=14403 win2=18003 specialmix=342
PASS tb_equiv4 (240000 quadruples x 2 structures x 2 references)
```

**240,000 quadruples, two structures, two reference styles, zero mismatches** — the golden chain matched on result bits *and all three ORed flags, NaN bits included*; the round-trip chain matched on result bits, NaN by class. Two references that both equal the DUT equal each other, so there is no vector in the campaign on which the golden-algorithm chain and the round-tripped `shortreal` chain diverge. The python twin behind the sweep's expected values was validated first: the fast host adder and the single-rounding oracle used later in this chapter were checked against chapter 8's exact rational `exact_add` on 620,400 directed-plus-random cases this session, zero mismatches.

**What this proves, precisely — and what it deliberately does not.** Each structure is a correct implementation of *its own composition order*: three correctly rounded IEEE additions in the declared association, special values included (60,000 quadruples salted with zeros, infinities, and both NaN classes produced no case where the screen logic failed to compose). What it does not prove is that either structure computes the correctly rounded four-operand *sum*. It does not — "How Often the Shape Matters" and "Accuracy Against the Correctly Rounded Sum" quantify the gap. Correct-per-composition and correctly-rounded-as-one-operation are different specifications, and everything from here to the end of the chapter is about keeping them apart.

## What Skipping the Round-Trip Costs, by Regime

Chapter 5's "The Reference Model" put a number on the tempting shortcut — sum everything in host doubles, round once at the end — and its seed callout for this chapter measured it on quadruples: about 1.3 % of full-range vectors wrong (1.298 % tree order, 1.295 % sequential, its RNG). That figure has been quoted around this guide ever since, and this section's job is to correct how it gets quoted, because at four inputs it is a *best case*, not the cost.

The naive model was rebuilt for quadruples this session (`random.Random(20261005)`, 200,000 quadruples per regime, generators stated in "How Often the Shape Matters"): all four operands summed in doubles, one rounding at the end, compared against the round-tripped binary32 chain in each structure's order. Measured disagreement with the correct reference (condensed from the run's output; the raw lines carry the counts — tree 2,537 / 56,262 / 63,226 and seq 2,523 / 56,001 / 66,039 out of 200,000 each):

```
full : naive disagrees   tree 1.268 %   seq 1.262 %
win10:                   tree 28.131 %  seq 28.000 %
win2 :                   tree 31.613 %  seq 33.020 %
```

The full-range row reproduces chapter 5's figure with this chapter's own generator and RNG. The clustered rows are the sharpening chapter 12 must hear: **the un-round-tripped reference is wrong on more than a quarter of accumulator-style vectors.** A testbench built on it would report thousands of false failures against a perfect DUT — or, worse, be "fixed" by breaking the DUT until it matches. The corresponding Verilog mutation was run against the *correct* shipped design: replacing the tree's chained `rt_add` reference with a single-rounding double-sum makes `tb_equiv4` fail 1,562 of 10,000 probe quadruples, every failure a lie about working hardware.

Note the mechanism, because it inverts the usual intuition about broken references: the naive model is not noisy or sloppy — it is *more accurate* than binary32, because it never rounds an intermediate to binary32 (its three double-precision roundings are all it does). That is exactly what disqualifies it as an oracle for hardware that must round three times. And the regime dependence carries a strategic warning: a chapter 12 random campaign biased toward clustered exponents — which it must be, since that is where association matters and where an accumulator lives — makes the round-trip discipline roughly 25× *more* load-bearing than the full-range figure suggests. The better your generator gets at finding interesting vectors, the more wrong the lazy reference becomes.

## How Often the Shape Matters

Chapter 8 measured association-sensitivity for *triples* and handed this chapter the design consequence. The quadruple version was measured this session — 400,000 quadruples per regime in python (`random.Random(20261020)`, seeded once), with the generators stated exactly, because the rate is a property of the stimulus and must never travel without it. All operands: sign one uniform bit, fraction 23 uniform bits. `G_full`: exponent uniform on [1, 254] per operand. `G_win10`: per-quadruple base e₀ uniform on [11, 244], each exponent uniform within ±10 of it. `G_win2`: e₀ on [3, 252], exponents within ±2. Comparing final bit patterns of `(a+b)+(c+d)` against `((a+b)+c)+d`:

| regime | quadruples: tree ≠ seq | (ch08 triples, for shape) |
|---|---|---|
| full | 2,617 / 400,000 = **0.65 %** | 0.64 % |
| win10 | 95,972 / 400,000 = **23.99 %** | 21.79 % |
| win2 | 120,457 / 400,000 = **30.11 %** | 32.41 % |

The quadruple rates land within about two points of chapter 8's triple table — the shape carries over intact. Spread the exponents and grouping almost never matters; cluster them, which is the accumulator case, and **roughly one random quadruple in four to three changes its bit pattern when you change the association.** The Verilog side of the campaign confirms this with an entirely independent RNG: `tb_equiv4`'s own in-testbench census printed `402 / 14403 / 18003` out of 60,000 per regime (0.67 % / 24.0 % / 30.0 %) in the headline run — `$urandom` versus python's Mersenne generator, different draw counts, same rates. Measured only up to n = 4; the numbers claim nothing beyond that.

Two readings the rest of the chapter depends on keeping apart:

1. **This is not an error rate.** Neither structure is wrong on any of these 120,457 quadruples; each returns exactly the value its own three-add composition specifies, and the 240,000-quadruple sweep proved it. The number measures how *underdetermined* "the sum of four numbers" is until a design document fixes the order.
2. **The rate belongs to the generator.** Quote it with the exponent regime attached — never as "FP addition disagrees with itself 30 % of the time." Chapter 8 issued the same warning for its census; at four inputs the spread between regimes is a factor of forty-six, so an unqualified quotation is not simplified, it is wrong.

## Accuracy Against the Correctly Rounded Sum

Now the question the section titles have been circling: given that the two structures disagree with each other, which one is *right*? The yardstick is the "as if infinitely precise, rounded once" answer `CR(a,b,c,d)`: each finite operand taken to its exact scaled-integer form M·2^q, the four integers aligned and summed exactly, the total rounded **once** by chapter 8's validated from-scratch RNE encoder (exact zero → +0 under round-to-nearest-even). The oracle was itself cross-checked against `exact_add` on 20,000 zero-padded pairs before use — part of the 620,400-check validation. Error is measured in **representable-step distance**: map each finite bit pattern to its signed lattice index (negative patterns → −magnitude), subtract; one step is one representable binary32 value. The same 1.2 million quadruples as the census; quadruples whose structure result or CR overflowed are excluded from distance statistics and counted (79 / 50 / 453 per regime).

| regime | structure | ≠ CR | steps: 0 / 1 / 2 / ≥3 | mean | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| full | tree | 1.63 % | 393390 / 6360 / 80 / 91 | 0.0231 | 1288 |
| full | seq | 1.63 % | 393383 / 6370 / 82 / 86 | 0.0231 | 1288 |
| win10 | tree | 28.05 % | 287746 / 103555 / 3665 / 4984 | 0.5272 | 5162 |
| win10 | seq | 27.99 % | 287997 / 102428 / 4481 / 5044 | 0.5312 | 4096 |
| win2 | tree | 31.74 % | 272749 / 97020 / 14945 / 14833 | 1.9664 | 196608 |
| win2 | seq | 33.09 % | 267331 / 100684 / 16859 / 14673 | 1.6214 | 196608 |

Strictly-closer counts (both finite, unequal distances): full — tree 1,305 vs seq 1,299; win10 — tree 46,965 vs **seq 47,750**; win2 — **tree 59,799** vs seq 56,480. Read those three comparisons together: the winner *flips between regimes*, and within win2 the mean favors seq while the strictly-closer count and the ≠ CR rate favor tree.

**The honest headline: neither structure is systematically more accurate at n = 4.** The tree-vs-seq differences are small fractions of the between-regime differences, their direction is not stable, and the means are dominated by rare massive-cancellation outliers — the win2 maximum of 196,608 steps is hit by *both* structures on the *same* quadruple (`FB284946 FB17B457 7AC276BF 7B5EC2A8`, two large negatives against two large positives; both structures return `72D80000` where CR is `72D50000` — every individual add correctly rounded, the cancelled leading bits making the absolute step distance enormous). This is where the folklore dies. The folklore is not baseless: pairwise summation's worst-case error bound grows as O(ε log n) against sequential's O(ε n) — Higham's analysis, and the asymptotic reason big dot-product units are trees. But at n = 4 that is a critical-chain length of 2 roundings versus 3, a bound ratio of 1.5, invisible under random stimulus. **The tree's accuracy advantage is an asymptotic story. Do not sell it as a measurable n = 4 effect, because this sweep looked for it and it is not there.** Choose the tree for depth and pipelining — the grounds the cost table gave — and say so in the design document, because a reviewer who believes the accuracy folklore will otherwise "simplify" your verification plan around a property the hardware does not have.

What *is* measurable, stated with its generator: (a) with full-range exponents, both structures return the exactly rounded four-operand sum on 98.37 % of quadruples and land within one step of it on 99.96 %; (b) clustering the exponents moves 28–33 % of results off the correctly rounded value, but 96–99 % stay within two steps (98.7 % win10, 96.3 % win2); (c) the tail is where composition genuinely hurts — inter-pair cancellation costs thousands of steps in vectors worked in "Inter-Pair Cancellation, Both Directions", and no choice of association order fixes it; only deferred rounding does.

## "Both Orders Agree" Is Not an Oracle

Having built two structures, you will be tempted by a seductive verification bargain: run both, and where they agree, trust the common value — two independent association orders arriving at the same bits surely certify the sum. It costs nothing, it needs no reference model, and chapter 5's "The Green Run That Means Nothing" should already have you reaching for a counterexample. Here are two, one statistical and one mechanical, both measured this session.

**Statistical: agreement does not mean correctness.** 50,000 quadruples per regime (`random.Random(20261010)`): among quadruples where tree and sequential agree bit-for-bit and the value is finite, the common value still differs from the correctly rounded sum on **1.32 %** (full), **20.46 %** (win10), and **21.51 %** (win2). A fifth of the agreeing results in the clustered regimes are the same wrong answer — both orders made the *same* rounding sacrifice, most simply when the damage happens in the `a+b` add that both associations share. Agreement between two computations that are wrong in the same way certifies the wrongness.

**Mechanical: a cross-check bench cannot see a shared bug.** The strategy was written out as a real testbench — scratch-only, deliberately not shipped — that drives the three-regime campaign, X-guards, count-guards, checks that full-range agreement stays above 95 % (measured ~99.3 %, so gross breakage is caught), and treats every `tree === seq` quadruple as "cross-checked." Against the correct design:

```
PASS tb_cross4 (7500 quadruples, 6145 cross-checked by tree==seq agreement)
```

Then chapter 9's rounding mutant — the RNE `lbit` term dropped, ties-to-even degraded to ties-down — was planted in the **shared** `fp32_round_pack`, which every one of the six `fp32_add2` instances in both structures instantiates. The same bench, against six broken rounders:

```
PASS tb_cross4 (7500 quadruples, 6159 cross-checked by tree==seq agreement)
```

A false pass — and look closely at the number: the broken design *agreed with itself on 14 more quadruples than the correct one*. Cross-agreement is not merely blind to shared bugs; a shared bug can make the two structures agree *more*, because both now round wrong identically. Meanwhile `tb_equiv4` kills the same mutant 3,152 times in the 10,000-quadruple probe — 785 tree-side and 791 seq-side against the chained golden algorithm, and the *same counts again* against the chained `shortreal` round-trip, two reference styles that share nothing with the DUT's rounder converging on the same verdict.

The general statement is worth a box around it: **two implementations cross-checked against each other verify only that they implement the same thing — never that the thing is right.** The tree and the chain share `fp32_add2`, which is nearly all of the design, so their agreement carries almost no evidence. Only a reference that is independent of the shared code — the golden chain, the round-tripped `shortreal` chain, or the exact single-rounding model — measures correctness, and only the exact model measures *accuracy*. Chapter 12 must not let "the two orders agreed" into its verification plan as anything more than a smoke test.

## One Multiset, Three Answers

Chapter 5 seeded a corner this guide has been carrying for five chapters: intermediate overflow that later cancels. Chapter 8's "Order Is Part of the Answer" showed the triple version (T2: infinity is absorbing). The four-input version is sharper than either, and it ends in a specification requirement no earlier chapter could state. All vectors below are from `tb_corners4.v`, every expected value and flag pinned by the exact per-stage python model first; the RTL matched on all of them.

**Q1: `a = b = maxnormal, c = d = −maxnormal` (`7F7FFFFF ×2, FF7FFFFF ×2`). True sum: 0.** Captured:

```
Q1: tree=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=111 | seq=7f800000 inv/ovf/inx=011
```

The tree's ledger: level 1 computes max+max, whose exact sum 2¹²⁹·(1−2⁻²⁴) is beyond the format and rounds under RNE to **+inf** with overflow and inexact; the other pair gives **−inf** symmetrically; level 2 then faces `(+inf) + (−inf)` — the one case where infinity arithmetic has no answer — and produces **qNaN, invalid**. Final: NaN with all three flags up, each earned at a different node. The chain's ledger: max+max → +inf as before; then `+inf + (−max)` is +inf *exactly* — the screen absorbs, no new flag — and again. Final: **+inf, overflow+inexact, no invalid**: the second infinity never meets a −inf, so invalid never fires.

**Q2: `max, max, −max, +0`. True sum: maxnormal — finite.** Both structures return `7F800000` with overflow+inexact; the tree's level-2 `(+inf) + (−max)` and the chain's stages both absorb. The correctly rounded answer `7F7FFFFF` is representable, and both compositions lose it anyway — not to rounding, to *range*. This is the sharpest available demonstration that **binary32's range, not just its precision, is part of what composition sacrifices**: once an intermediate saturates, no later operand can repair it.

**Q3: the same multiset as Q1, interleaved — `max, −max, max, −max`.** Both structures: `+0`, no flags. Every stage cancels exactly, matching the single-rounding answer.

Line the three up. From **one multiset of four operands**, the delivered result across structures and port orders is **qNaN, +inf, or +0** — different in *class*, not merely in value — and only the last equals the single-rounding sum. And here is the part that must survive into chapter 12's design document: Q1 and Q3 differ **only in the order the same four values arrive at the ports**. `fp32_add4_tree` applied to a permutation of its own inputs changes result class. Operand order at the ports is part of the arithmetic specification, exactly as much as the association shape is.

**Resist the instinct to call any of this a bug.** Computed as three separate binary32 additions in the declared order, +inf (chain) and qNaN (tree) are *the correctly rounded results of those operation sequences* — every single add above is exactly what IEEE 754 specifies for its operands, and the 240,000-quadruple sweep holds both structures to it. The finite true sum is the answer to a *different question* — the single-rounding compound sum — which no composition of correctly rounded two-input adds is obliged to produce, and which, once an intermediate saturates, *cannot* be produced. There is no bug to fix here; there is a specification to choose. Chapter 12 must therefore pin three things, in writing: the association structure, the port-to-operand mapping, and which specification the flags describe — and its verification plan must treat permutations of the same values as distinct vectors, as `tb_corners4.v` already does. How often does random stimulus brush against this? The full-range sweep produced 79 nonfinite-implicated quadruples in 400,000 and win2 produced 453 — rare enough that only directed vectors pin the three-way split, common enough that a long campaign will trip over it unprepared.

## NaN Birth Sites and Signed Zero, Composed

**NaN poisons from every position, in both structures.** Five directed vectors, RTL output captured this session:

```
N1: tree=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100 | seq=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100
N2: tree=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100 | seq=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100
N3: tree=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100 | seq=7fc00000 inv/ovf/inx=100
N4: tree=7fc00001 inv/ovf/inx=000 | seq=7fc00001 inv/ovf/inx=000
N5: tree=7fe00000 inv/ovf/inx=100 | seq=7fe00000 inv/ovf/inx=100
```

- **N1** `(+inf, −inf, 1, 1)`: the NaN is born in stage 1 and survives two subsequent adds in the chain, one in the tree; `invalid` is raised at birth and carried to the output by the OR.
- **N2** `(1, 1, +inf, −inf)`: the tree births the NaN in its second pair — but the chain *never computes* `inf + (−inf)` here. Its path is `2 + inf = inf`, then `inf + (−inf)` = NaN at stage **three**. Same final class and flags, different birth site: the two structures agree at the ports while executing different exception histories.
- **N3** `(+inf, 1, 2, −inf)`: constructed so the NaN is born only at the **last** level in *both* structures — the tree's level 2 receives +inf and −inf from its two pairs; the chain's stage 3 does the same. This is the vector proving the final adder's invalid path reachable from *composed* inputs, a distinct coverage obligation from N1/N2 (the regression section makes it a bin).
- **N4** — a payloaded quiet NaN operand, `7FC00001`: propagates **with its payload intact through both compositions**, and `invalid` stays **0**. A quiet NaN in is not an invalid operation; every later stage merely forwards it. A testbench that asserts `invalid` on every NaN result dies on this vector, which is precisely why it is in the suite.
- **N5** — a signaling NaN operand, `7FA00000`, delivered as bits: result `7FE00000` — payload kept, quiet bit set — with `invalid` raised. Chapter 9's quieting behavior survives two levels of composition; the quieted NaN then rides the same propagation as N4.

The general fact, stated plainly: **NaN and infinity are absorbing through every later stage, so a stage-1 special event is never diluted by three more operands.** But notice what the *result* cannot tell you: N1 and N3 deliver identical words with identical ORed flags. Only the per-instance flags distinguish a NaN born early from a NaN born at the output — a fact the coverage bins exploit and the flags section returns to.

**Signed zero composes cleanly — all sixteen combinations, measured.** The two-input rule (like signs keep their sign; `(+0) + (−0)` is +0 under RNE) generalizes with no new cases: **the four-input result is −0 iff every operand is −0, else +0**, in both structures, all flags low — all 16 sign combinations checked against the exact model, both structures, every run. The induction is two sentences: any +0 anywhere forces the running or pair result to +0, and +0 meeting −0 stays +0. One more vector earns its place: Z1 `(1, −1, −0, −0)`, where the +0 *generated by cancellation* — a different producer path than a zero operand, chapter 5's distinction — meets the −0 chain and correctly yields +0 in both structures. The signed-zero specification survives composition unchanged; one table retires the worry, and chapter 12 can carry the sixteen-row check as-is.

## Inter-Pair Cancellation, Both Directions

Cancellation between *pairs* — where the operand that should cancel a value sits in a different subtree or a later stage than the value itself — is where the two structures genuinely part company, and it is the one corner family where each structure beats the other on a directed vector. Both directions were demonstrated on the RTL and both were then found in the wild by the random sweep.

**Directed, the chain wins — S1: `(2²⁵, 1, −2²⁵, 1)`. True sum: 2.**

```
S1: tree=00000000 inv/ovf/inx=001 | seq=3f800000 inv/ovf/inx=001
```

The tree pairs each small operand with a big one and loses both: `2²⁵ + 1` rounds down to `2²⁵` (the ulp at 2²⁵ is 4; the added 1 is a quarter-ulp, below the rounding threshold), and `−2²⁵ + 1` — exact value −(2²⁵ − 1), which needs 25 bits — is the tie, and ties-to-even rounds it *away* to `−2²⁵`. Level 2 computes `2²⁵ − 2²⁵ = +0`: both units of true sum gone, result +0 where the truth is 2. The chain recovers half: `2²⁵ + 1 → 2²⁵`, then `+(−2²⁵)` cancels *exactly* to +0, then `+1` survives — result 1.0. The correctly rounded answer is `40000000` (2.0); neither structure reaches it; the chain is strictly closer. Note the two error metrics diverging: the absolute error is tiny — one or two — while the *step* distance from +0 to 2.0 spans the entire subnormal range and more. Quote both, labelled, or quote neither.

**Directed, the tree wins — S2: `(2²⁴, 1, 1, 1)`. True sum: 2²⁴ + 3.**

```
S2: tree=4b800001 inv/ovf/inx=001 | seq=4b800000 inv/ovf/inx=001
```

The chain is chapter 8's T1 fired twice more: each `2²⁴ + 1` is the exact tie, RNE rounds down to even, and the increment is lost **three separate times** — result `2²⁴`, all three units gone. The tree first gathers the small operands — `1 + 1 = 2`, exact — and `2²⁴ + 2` is representable: `4B800001`. CR is `4B800002` (the exact sum ties between `4B800001` and `4B800002`; even mantissa wins). Tree one step off, chain two. **This is the accumulator lesson in one vector**: a sequential chain re-loses a sub-ulp addend at every stage, while a tree lets small operands reinforce each other before meeting the big one — the visible n = 4 germ of the O(ε log n) versus O(ε n) bound, and precisely the pathology the accumulator option from the cost section computes by construction.

**Found in the wild** — win2 sweep, seed 20261020, first 200,000 quadruples, one failure each way, both exactly 4,096 steps:

```
tree exact, seq off 4096: DC44714B 5C3955A1 DBF86B93 5C0749DD
                          tree=D5F2D000=CR    seq=D5F2E000
seq exact, tree off 4096: 7C8D49C8 7D4A751E FD655607 FC2F3DA8
                          tree=F736E000       seq=F736F000=CR
```

Both are two-large-positives-versus-two-large-negatives quadruples whose sum cancels about 12.7 binary orders of magnitude (measured for the first: max operand over surviving sum); the structure whose intermediate happens to align with the surviving bits lands exactly on CR, and the other is 4,096 representable values away. The symmetry is the finding: **the sweep produced mirror-image failures inside the same generator, so neither structure can be presented as the safe one against cancellation.** No association order fixes inter-pair cancellation; only architectures that defer rounding do — see "Rounding Once Instead of Three Times".

## Flags Under Composition

The OR rule from the structures section says *what* the composed flags are; this section pins down what they *mean*, because one of them quietly changes meaning under composition and chapter 12's flag specification has to choose its words.

**`invalid` and `overflow` compose transparently.** Each is raised by the stage that earns it and carried by the OR: Q1's tree raises overflow at level 1 and invalid at level 2 and reports both; N4 proves the OR invents nothing (a quiet-NaN operand propagates with all three flags low — propagation is not invalid). What the ORed output *loses* is the birth site: N1 and N3 are indistinguishable at the ports, and Q1's flags do not say which node overflowed. If a consumer needs provenance — and chapter 12's coverage bins do — it must come from per-instance flags, which is exactly how the corner suite's bins are sampled. And one standing simplification survives composition untouched: a binary32 adder can never raise underflow (chapter 7's measured proof — a tiny sum of two binary32 operands is always exact), so three of them ORed cannot either; the four-input flag specification stays a three-flag specification.

**`inexact` is the subtle one: it diverges from the single-rounding model even when the result bits agree.** Measured this session — 100,000 quadruples per regime (`random.Random(20261011)`), tree order, per-stage exact model: among quadruples whose composed result is **bit-equal to CR**, the composition's ORed `inexact` differs from the single-rounding model's on **0 (full), 0.362 % (win10), 7.095 % (win2)** — and every single disagreement, 5,094 across 300,000 quadruples, is composed-inexact-set against CR-exact. The reverse direction occurred **zero times**, and zero is not luck; it is a two-line theorem. If all three stages are exact, the composed result *is* the exact four-operand sum; the sum is therefore representable, so CR's inexact is 0 too. Composition can round twice and land back on the exact value — it can never be exact while the true sum is not. A concrete swept example: `464F7804 47B381F1 466DF600 C6A45DF6` — stage inexact pattern 1,0,1, yet the final `47C21834` equals CR and the true sum is exact. The intermediate roundings cancelled in *value*; the flag records that they happened.

So the composed `inexact` is the honest flag *for the composition* and an over-report *relative to the compound-sum reading* — one more place the two specifications part company, this time in a status bit rather than a result bit. Two instructions follow for chapter 12, both non-negotiable. First, the design document must define `inexact` **as the OR of the three component operations' flags** — the IEEE reading of what this hardware is — and not as "the result differs from the infinitely precise sum," which is a different (one-directionally stricter) predicate the hardware does not compute. Second, the verification consequence: **predict flags from the chained reference, never from the single-rounding model.** A testbench that checks the composed `inexact` against CR's inexact will fail a correct DUT on 7 % of clustered bit-agreeing vectors — the false-failure twin of the naive-reference trap, one bit wide.

## Rounding Once Instead of Three Times

Everything in this section is documentation from the literature, flagged as such — nothing here was built or measured, and it exists because the last six sections measured what per-stage rounding costs and the natural question is "why round three times at all?"

**The architecture family.** Multi-operand floating-point adders that keep the intermediate sum wider than the format and round **once** at the end are established practice: three-input and general multi-term *fused* adders align all significands to a common exponent, sum them in a wide carry-save datapath, and normalize and round once (Ercegovac & Lang's multi-operand addition chapters; Sohn & Swartzlander's fused three-term adder; Tenca's multi-operand addition survey — all by title in the sources). The limiting case is Kulisch's exact long accumulator: a fixed-point register wide enough — on the order of 640 bits for binary32 — that *any* number of operands accumulates exactly, rounded once at readout. What such a unit would change in this chapter's measurements is precise: the 0.65–30 % order-dependence drops to zero by construction; Q1 and Q2's intermediate overflow disappears, because ±2¹²⁸-scale partials fit the wide accumulator; and S1/S2's lost units are all recovered. The price is a wider datapath and a normalize/round stage that sees the full accumulated width — the cost side is the literature's subject, not this project's measurement. This is also the honest completion of chapter 8's "How Production Adders Rearrange It": production hardware rearranges *within* one rounding's evidence; fused units rearrange *how many* roundings there are.

**What the standard actually says.** IEEE 754-2019 added **augmented arithmetic operations** — `augmentedAddition`, `augmentedSubtraction`, `augmentedMultiplication` — which produce a pair: the correctly rounded result *and* the error term, exactly representable in the format. They are recommended, not required, and at publication no hardware implementations were known, though the same operations were long-established in software as the 2Sum family of error-free transformations (fetch-verified via the Wikipedia IEEE 754 article; the normative text is the standard's). That is the standardized face of the idea this chapter keeps brushing against: `augmentedAddition(a, b)` returns the rounded sum *and* the exact residue, so a four-input sum can carry its own rounding error forward and re-inject it — the software route to a correctly rounded multi-operand sum on top of exactly the two-input hardware this guide builds.

**The seam for chapter 14.** The measured gap of this chapter — correct compositions that miss the correctly rounded sum on up to a third of clustered inputs — is precisely the gap this literature exists to close. Chapter 14 can pick up the thread with the fused adders, the Kulisch accumulator, and 2Sum-based compensation, using S1 and S2 as its worked examples; this chapter's job was only to measure the gap honestly and to refuse to pretend an association order closes it.

**The specification chapter 12 inherits, pinned.** Build the **tree**. The grounds, in order and with their evidence: shallower composition (2 versus 3 adder-delays, counted); no balancing registers under chapter 11's pipelining (3S × 32 bits saved, counted); a special event is at most one stage from the output; and a more symmetric verification structure (two interchangeable level-1 bins). Accuracy is explicitly *not* a ground — the 1.2-million-quadruple sweep found no systematic winner. Alongside the shape, pin the port-to-operand mapping (Q1 versus Q3: a permutation changes the result class), the flag rule (the OR of three operations, with `inexact` defined against the composition, not the compound sum), and the reference discipline (order-faithful, round-tripped, NaN by class, `!==` behind an X-guard). That one paragraph is the contract; everything else in chapter 12 is executing it.

## Traps Beginners Fall Into

Every entry below was demonstrated this session against this chapter's own structures; nothing is folklore.

1. **Believing trees are more accurate at small n.** A 1.2-million-quadruple sweep against the exact single-rounding oracle found no systematic winner between `(a+b)+(c+d)` and `((a+b)+c)+d` — the strictly-closer counts flip between regimes, and the worst outlier (196,608 steps) hits both structures on the same quadruple. The O(ε log n) advantage is real and asymptotic; at n = 4 it is a bound ratio of 1.5 and invisible. Choose the tree for depth and pipelining, and write those grounds down.

2. **Cross-checking the two orders and calling it verification.** Among clustered quadruples where tree and sequential agree bit-for-bit, 20–21 % still miss the correctly rounded sum — and a cross-check testbench PASSed a design with the RNE tie-break broken in all six shared rounders, cross-checking *more* vectors than the correct design did. Agreement measures sameness, not rightness.

3. **Writing the reference in the wrong association order.** An order-mismatched `shortreal` reference failed a correct DUT on exactly 1,369 of 10,000 probe quadruples — precisely the vectors where the two orders differ, a rate the census table predicts regime by regime. The reference replicates the DUT's wiring, add by add, or it is a random-failure generator.

4. **Skipping the round-trip because "1.3 % barely matters."** That figure is the full-range best case. Under clustered exponents the un-round-tripped reference is wrong on 28–33 % of quadruples — and it fails *correct* hardware, because a no-intermediate-rounding model is more accurate than binary32, which is exactly what disqualifies it.

5. **Treating operand order at the ports as cosmetic.** One multiset, `{max, max, −max, −max}`, returns qNaN, +inf, or +0 depending on structure *and* port order — each result the correct IEEE outcome of its operation sequence. The specification must pin the port-to-operand mapping, and the corner suite must run permutations as distinct vectors.

6. **Reporting only the final stage's flags.** Dropping the flag OR leaves a stage-1 `inf + (−inf)` delivering its qNaN with `invalid = 0` — propagation is not invalid — and loses Q1's overflow entirely. Twelve directed checks die on this mutant; every result-bits-only comparison passes it. Flags accumulate as a union or they lie.

7. **Predicting the composed `inexact` from the single-rounding model.** On 7.1 % of clustered bit-agreeing quadruples the composition's inexact is set while the true sum is exact — one-directionally, provably. Define inexact as the composition's OR and predict it from the chained reference, or fail a correct DUT one status bit at a time.

8. **Expecting NaN bits to survive a value-domain reference.** Only the chained-golden reference may assert NaN payloads (it caught a final-stage operand swap that *only* dual-NaN payload ordering distinguishes — eight kills in 10,000, all in the special-mix regime, zero visible to the class-only `shortreal` reference). And sNaN operands must be built from bits: a `shortreal` round-trip quiets them with no arithmetic.

9. **Trusting random stimulus to exercise the last adder.** Its operands are derived partial sums nobody chose. The rounding-carry renormalize fires 0 times in 10⁶ random *pairs*; composed, the blindness applies at all three instances. Backwards-engineered reachers plus per-instance bins with empty-bin gates — both shown firing — are the only guarantee, and a re-aimed bin that still names an existing wire reads zero forever unless a fire-count guard backs it.

10. **Assuming module reuse flushes out latent unit-level bugs.** Chapter 9's `exact_zero` mutant — killed by one unit vector, invisible end to end — was planted in the shared adder and survived this chapter's entire kit: sweeps, corners, reachers. The structural mask travels with the module. The unit suites are not scaffolding to discard after integration; they are the only witness some bugs will ever have.

## Bridge to Chapter 11

The composition question is settled and the settlement is written down: chapter 12 builds the tree, with the port order pinned, the flags ORed, and a reference that follows the wiring. What stands between this chapter's tree and that chapter's deliverable is *time* — every adder in this guide so far has been a combinational cloud that settles when it settles.

**Chapter 11** makes the clock real, and this chapter has already surveyed its terrain. The tree's two levels pipeline into 2S stages with no balancing registers; the chain would cost 3S plus 3S × 32 bits of delay matching — the counted reason the tree won. The cut points are chapter 9's module boundaries at 100/95/73/72 bits, now crossed by *three* instances each, and the screen side channel rides every cut. The third design point — one adder, an accumulator register, 4-beat multiplexing — trades throughput for area and computes the sequential association by construction, so every number this chapter measured about `((a+b)+c)+d`, S2's triple tie-loss included, is the price tag on that trade. And one measured cost travels directly into regression planning: the shipped equivalence sweep runs about 700 quadruples per second with two DUTs, two golden chains, and six `shortreal` conversions per vector — chapter 5's ~288,000 transactions-per-second bare-reference figure does not survive this much instantiated RTL, and chapter 11's pipelined testbenches will pay the same rent plus a clock.

What chapter 11 must add that nothing here anticipates: registers between the levels mean the two DUT structures stop being combinational equals of their references — the reference stays combinational while the DUT answers S or 2S cycles late, the corner suite's `#1`-and-check idiom stops working, and the coverage bins need re-timing to sample on the right edge, exactly as chapter 9 predicted when it priced the cuts. The tree you verified in zero time is about to acquire a latency, a throughput, and a timing budget — and the arithmetic, for the first time since chapter 6, will not change at all.
