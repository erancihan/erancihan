## The Algorithm: Ten Steps, Stated Once

Chapter 7 closed with a promise: every mechanical consequence of the format — the unsigned-comparable magnitude bits, the cancelling biases, the `max(E, 1)` decode, the carry that re-encodes itself across the subnormal boundary — assembles into one algorithm. This chapter is that algorithm, taught until you can execute it **by hand on any pair of binary32 values** and justify every step. No pipeline, no clock; chapter 9 turns each step into a module. What this chapter fixes forever is the *order* the steps run in, the *width* every intermediate needs so nothing is lost before the rounder decides, and the small set of genuinely hard cases — each a row of chapter 5's "Verifying Floating Point Specifically" taxonomy wearing datapath clothes.

The working method is unchanged from the last seven chapters, leaned on harder than ever. Ground truth is `python3` exact rational arithmetic: every sum below was computed exactly with `fractions.Fraction` and rounded once by a from-scratch round-to-nearest-even encoder, itself first validated against `struct`'s double-to-binary32 conversion on 100,000 random doubles — zero mismatches — plus chapter 7's boundary probes. Alongside it ran a datapath-faithful model keeping *only* the bits the hardware will keep; it matched the exact reference on **1,000,058 operand pairs this session — 29 directed pairs in both orders plus one million mixed random pairs — with zero mismatches**. The procedure below is measured, not asserted. Every simulator claim is Icarus Verilog 13.0 (`iverilog -g2012 -Wall`, zero-output compiles), every listing is a file in `src/ch08/`, and the four build targets are green through `run_all.sh ch08`, full-repo regression 86/86.

Here is the whole algorithm, once. The chapter walks it step by step; "Twelve Additions, Traced End to End" executes it twelve times with every intermediate printed by the machine.

1. **Unpack.** Split each operand into `{s, E[7:0], F[22:0]}`; form the effective exponent `eff_e = max(E, 1)` and the 24-bit significand `sig = {(E > 0), F}`.
2. **Screen the specials.** NaN, infinity and zero operands are pure control, handled before any arithmetic. NaN beats infinity; `inf + (−inf)` invents a quiet NaN and raises invalid; unlike-signed zeros give +0.
3. **Compare and swap.** One unsigned compare of the magnitude bits `w[30:0]`; swap so the bigger operand leads.
4. **Align.** Shift the smaller significand right by the exponent difference `d`, saturated at 26, capturing the first bit out as **G** (guard), the second as **R** (round), and the OR of everything below as **S** (sticky).
5. **Effective operation.** Signs agree: add the 26-bit quantities into a 27-bit sum. Signs differ: subtract — and subtract the sticky bit too, as a borrow.
6. **Hold 28 bits of state.** 24 significand bits + G + R + carry is a 27-bit adder result, with the sticky flag alongside: 28 bits, the number chapter 9 builds registers around.
7. **Normalize.** Three mutually exclusive outcomes: a carry out shifts right by 1; a leading 1 already in place needs nothing; leading zeros after a subtraction shift left by `min(LZC, eff_e − 1)` — the clamp that *stops* at effective exponent 1.
8. **Round to nearest, ties to even.** One gate expression: `round_up = G & (R | S | L)`, with L the kept LSB. If the increment carries out of bit 23, renormalize a *second* time — the rounding-carry renormalize.
9. **Pack.** Exponent past 254 is overflow: ±infinity under this attribute. A significand with bit 23 clear can only be at effective exponent 1: encode E = 0. Otherwise assemble `{s, e, F}`.
10. **Flags.** invalid (sNaN, or `inf + (−inf)`); overflow (step 9, both paths); inexact (`G | R | S` after normalization, or overflow). Underflow: **no wire at all** — chapter 7's "The Flags an Adder Can Actually Raise" proved a tiny addition result is always exact.

Chapter 7's bridge promised the first number this chapter adds: `3DCCCCCD`, the stored 0.1. Kept, python-verified: 0.1 + 0.1 doubles exactly (same significand, exponent up one) to `3E4CCCCD`; but 0.1 + 0.2 (`3DCCCCCD + 3E4CCCCD`) runs the machinery — d = 1 alignment, carry out, right-1 renormalize, G = R = 1 — and rounds up to `3E99999A` = 0.30000001192…, the most famous inexact flag in programming, produced by steps you can now name.

Two things to notice before diving in. First, there is exactly one rounding, at step 8; every earlier step exists to deliver *enough evidence* to that single decision, and the chapter is the story of what "enough" means. Second, nothing in the list is new hardware: the aligner is chapter 2's `align_sticky` from "Reduction Operators and the Sticky Bit" at `W = 24`, the significand add is chapter 6's odometer, the rounding equation and the unpack are chapter 7's. What is new is the choreography — and the proof that this choreography, at these widths, is exactly right.

## Unpack and Screen: Control Before Arithmetic

**Step 1 is chapter 7's three-line decode, verbatim.** For each operand: `eff_e = max(E, 1)`, `sig = {(E > 0), F}`. That is the entire subnormal story in the datapath, and the alternative is chapter 5's row B — the commonest subnormal bug. Stored exponents 0 and 1 share the scale 2⁻¹²⁶ (chapter 7's "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp"): E = 0 is *E = 1 with a zero hidden bit*, and *no other subnormal case exists* anywhere in alignment or addition. Decode a subnormal as `E − 127 = −127` instead and the alignment distance comes out one too large. Measured on the first subnormal pair you might try, 2⁻¹²⁷ + 2⁻¹²⁶ (`00400000 + 00800000`): the buggy decode aligns the subnormal one bit too far right and produces `00A00000` where the truth is `00C00000` — wrong by 2⁻¹²⁸ on operands with three significant bits between them. The tracer executes this pair as W8 below; mutation M10 shows the shipped testbenches killing exactly this bug.

**Step 2 dispatches everything that is not ordinary arithmetic, as pure control.** The screen table, every row running as a `tb_corners.v` check this session:

| operands | result | flags |
|---|---|---|
| either NaN | a quiet NaN (sNaN operand additionally raises invalid) — and **both operand orders**, chapter 5's row C | invalid iff any sNaN |
| inf + inf, same sign | that infinity | — |
| **inf + (−inf)** | qNaN — the one addition that invents a NaN | invalid |
| inf + finite | that infinity | — |
| both zero, like signs | that signed zero | — |
| both zero, unlike signs | **+0** (round-to-nearest rule; −0 only under roundTowardNegative — "Rounding: Five Attributes, One Default") | — |
| one zero | the other operand, unchanged — including a subnormal, including its sign | — |

Everything else falls through to the datapath. Three details carry weight. First, NaN must beat infinity in the priority order, because `inf + (−inf)` shares the E = 255 encoding with NaN operands and must *lose* to an actual NaN input; mutation M11 inverted this priority and only the corner library's reversed-order NaN-plus-infinity row caught it. Second, quieting is separate logic from propagation: the shipped module forwards the first NaN operand's payload with the quiet bit forced high — the standard's *should* (754-2019 §6.2.3) — but its testbenches never compare a NaN result's sign, per chapter 7's rule. Third, one rule belongs to the datapath, not the screen: an *exact cancellation* — `x + (−x)`, x finite and nonzero — must return **+0**, because a magnitude subtraction that comes out zero has no sign of its own (row A). That rule lives at step 5, where the zero is discovered; `1.5 + (−1.5)` returns `00000000` in both operand orders, measured in `tb_corners.v`, and mutation M13 — keep the bigger operand's sign — died on the reversed order.

The screen is where chapter 7's shipped modules plug in: `fp32_class` is this table's condition set, `fp32_fields` is step 1. This chapter's algorithm module inlines both for readability — the equations are identical — and chapter 9 instantiates the real files.

## Compare, Swap, and the Alignment Shift

**Step 3 buys three simplifications with one comparator.** Compare the magnitude bits `w[30:0]` as plain unsigned integers — legal across zero, subnormal, normal and infinity with no carve-outs, because the encoding is monotone in the magnitude bits ("Bias 127, and What the Missing One Buys" verified all 255 exponent-boundary crossings) — and swap so the bigger operand leads. All three payoffs are load-bearing: (a) the alignment shift is always a **right** shift of the smaller operand, by `d = eff_e_big − eff_e_small ≥ 0`, so one shifter direction suffices; (b) the significand difference at step 5 is never negative — no end-around carry, no recomplement; (c) the result's sign is simply the bigger operand's. Equal magnitudes make the swap a no-op, and the subtract case falls into the exact-zero rule. The shipped module's steps 3 and 4, verbatim from `src/ch08/fp32_add_alg.v`:

```verilog
  // ---- step 3: compare and swap. One unsigned compare of the magnitude
  // bits w[30:0] is valid across zero/subnormal/normal/infinity (chapter 7's
  // encoded-order property). The bigger operand leads: the alignment shift
  // is always rightward, the difference never negative, the result's sign
  // simply the bigger operand's.
  wire        swap     = (b[30:0] > a[30:0]);
  wire        sign_big = swap ? sb   : sa;
  wire [7:0]  e_big    = swap ? eb   : ea;
  wire [7:0]  e_sml    = swap ? ea   : eb;
  wire [23:0] sig_big  = swap ? sigb : siga;
  wire [23:0] sig_sml  = swap ? siga : sigb;
  wire        eff_sub  = sa ^ sb;

  // ---- step 4: alignment with G/R/S capture -- chapter 2's align_sticky
  // at W = 24, saturation at W+2 = 26 (a shift of 26 already puts the whole
  // significand below the round position). The exponent difference is 8
  // bits (0..253); the shifter count is 5 bits with an explicit clamp --
  // chapter 2's full-width-localparam lesson, applied.
  wire [7:0]  d     = e_big - e_sml;
  wire [4:0]  shamt = (d > 8'd26) ? 5'd26 : d[4:0];
  wire [23:0] aligned;
  wire        g_al, r_al, s_al;
  align_sticky #(.W(24), .SHW(5)) u_align
    (.mant(sig_sml), .shamt(shamt),
     .aligned(aligned), .guard(g_al), .round(r_al), .sticky(s_al));
```

**Step 4 is a module you already own.** Chapter 2's "Reduction Operators and the Sticky Bit" built `align_sticky` before there was a floating point number in sight, and it is instantiated here *unchanged* — the chapter 8 build targets compile `../ch02/align_sticky.v` into this datapath, so the claim is regression-guarded, not sentimental. At `W = 24` its contract is exactly step 4: keep the top 24 shifted bits as `aligned`, expose the first bit shifted out as **guard** (weight ½ ulp of the 24-bit frame), the second as **round** (¼ ulp), and the reduction OR of *every* bit strictly below round as **sticky**. Three properties of this stage deserve their own sentences.

**Sticky must OR all the way down.** The sticky bit answers one question — is there *anything* nonzero below the round position? — and "anything" has no depth limit. The pair `3F800000 + 33800001` = 1 + (2⁻²⁴ + 2⁻⁴⁷) makes it concrete: alignment by d = 24 puts the small operand's leading 1 in G and its trailing 1 **twenty-three positions below R**. That single deep bit is what makes the exact sum strictly greater than a half-ulp tie, so RNE must round up to `3F800001`; a sticky that misses it returns `3F800000`, one ulp low. "Why the Shortcuts Fail" measures the bulk cost; mutation M2 — a sticky that sees only the first lost bit — died on exactly this vector.

**Saturating the shift at 26 is correct, not lazy.** A shift of 26 already puts the entire 24-bit significand below the R position: `aligned = G = R = 0` and `sticky = |sig`, and no larger shift can change anything. The saturation point is `W + 2`, encoded in chapter 2's module as a full-width localparam for the reason that chapter belabored; the clamp above the instantiation (`d > 8'd26 ? 5'd26 : d[4:0]`) is this chapter's own application of the same lesson, since `d` spans 0…253 and the shifter count is 5 bits. Delete that clamp and the distance wraps mod 32: mutation M15 turned `2²⁴ + 2⁻²⁴` (d = 48, wrapping to 16) into `4B800080` — garbage bits injected into a sum whose true answer changes only in the inexact flag. `-Wall` says nothing about any of it (the chapter 2 finding); only a testbench catches it.

**This region is where random stimulus lives.** Chapter 5's row E computed it exactly: 81.6 % of uniform-random normal pairs have exponents at least 25 apart, and 80.9 % are at least 26 apart — the distance at which the small operand vanishes below R entirely, influencing only sticky and the inexact flag. (At exactly d = 25 its leading bit still lands *in* R, where the effective-subtract borrow can move the result: `4B000000 + BEFFFFFF` = `4AFFFFFF`.) Random testing thus overweights the easiest case and starves every other path in this chapter; the census in "The Machine: One Module, Four Testbenches" prints the bias live.

## The Width Budget: 28 Bits

How many bits does the datapath need so that one rounding at the end equals rounding the exact sum? The answer — **24 significand bits + G + R + carry = a 27-bit adder, with the 1-bit sticky flag alongside: 28 bits of state** — is the most consequential number in this guide. Concretely:

```
bit:   26   25 ........ 2   1   0     + 1 sticky flag alongside
       cy   [ 24-bit sig ]  G   R
       └─ 27-bit adder result ─┘          total datapath state: 28 bits
```

The aligned small operand and the big operand each occupy 26 bits (`{sig[23:0], G, R}`); their sum needs one carry bit, hence 27; sticky rides beside the adder and enters it only as the effective-subtract borrow of "The Effective Operation and the Sticky Borrow". Whether sticky is drawn as "bit −1 of a 28-bit vector" or as a flag beside a 27-bit vector is layout taste — `align_sticky` chose the flag form — but the state count is 28 either way.

**Why exactly these bits.** Let w be the weight of the R position; after alignment the kept small operand is a truncation: true value = kept + f·w with 0 ≤ f < 1, sticky ⇔ f ≠ 0. Enumerate what normalization can do to the frame, and what the final rounding needs in each case:

| normalize outcome | final ulp | rounding needs | supplied by |
|---|---|---|---|
| right-1 (add carried) | 8w | half-ulp = old LSB; below = old G, R, S | `G′ = LSB`, `S′ = S ∨ R`, `R′ = G` after the shift |
| none | 4w | half-ulp = G; below = R ∨ S | G, R, S directly |
| left-1 (subtract, d ≥ 2 possible) | 2w | half-ulp = old R; below = S | **R becomes the new G**; S unchanged |
| left ≥ 2 (subtract, only at d ≤ 1) | ≤ w | nothing — the result is exact | R = S = 0; zeros shift in |

Read the rows as the reason each bit exists. **G** is the half-ulp bit of the common, no-shift case. **R** exists *solely* to survive the one-bit left normalize: after that shift the old R is the new half-ulp bit, and folding R into S destroys precisely that information — the GS counterexample in "Why the Shortcuts Fail" is this row failing. **S** exists because "strictly past the tie" differs from "exactly the tie" by *any* nonzero residue, however deep; one OR-tree bit represents it. The last row explains why nothing *more* than R is ever needed: a left shift of two or more can only happen at d ≤ 1, since with d ≥ 2 the aligned small operand is at most half the big one's frame value — `big − small ≥ 2²⁵·w − 2²⁴·w = 2²⁴·w` — and the difference keeps its leading 1 within one position of the top. At d ≤ 1, at most one bit (into G) ever left the significand, so the long left shift pulls zeros in under a value that is already exact.

That argument has teeth because it was *asserted*, not assumed: the datapath model checks `shift ≥ 2 ⟹ R = 0 ∧ S = 0` on every pair, and the assertion stayed silent across all 1,000,058 sweep pairs plus a 200,000-pair census run. The honest statement this chapter is entitled to print: **28 bits of state sufficed on every pair we measured — a million-pair mixed sweep plus every taxonomy vector — and the exactness invariant the sufficiency argument leans on never fired its assertion.** (The normalize section has a sharper story about that invariant's exact *shape*, because its first draft was wrong.)

**The exponent side has a budget too**, and `-Wall` will warn about none of it. The exponent difference `d` needs 8 bits (0…253) — 9 if computed signed, before the swap. The alignment shift count must express the saturation point 26: 5 bits, with the explicit clamp of "Compare, Swap, and the Alignment Shift". Result-exponent arithmetic wants a guard bit — 9 bits — so 254 + 1 and the normalize subtraction cannot wrap; mutation M8's most spectacular kill, `00000001 + 00000001` returning `7F800000`, is a 9-bit exponent wrapping under a clamp-deleted normalizer: two copies of the smallest positive number, added, producing infinity. And the *normalize* shifter's range is **0…24, not 0…23**. The bound is reached: the smallest nonzero difference the frame can hold is a lone G bit (from a d = 1 subtract), 24 positions below the top. `1.0 − (1 − 2⁻²⁴)` — `3F800000 + BF7FFFFF` — leaves exactly that bit; its trace in "Twelve Additions, Traced End to End" shows `left 24`, and the result `33800000` = 2⁻²⁴ is exact. A counter sized 0…23 "because the significand has 24 bits" is off by one; mutation M6 built exactly that, and only this vector killed it.

> **Seed for chapter 9.** These are the register widths, fixed now: a 27-bit significand adder plus a sticky flag (28 bits of state), 8-bit exponent difference, 5-bit shift counts on both shifters (alignment clamped at 26, normalize spanning 0…24), 9-bit result-exponent arithmetic. Chapter 9 declares these wires on day one and never revisits them — and its testbenches, not its compiler, are what guard them, because silent truncation warns nowhere (the chapter 2 finding, still true at 13.0).

## The Effective Operation and the Sticky Borrow

The operation the adder performs is not the operation the program wrote. `eff_sub = sign_a XOR sign_b`: adding unlike signs is a magnitude *subtraction*, subtracting unlike signs a magnitude *addition*. With the bigger magnitude leading, both cases are one unsigned operation on the 26-bit frames — chapter 6's odometer, whose 27th bit "Carry Is Not Overflow" taught you to read as information, not error. From `src/ch08/fp32_add_alg.v`:

```verilog
  // ---- step 5: effective operation. Add the two 26-bit quantities into a
  // 27-bit sum; on an effective subtract, inject sticky as a BORROW: the
  // true small operand is (kept + f) with 0 < f < 1 in round-bit units
  // whenever sticky is set, so the true difference is one less than the
  // truncated difference -- and still inexact, so sticky stays set.
  wire [26:0] big27 = {1'b0, sig_big, 2'b00};
  wire [26:0] sml27 = {1'b0, aligned, g_al, r_al};
  wire [26:0] sum27 = eff_sub ? (big27 - sml27 - {26'd0, s_al})
                              : (big27 + sml27);

  // An effective subtract that comes out exactly zero has no sign of its
  // own: the result is +0 by rule (IEEE 754-2019 6.3), not by datapath.
  // (sum27 == 0 with sticky set cannot happen: sticky needs d >= 3, and
  // then the aligned operand is under a quarter of the big one.)
  wire exact_zero = eff_sub & (sum27 == 27'd0);
```

The add direction is unremarkable: 26 bits plus 26 bits into 27, sticky untouched. The subtract direction hides this chapter's subtlest line: **sticky enters the subtraction as a borrow.** "Subtract one more, keep sticky set" looks arbitrary until derived. When sticky is set, the *true* small operand is `kept + f` with 0 < f < 1 in units of the R bit — the aligner truncated it. So the true difference is

```
big − (kept + f)  =  (big − kept − 1) + (1 − f)
```

— one less than the truncated difference, plus a *positive* residue `(1 − f)` strictly between 0 and 1, nonzero exactly when sticky was set. The datapath therefore subtracts the extra 1 and *leaves sticky set*: the computed frame is again a truncation of the true result, with the same flag telling the rounder "there is a little more below R than you see". This is the width-budget table's truncation-plus-residue framing — the borrow is what makes that table apply to subtraction unchanged.

Omitting the borrow is a real bug with a measured cost: a variant identical except for this one term got **5,310 of 200,000 mixed random pairs wrong (2.66 %)** this session. The cleanest kill is `40000000 + B3800001` — 2.0 − (2⁻²⁴ + 2⁻⁴⁷). The true difference is a hair *below* 2.0, so the answer must be `3FFFFFFF` = 1.99999988…; without the borrow the datapath computes exactly 2.0, because the entire subtrahend lives below the R position and only the borrow lets it matter at all. One missing wire, and subtraction can no longer tell "slightly less than x" from "exactly x". Mutation M7 is this bug; the corner library kills it on `3F800001 + B3800001` (truth `3F800000`, mutant `3F800001`).

The `exact_zero` wire is the step 2 leftover: a complete cancellation is +0 *by rule*, not by any sign computation — and the listing's parenthetical is why no `~sticky` guard is needed, an invariant the million-pair sweep never contradicted. (Mutation M18 added the redundant guard and survived every testbench, because it is provably equivalent — an equivalent mutant, not a testbench gap.)

## Normalize: Three Outcomes and One Corrected Invariant

The sum leaves step 5 as a 27-bit frame whose leading 1 may not be where the format wants it. Chapter 5's row I named the three shifter behaviours; here is each as hardware, verbatim from `src/ch08/fp32_add_alg.v`:

```verilog
  wire        carry = sum27[26];
  wire        right1 = ~eff_sub & carry;
  wire [25:0] frame = sum27[25:0];

  function [4:0] lzc26(input [25:0] v);
    integer i;
    begin
      lzc26 = 5'd26;
      for (i = 0; i <= 25; i = i + 1)
        if (v[25 - i] == 1'b1 && lzc26 == 5'd26)
          lzc26 = i[4:0];
    end
  endfunction

  wire [4:0]  lz    = lzc26(frame);
  wire [7:0]  e_lim = e_big - 8'd1;
  wire [7:0]  shl8  = ({3'b000, lz} > e_lim) ? e_lim : {3'b000, lz};
  wire [4:0]  shl   = shl8[4:0];            // min(lz, e_big-1) <= 26: lossless
  wire [25:0] framel = frame << shl;        // zeros shift in: exact, because a
                                            // left shift of 2+ implies d <= 1,
                                            // where R and sticky are 0
  wire [23:0] nsig = right1 ? sum27[26:3]         : framel[25:2];
  wire        ng   = right1 ? sum27[2]            : framel[1];
  wire        nr   = right1 ? sum27[1]            : framel[0];
  wire        ns   = right1 ? (s_al | sum27[0])   : s_al;
  wire [8:0]  e_norm = right1 ? ({1'b0, e_big} + 9'd1)
                              : ({1'b0, e_big} - {4'b0000, shl});
```

**Carry out — right 1** (effective add only; an effective subtract can never carry, since the bigger magnitude leads). Shift right one, increment the exponent, and route the three low bits with care: the old LSB becomes the new G, the old G becomes the new R, and the old R **folds into sticky** — `S′ = S | R`. Each routing is a separate way to be wrong, and the mutation record kills each: discard the shifted-off bit instead of making it G (M9) and `(2 − 2⁻²³) + 1.5` comes out `405FFFFF` instead of `40600000` — one ulp low on a pair you can check on your fingers; drop old R from the fold (M16) and the bug is so quiet it *survived the directed library's first draft* — the story is in "Why the Shortcuts Fail".

**No shift.** The leading 1 is already at bit 23 — the common case, and the reason G had to exist at all.

**Leading zeros — left N** (effective subtract only). Count leading zeros of the 26-bit frame, shift left by `min(LZC, eff_e − 1)`, decrement the exponent by the shift. The clamp is the entire subnormal-output story: a result still unnormalized when the effective exponent reaches 1 *is a subnormal*, E field 0, and chasing the leading 1 further fabricates exponents the format cannot encode. Gradual underflow, at the datapath level, is **doing nothing** — leave the leading zeros and let the pack stage write E = 0. Delete the clamp (M8) and minnormal − minsubnormal comes out `007FFFFE` instead of `007FFFFF`, while `00000001 + 00000001` — leading 1 twenty-two places down — walks the 9-bit exponent through zero and returns **infinity**. W6 below traces the clamp doing its job.

**Why shifting zeros in is legal — and the invariant that had to be corrected.** The left shift pulls fresh zeros into the bottom of the frame, sound only if nothing real was down there. The width-budget section argued it: a left shift of 2 or more forces d ≤ 1, where at most one bit ever left the significand. This chapter's research first drafted the invariant as "after massive cancellation, G, R and S are all dead" — and the million-pair sweep falsified it within seconds. The truth, measured: **only R and sticky are provably zero; G can be live.** At d = 1 the guard position can hold a live bit of the small operand, and the left shift carries it back *into* the significand, exactly. Not even rarely: in a 200,000-pair census, 2,585 of the 13,493 massive cancellations — **19.2 %** — entered the left shift with G set; the lone-G case `1.0 − (1 − 2⁻²⁴)` cancels everything *except* the guard bit and promotes it 24 places to a full, exact result. The corrected assertion (`shift ≥ 2 ⟹ R = 0 ∧ S = 0`) then survived every pair measured. Keep the episode: a plausible invariant, one bit too strong, dead in seconds under a sweep — claims-versus-measurements is not a slogan, it is the workflow.

**Sterbenz's lemma is this datapath fact wearing a mathematician's clothes.** The lemma (Sterbenz, 1974): if x and y are same-format floating point values with y/2 ≤ x ≤ 2y, then x − y is exactly representable — the subtraction commits no rounding error at all. In this chapter's terms, the condition forces d ≤ 1 and `|x − y| ≤ min(x, y)`, so the difference sits on the operands' shared grid and needs no more bits than they had. Verified three ways this session: *exhaustively* in an enumerable toy format (5-bit significand, exponents −2…3, subnormals included) — all 3,151 in-condition pairs subtracted exactly, zero violations; *at scale* — 200,000 random conforming binary32 pairs, zero inexact differences; and *at the boundary*: `(2 − 2⁻²³) − (1 − 2⁻²⁴)` is exactly x = 2y and subtracts exactly, while `2.0 − (1 − 2⁻²⁴)` — a ratio one ulp past 2 — needs 25 bits and rounds. One ulp of operand change across the 2:1 line switches the guarantee off.

Two consequences, stated precisely. First, **"catastrophic cancellation" is not error committed by the subtraction** — the subtraction is exact; the catastrophe is that the few surviving bits inherit whatever rounding error the *operands* brought in, at a hugely larger relative scale (Goldberg's benign/catastrophic distinction: catastrophic when the operands are themselves rounded, benign when exact). The adder's whole obligation is to add no insult, and Sterbenz says it doesn't. Second, the lemma **requires gradual underflow**: 932 of the toy format's 3,151 in-range pairs — 29.6 % — have a nonzero exact difference that is *subnormal*. Flush-to-zero turns every one into 0, and `x == y` becomes indistinguishable from `x` near `y`. That number is the strongest single argument behind the decision, recorded in chapter 7, that this guide's adder will not flush.

## Round, and the Renormalize Random Never Finds

After normalization the evidence is assembled: a 24-bit significand, its LSB **L**, and the bits below — G at half an ulp, R at a quarter, S for everything further down. Round to nearest, ties to even, is the one-gate decision chapter 7 verified exhaustively at small width and chapter 6 first taught in "Shortening a Result: Rounding and Its Bias":

```verilog
  wire        lbit     = nsig[0];
  wire        round_up = ng & (nr | ns | lbit);
  wire [24:0] rsig     = {1'b0, nsig} + {24'd0, round_up};
  wire        round_renorm = rsig[24];
  wire [23:0] fsig     = round_renorm ? 24'h800000 : rsig[23:0];
  wire [8:0]  e_rnd    = e_norm + {8'd0, round_renorm};
```

Reading `round_up = G & (R | S | L)`: G = 1 with anything below means strictly past halfway — up. G = 1 with nothing below is an *exact tie* — up only if L is odd, so ties split by parity and long accumulations pick up no drift. G = 0 — down, always. Both tie directions must appear in any vector list, because an always-up rounder passes every test that exercises only one (row G; W5a and W5b are the pair).

The two lines after the equation are this section's real subject. When the increment lands on a significand of twenty-four ones, it carries out: 24 ones + 1 = 2²⁴, and the exponent must step up — a **second, rarely-taken renormalize**, after the rounding the first normalize was supposed to precede. `fsig` collapses to the constant `24'h800000`, the only value a rounding carry can produce.

Now the measurement that should change how you write testbenches. In this session's million-pair mixed random sweep — a generator deliberately biased toward the interesting regimes, not just uniform bits — the rounding-carry renormalize fired **zero times in 1,000,000 pairs**. In the simulator, `tb_random.v`'s five-regime campaign hit it **zero times in 10,000 pairs** and prints that census on every run. The path is real, mandatory, one gate from the most-travelled wire in the design — and random stimulus of any practical size will likely never toggle it, because reaching it needs a post-normalize significand of all ones *and* bits below that force a round-up: directed vectors or nothing. The corner library carries three distinct pairs that reach it — `3FFFFFFF + 33800000` (in range: (2 − 2⁻²³) + 2⁻²⁴ rounds up to exactly 2.0), `4B800000 + B3800000` (the subtract side: 2²⁴ − 2⁻²⁴, where the *sticky borrow* creates the all-ones significand that rounds back up), and `7F7FFFFF + 73000000` (the overflow side, next section) — and a **coverage bin that fails the regression if none of them reaches the wire**. Mutation M4 deleted the renormalize; the directed set killed it instantly, and the random testbench never noticed. That asymmetry, printed by your own regression, is chapter 5's "Coverage, and Building One by Hand" with a sharper example than that chapter had available.

> **Seed for chapters 9 and 12.** Carry the four renormalize vectors forward by name, and keep the bin. "The rounding-carry renormalize ran" must be a checked property of every regression, not a hope — this chapter measured how unreachable the path is by chance (0 in 10⁶), and the mutation record shows what a coverage gate catches that a check count cannot: T3c deleted all four vectors, *updated the check count to match*, and only the empty bin failed the run.

## Pack, Overflow, and the Flags

Packing is four lines, each a policy this guide has already argued for. From `src/ch08/fp32_add_alg.v`:

```verilog
  wire        ovf = (e_rnd > 9'd254);
  wire [31:0] dp_res =
      exact_zero ? 32'h00000000 :
      ovf        ? {sign_big, 8'd255, 23'd0} :
      fsig[23]   ? {sign_big, e_rnd[7:0], fsig[22:0]} :
                   {sign_big, 8'd0,       fsig[22:0]};
```

**Overflow is a property of the exponent after rounding** — hence the test reads `e_rnd`, not `e_norm`, and needs the 9-bit guard — and two distinct hardware paths land here. *Magnitude* overflow arrives through the right-1 normalize: maxnormal + maxnormal carries out, 254 becomes 255. *Rounding-induced* overflow arrives through the renormalize of "Round, and the Renormalize Random Never Finds": maxnormal + 2¹⁰³ has a **finite** exact sum, but the tie against an all-ones (odd) significand rounds up, carries out, and pushes 254 to 255 — an infinity manufactured entirely by the rounder (chapter 7's "The Flags an Adder Can Actually Raise" pinned both paths; its threshold `(2 − 2⁻²⁴)·2¹²⁷` is exactly this pair's sum). Under RNE the overflow result is ±infinity with the result sign. The near miss matters as much as the hit: one ulp less in the small operand — `7F7FFFFF + 72FFFFFF` — stays at `7F7FFFFF`; the corner library checks both sides of the line.

**A clear bit 23 means the exponent is 1 and the result is subnormal (or zero) — write E = 0 and stop.** No shift, no special case: the normalizer's clamp paying off. Its mirror image is chapter 7's promotion-for-free — a significand whose bit 23 *is* set at effective exponent 1 packs as E = 1 with the same fraction bits, which is how `007FFFFF + 00000001` crosses the subnormal/normal boundary without a seam (W8b).

**The flags, and the wire that is deliberately missing.** invalid comes from the screen alone. overflow is the wire above. inexact is `G | R | S` after normalization, OR overflow — needed because a magnitude overflow can have all-zero G/R/S (2 × maxnormal is an exact sum; its infinity is still not that sum). And there is **no underflow output at all**, with the proof in the module header rather than an apology: a tiny addition result is always exact — chapter 7 proved it from the 2⁻¹⁴⁹ grid, and this session's re-measurement found 10,635 subnormal-or-zero results in a 200,000-pair mixed sweep with **zero** inexact among them. A flag that provably cannot fire deserves no register; chapter 12's specification can cite this paragraph. The remaining discipline is a testbench lesson: mutation M14 made inexact ignore sticky, and *only* a testbench that checks flags — both shipped directed testbenches do, on every vector — could see it; the result bits are identical.

## Why the Shortcuts Fail

Every bit in the width budget earns its keep by the failures its absence causes. The budget's negative space: four degraded datapaths, identical to the shipped one except for the bits kept, against the exact reference on 200,000 mixed random pairs this session:

```
bulk failure rates over 200000 random mixed pairs:
  GR   : 5347 wrong of 200000  (2.674 %)    <- sticky dropped
  GS   : 950 wrong of 200000  (0.475 %)     <- R dropped
  G    : 15179 wrong of 200000 (7.590 %)    <- G only
  none : 52826 wrong of 200000 (26.413 %)   <- truncate everything
```

(The research pass's differently-weighted generator measured the same variants at 3.87 %, 0.35 %, 9.51 % and 28.64 % — sticky loss there broke eleven times more pairs than R loss, here about six times. The *ratio* is a property of the stimulus mix; what is stable, and what to teach, is the ordering: *G loss worst, sticky loss next, R loss rarest* — and rarest is the dangerous kind, the one a casual regression misses.) Every failure observed is a final result off by exactly one ulp, or the flag side of a tie: not crashes but the worst kind of bug — small, rare, invisible to any "close enough" testbench, which is why chapter 5 banned tolerance comparisons. One hand-verified counterexample per variant, all with deliberately simple operands:

- **Truncate everything (`none`): `3F800001 + 33800000`** = (1 + 2⁻²³) + 2⁻²⁴. The exact sum sits half an ulp above an *odd* significand, so RNE must round up to `3F800002`; truncation stays at `3F800001`.
- **G only, add side: `3F800000 + 33800001`** — the deep-sticky pair from the alignment section. The excess over 1 is *more* than half an ulp by 2⁻⁴⁷; with only G the datapath sees G = 1 and nothing else, indistinguishable from an exact tie, and ties-to-even keeps the even `3F800000`. One lost bit at position 47 flips the rounding.
- **G only, subtract side: `3F800000 + BE800003`** = 1 − 0.25·(1 + 3·2⁻²³), d = 2. The exact difference is a tie whose even neighbour is `3F3FFFFE`; the 2-bit alignment shifts two live bits out but G keeps one, the lost bit makes the computed difference *larger*, and the variant lands on `3F3FFFFF` — one ulp wrong on the wrong side of a tie.
- **R dropped (`GS`): `3F800000 + BE800001`** = 1 − 0.25·(1 + 2⁻²³), d = 2 — the left-1-normalize case, the rarest class. The exact difference is half an ulp below 0.75: a tie, even side `3F400000`. The GS datapath's borrow can only land at G weight — its only position — over-subtracting; after the left-1 normalize the information old R carried is gone, the datapath believes the value is strictly below the tie, and returns `3F3FFFFF`. With R present, the left shift promotes R into the new G and the tie is seen. That is the concrete meaning of "R exists to survive the one-bit normalize"; reaching it at random needs an effective subtract with d ≥ 2, cancellation of exactly one bit, and a live rounding boundary *simultaneously*.

Goldberg's classic statement of the positive result (fetched source): "By introducing a second guard digit and a third *sticky* bit, differences can be computed at only a little more cost than with a single guard digit, but the result is the same as if the difference were computed exactly and then rounded." The million-pair sweep is this project's measured version of that sentence; the four variants are its measured converse. And the failure *patterns* are diagnostic: wrong only on ties-that-should-not-be-ties → sticky; wrong only after cancellation by exactly one bit → R; wrong near every halfway case → G.

**Rounding early is a fifth shortcut, and the costliest.** A naive implementation that RNE-rounds the *aligned small operand* to the big operand's 24-bit grid before adding got **29,827 of 299,516 pairs wrong (9.96 %)** this session. The cleanest failure deletes an operand outright: in `3F800001 + 33800000`, pre-rounding 2⁻²⁴ to the grid of 1.0 is an exact half-step tie, ties-to-even rounds it to **zero**, and the addend evaporates — returning `3F800001` where the true sum rounds to `3F800002`. G/R/S exist precisely so the one legal rounding happens once, at the end, with the tie evidence intact.

**Double rounding is the same disease at the output.** Round the exact sum to an intermediate precision, then to binary32, and the composition is not rounding-once: the first rounding can land a just-below-tie value exactly *onto* the tie, and the second rounding's even-rule steps over the answer. Measured over 299,502 finite random pairs: through a 25-bit intermediate, **15,900 wrong (5.31 %)**; through a 53-bit intermediate, **0 wrong**. The directed pair, worked exactly: `3F800001 + 33040000` = (1 + 2⁻²³) + (2⁻²⁵ + 2⁻³⁰). The part below the final grid is less than half an ulp — rounding once goes down, to `3F800001`. Via 25 bits, the value sits 2⁻³⁰ *past* the intermediate's half-step: the first rounding goes up, creating an exact binary32 tie against an odd LSB, and the second goes up again — `3F800002`, one ulp high, each rounding individually correct. The 53-bit column is the safe harbor: double rounding is innocuous for sums of two p-bit values whenever the intermediate has at least 2p + 2 digits (Figueroa, title-only), and 53 ≥ 50 — the entire license behind the shortreal reference of "The Reference Model Verdict for Chapter 9".

**And one shortcut hid inside this chapter's own testbench.** Mutation M16 degraded the right-1 renormalize's fold from `S′ = S | R` to `S′ = S` — and on its first run it *survived the entire directed library*, dying only under `tb_random`. A mutant that survives your stimulus is not an equivalent mutant; it is a gap in your stimulus (chapter 4's lesson, back with teeth). A python search over simple d = 2 carry-out pairs produced the directed kill `3FFFFFFF + 3E800009 → 40100001` — d = 2 puts a live bit in R, the add carries out, and the folded sticky is the only witness the result is past the tie — which now sits in the corner library with a comment naming its origin. The library you inherit is one vector stronger than its author's first draft, and the mutation record is why.

## The Reference Model Verdict for Chapter 9

Chapter 9's testbench needs a reference model; here is the verdict with evidence attached. The trustworthy path is one line per operand pair — `$bitstoshortreal` each operand, one `+`, one `$shortrealtobits` — as `tb_refmodel.v`'s checker task applies it, verbatim:

```verilog
  task one_add(input [31:0] wa, input [31:0] wb, input [31:0] expct);
    reg [31:0] refbits;
    begin
      refbits = $shortrealtobits($bitstoshortreal(wa) + $bitstoshortreal(wb));
```

Why it is sound: Icarus stores `shortreal` as a double (the chapter 5 finding), assignment does not round (chapter 7's "shortreal: The Bit-Level Laboratory"), so the `+` is one double-precision add and `$shortrealtobits` is one binary32 rounding — a 53-bit intermediate, inside the 2p + 2 = 50 safe harbor measured at 0 wrong in 299,502 pairs. Exponent range is never a worry: binary32 sums live in [2⁻¹⁴⁹, 2¹²⁹), deep inside double's normal range. And it is not merely argued — `tb_refmodel.v` probes the path at the thirteen boundary cases where a shortreal reference is most suspect, and `tb_walk.v` plus `tb_random.v` hold it against the exact model and the DUT on every vector they run. Its transcript, captured this session:

```
  inf + (-inf) via shortreal = ffc00000 (class NaN; sign not asserted)
  sNaN 7fa00000 pure round-trip = 7fe00000 (quieted, payload kept)
  serial DUT chain  = 3f800000
  naive double chain= 3f800001
  disciplined chain = 3f800000
PASS tb_refmodel (13 boundary one-adds, NaN class, sNaN, chain)
```

Four exclusions, each measured, each with the prescribed alternative:

1. **NaN sign and payload.** The runtime `inf + (−inf)` NaN came out `ffc00000` — *negative* — on this host, while this same simulator constant-folds a *positive* one (chapter 7's three-act measurement). Compare NaN results **by class, never by bits**; payload checks are legitimate only against your own design's written contract, as `tb_corners.v` does.
2. **sNaN operands.** A signalling NaN cannot be *delivered* through the value domain: the pure round-trip above quiets it with no arithmetic involved (host-FPU behaviour, chapter 7). Build sNaN vectors as bit patterns in a `reg [31:0]`, drive them straight into the DUT, and compute expected outputs at the bit level.
3. **Chains.** One add is licensed; a chain is not. The transcript's middle lines are the measured trap: with a = 1.0 and b = c = `33000001` (2⁻²⁵ + 2⁻⁴⁸), the naive `$shortrealtobits(a + b + c)` gives `3f800001` while a *correct* serial binary32 adder — the DUT, run twice — gives `3f800000`. The naive chain is not wrong arithmetic; it equals the exact triple sum rounded once, a *more accurate* answer than binary32 hardware can produce — chapter 5's trap of a reference "more accurate than the specification", the most dangerous in this project, here live. The discipline: round-trip through the bits functions after **every** add, which the `disciplined chain` line confirms matches the serial DUT. Chapter 10's four-input reference inherits this rule squared.
4. **Flags.** The shortreal path yields result bits only; expected invalid/overflow/inexact come from the exact python model as expectation vectors beside the bits — exactly how `tb_corners.v` carries them.

Stimulus rules ride along: seed `$urandom` once and discard the first draw; echo the seed; and treat the directed list as the *only* coverage of several wires, not garnish on a random campaign that provably never visits them.

## How Production Adders Rearrange It

**Everything in this section is documentation from the literature, not something measured here** — the epistemic wall chapters 3 and 4 maintain, stated up front. It exists because chapter 11 will pipeline this design, and you should meet the two standard reorganizations with this chapter's measurements in hand.

**The two-path (close/far) organization.** The dichotomy this chapter *did* measure — massive cancellation only at d ≤ 1; at most a one-position shift everywhere else — is exploited by production adders as two parallel datapaths (Ercegovac & Lang; Koren; Muller et al.; Seidel & Even 2004 — all title-only). The **far path** (d ≥ 2, plus all effective additions) has the big alignment shifter with G/R/S capture, the wide add, at most a ±1 normalize, full rounding — steps 4–8 as taught here. The **close path** (d ≤ 1, effective subtraction) needs no big right shifter but owns the leading-zero problem and the big *left* shifter; its rounding is degenerate because, as this chapter measured, the d ≤ 1 difference is exact apart from the single G bit. Each path drops the other's expensive block, selection needs only the exponent difference and the sign XOR, and the two shorter paths balance better across pipeline stages. The motive is latency, not correctness — the one-path algorithm is complete and exact.

**LZC versus LZA.** This chapter's normalizer *counts* leading zeros after the subtraction completes — a serial dependency: subtract, count, shift. A leading-zero *anticipator* predicts the count from the operands in parallel with the subtraction, from a bit-pair propagate/generate/kill pattern, at the price of a possible off-by-one absorbed by a final one-bit correction shift (Schmookler & Nowka 2001 — title-only; existence confirmed by search, not fetched). For this guide: chapter 9 uses the honest LZC — the `lzc26` priority encoder you have already read — and chapter 11 may present LZA as the timing optimization whose correctness argument is precisely the step-7 invariant plus one correction shift.

**A calibration before quoting any census at the literature.** This chapter's numbers — 19 % saturated alignments, 7 % massive cancellations, 21 % carry renormalizes in the random campaign — are properties of *that stimulus generator*, not of workloads; the two-path argument in the literature is about critical paths, not case frequencies. Keep the two kinds of numbers apart.

## Order Is Part of the Answer

The guide's goal is a *four*-input adder, and this chapter ends by arming you for chapter 10's central fact: binary32 addition is **not associative**, so "the sum of four numbers" is undefined until the design document fixes an association order — and the reference model must fix the *same* one. Two triples, every add individually verified through the exact model this session, both reducible to this chapter's own worked examples:

**T1 — a = 2²⁴, b = c = 1.** Left association: a + b is W5a's exact tie, down to 16,777,216; adding the second 1 is *the same tie again*, down again — result 16,777,216. Right association: b + c = 2 exactly; a + 2 = 16,777,218 is representable (the ulp at 2²⁴ is 2) — result 16,777,218, the exactly rounded true sum. The left association lost both units to back-to-back ties; nothing exotic happened, W5a simply fired twice from a particular order.

**T2 — a = b = maxnormal, c = −maxnormal.** Left association: a + b overflows to +∞, and infinity is *absorbing* through the screen — adding −maxnormal returns +∞. Right association: b + c = 0 exactly, a + 0 = maxnormal, the exactly rounded true sum. One grouping is correct and finite; the other is infinite *and no later operand can repair it*. For a four-input adder, tree shape is part of the arithmetic specification, not an implementation detail.

How common is grouping-dependence? Measured this session, 200,000 random triples per regime — sign and fraction uniform, exponents drawn as stated — `(a+b)+c` versus `a+(b+c)`:

```
exponents uniform over the full normal range :  0.64 % differ
exponents within 10                          : 21.79 % differ
exponents within 2                           : 32.41 % differ
```

Not a corner-case curiosity: spread the exponents and grouping almost never matters, but for operands of similar magnitude — the accumulator case — nearly a third of random triples change value with association. Chapter 10 owns the design consequences; the testbench consequence you already hold: the reference must replicate the DUT's association order add by add, round-tripping between every one.
