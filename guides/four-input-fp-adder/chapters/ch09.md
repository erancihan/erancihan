## Cutting Along the Seams

Chapter 8 left you with a working adder and a warning. The working adder is `fp32_add_alg.v`: one combinational module, ten named steps, proven bit-for-bit against exact rational arithmetic over more than a million operand pairs. The warning was in its bridge: that module is *indifferent to how long its longest path is* — and to who reviews it, who reuses its pieces, and where chapter 11 will put its pipeline registers. This chapter turns the proven algorithm into a proven *design*: seven modules and a top, each with a port contract you can state as a checkable sentence, verified module by module AND end to end. The first RTL chapter of this guide is therefore mostly a verification chapter wearing RTL clothes, and that is deliberate — the composition step is precisely where a new class of bugs lives, and this chapter measures every one of them.

Why decompose at all? Four reasons, each of which this chapter demonstrates rather than asserts. **Review**: a 27-bit adder with a sticky borrow is checkable in isolation in six directed vectors; buried in a 170-line module it is checkable only by staring. **Reuse**: chapter 10's four-input adder instantiates these exact modules; a latent contract bug in one of them (you will meet it — `exact_zero`, measured below) surfaces exactly then. **Pipelining**: chapter 11 inserts registers *at these module boundaries*, and this chapter counts what each cut costs — 100, 95, 73 and 72 bits. **Verification**: module-level testbenches kill bug classes that a 100,092-check end-to-end campaign provably cannot see, and the end-to-end sweep kills classes that leave every unit testbench green. Both directions of that sentence are measured in this chapter, with mutation counts, and together they are its thesis.

And why *these* seams? Because the algorithm already drew them. `fp32_add_alg.v` names its step blocks — unpack, screen, swap, align, add/sub, normalize, round-pack, and a final mux — and the split falls out almost mechanically: one module per step block, the screen as a sibling of the datapath rather than a stage of it, and a top that owns exactly three assigns. The one genuinely free choice is where the boundaries sit relative to chapter 11's registers, and each section below defends its boundary on those grounds. Nothing in the arithmetic changes. The proof standard does not change either: the composed design is **bit-identical to chapter 8's golden module — result and all three flags — over 200,092 checks** this session, and the testbench that proves it is this chapter's centerpiece.

Chapter 8's bridge promised that the first pair this adder adds would be one you can trace blindfolded: `3FC00000 + 40100000`, its tracer's W1. Promise kept — here is the composed design executing it, every value printed from a *module boundary* rather than an internal wire, captured this session:

```
unpack : ea=127 eb=128 siga=c00000 sigb=900000
swap   : sign_big=0 e_big=128 e_sml=127 sig_big=900000 sig_sml=c00000 eff_sub=0
align  : aligned=600000 g=0 r=0 s=0
addsub : sum27=3c00000 exact_zero=0
norm   : nsig=f00000 ng=0 nr=0 ns=0 e_norm=128
round  : dp_res=40700000 ovf=0
top    : result=40700000 flags=000 (screen=0)
```

1.5 + 2.25 = 3.75, `40700000`, exact. Same steps, same numbers as chapter 8's trace — but now every line of that printout is a *port list*, and every port list is a contract with its own testbench. The working method is unchanged from eight chapters: Icarus Verilog 13.0, `iverilog -g2012 -Wall` with zero-output compiles on every run target, expected values from independent python3 models, every claim below re-measured this session. All six build targets are green through `run_all.sh ch09`; the full-repo regression stands at 92/92.

## Seven Modules and a Top: The Port Map

Here is the whole design, once. Golden step numbers in brackets; every module is a file in `src/ch09/` and every width below is a shipped port.

| Module | Steps | Owns | Instantiates |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fp32_unpack` | 1 | field decode ×2, effective exponent, hidden bit | `fp32_fields` ×2 (ch07) |
| `fp32_screen` | 2 | special-case result + `invalid` | `fp32_class` ×2 (ch07) |
| `fp32_swap` | 3 | magnitude compare, operand ordering, `eff_sub` | — |
| `fp32_align` | 4 | exponent difference, 5-bit clamp at 26, G/R/S capture | `align_sticky #(24,5)` (ch02) |
| `fp32_addsub` | 5 | 27-bit add/sub, sticky borrow, `exact_zero` | — |
| `fp32_normalize` | 6–7 | right-1 / none / left-N, LZC, subnormal stop | — |
| `fp32_round_pack` | 8–9 | RNE decision, rounding-carry renormalize, pack, `ovf` | — |
| `fp32_add2` (top) | 10 | screen mux, flag gating — *three assigns plus wiring* | all of the above |

**Each module's invariant, stated as a sentence a testbench can check.** `fp32_unpack`: every finite operand leaves as `(-1)^s · sig · 2^(e-127-23)` with no subnormal special case anywhere downstream — `e` is `max(E, 1)`, `sig` is `{hidden, F}` (chapter 7's "The Hidden Bit and the Subnormal Ramp", now a port contract). `fp32_screen`: `screen` is high iff any operand is NaN, infinite or zero, and when it is high, `screen_res` *is* the final result — the datapath's output is garbage-but-ignored on those patterns, a "don't care under screen" that the X-propagation section cashes. `fp32_swap`: `{e_big, sig_big}` is the larger magnitude, so the alignment shift is always rightward, the subtract never borrows past the top, and the result's sign is `sign_big` — one invariant that makes every downstream width unsigned-safe. `fp32_align`: `{aligned, g, r}` plus the sticky bit is the small operand *exactly*, in round-bit units — nothing lost, only summarized. `fp32_addsub`: on a subtract, `sum27 ≤ big27`; and `exact_zero` implies G, R, S all zero (chapter 8's proof: sticky needs d ≥ 3, and then the aligned operand is under a quarter of the big one). `fp32_normalize`: either `nsig[23]` is set or `e_norm == 1` — the still-unnormalized case is exactly the gradual-underflow encoding. `fp32_round_pack`: owns the second renormalize, the one chapter 8 measured firing zero times in a million random pairs.

**The 28-bit budget flows through the ports.** Chapter 8's "The Width Budget: 28 Bits" proved that 24 significand bits + guard + round + carry — a 27-bit adder — with one sticky bit alongside is exactly enough evidence for the single rounding. In the split design that budget is not a comment, it is the port list: `fp32_align` emits `aligned[23:0], g, r, s`; `fp32_addsub` consumes them as `sml27 = {1'b0, aligned, g, r}` with `s` feeding only the borrow; `sum27[26:0]` plus the pass-through `s` crosses into `fp32_normalize`. One width in the design *grows* across a boundary: `e_norm` is 9 bits, because `e_big + 1` on a right-1 normalize can reach 255 and the rounding stage's own `+ round_renorm` can push its copy to 256, and overflow detection must see those values rather than their 8-bit wrap. The interface-hygiene section measures what happens when a reader wires that port to an 8-bit net — and why the same warning text can be fatal elsewhere.

**What the cuts will cost chapter 11.** A pipeline stage register holds every wire crossing its boundary, so the port map prices the cuts now. The screen side channel — `screen`, `screen_res[31:0]`, `invalid`, 34 bits — must travel with the data in every cut:

| Cut (after) | Datapath wires crossing | Bits | + screen side | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| unpack/screen/swap | `sign_big, e_big, e_sml, sig_big, sig_sml, eff_sub` | 66 | 34 | **100** |
| align | `sign_big, e_big, eff_sub, sig_big, aligned, g, r, s` | 61 | 34 | **95** |
| addsub | `sign_big, e_big, eff_sub, sum27, s, exact_zero` | 39 | 34 | **73** |
| normalize | `sign_big, exact_zero, nsig, ng, nr, ns, e_norm` | 38 | 34 | **72** |

(Simple sums over the built design's ports, machine-checked this session — counts, not estimates.) Two observations for chapter 11 to inherit: the widest cut is the first, where both significands are still alive; and the cheapest cuts sit right after the deepest logic — the 27-bit subtract, and the LZC-plus-shift — which is exactly where a pipeline wants them. `e_sml` and `sig_sml` die at the align boundary; `e_big` and `eff_sub` die after normalize, folded into `e_norm` and the flags.

## Interface Hygiene at the Boundaries, Measured

A decomposition buys its benefits by multiplying interfaces, and Verilog's interfaces are permissive: they copy bits, and almost nothing else is checked. Chapter 2 established the width rules abstractly ("Width and Signedness: The Rules That Will Break Your Adder"); this section re-measures them at *this design's own boundaries*, because every one of these traps is now a wiring mistake you can actually make between the seven files you just met.

**One warning text, two opposite outcomes.** Wire the 9-bit `e_norm` output to an 8-bit top-level net and Icarus says — with and without `-Wall`, byte-identical, exit 0 (chapter 2's "ungated" class, confirmed here; file-and-line prefixes trimmed):

```
warning: Port 9 (e_norm) of module fp32_normalize expects 9 bit(s), given 8.
       : Padding 1 high bits of the port.
```

Note the text: Icarus says "Padding" even though this connection *truncates* an output — the message describes the port-expression coercion, not the direction your data is flowing. Quote it as-is; do not paraphrase it into sense. Now the two experiments, both swept through the full 100,092-check equivalence harness this session. Narrowing `e_norm` to 8 bits: two warnings, and the sweep **passes**, 100,092/100,092 — the truncation happens to be value-preserving, because `e_norm` itself never exceeds 255; the ninth bit exists for the `+ round_renorm` headroom *inside* `fp32_round_pack`, where `e_rnd` reaches 256. Narrowing `sum27` to 26 bits: the *same* warning class, and the design is destroyed — the adder's carry bit is gone, and the sweep fails at once with `4b7fffff+3f800000: split 00000000/000 golden 4b800000/000` (16,777,215 + 1 comes out zero; 20,573 mismatches). The diagnostic text cannot tell the benign narrowing from the killer. That is why this guide's harness fails any `run` target with *any* compile output — a rule adopted back in chapter 6's review, earning its keep here: since no human can triage this warning reliably, the only sane policy is that it never ships.

**The misspelled one-bit net, three ways.** Widths cannot save you when the port is one bit wide. Typo the top's `.s_in(s_al)` into `.s_in(s_a1)` — one character — and measure:

| Configuration | Compile result |
|---|---|
| no `-Wall`, default nettype `wire` | **total silence, exit 0** |
| `-Wall`, default nettype `wire` | `warning: implicit definition of wire 's_a1'.`, exit 0 |
| `` `default_nettype none `` | ``error: Unable to bind wire/reg/memory `s_a1' in `fp32_add2'`` — and a second line naming the instance and port: `error: Failed to elaborate input port 's_in' expression (s_a1) in instance fp32_add2.u_norm of module: fp32_normalize.` — exit 2 |

The `implicit` warning is `-Wall`-gated (chapter 2 measured this; reconfirmed), so a reader compiling casually gets nothing at all. `` `default_nettype none `` converts the one silent-without-flags wiring bug into an elaboration error that tells you which instance and which port. Every module in this chapter carries the directive, and the build still compiles with zero output — the directive costs nothing when the wiring is right. What the typo does at *runtime* is this chapter's `!==` story, told in the composition section: the undriven wire reads `z`, becomes `x` in the normalize logic, and whether any testbench notices depends on one character in the comparison operator.

**Signedness does not travel through a port.** The shipped datapath is all-unsigned by construction — the swap-first structure makes every difference non-negative, which is *why* production adders swap first. To measure what the alternative costs, eight known-answer probes were built around the software-natural signed front end (`sd = ea - eb` as a signed 9-bit port, then `sd < 0`, `sd < lz`). All eight compile with **zero diagnostics at `-g2012 -Wall`**. Captured verbatim:

```
A bits: sd_signed=110011100 sd_plain=110011100 (same bits: 1)
B interp: sd_signed=-100  sd_plain=412
C  (sd_signed < 0) = 1   correct
D  (sd_plain  < 0) = 0   parent dropped 'signed': never true
E  (sd_signed[8:0] < 0) = 0   full-width part-select kills signedness
F  (sd_signed < lz) = 0   one unsigned operand poisons the compare
G  (sd_signed < $signed({4'b0,lz})) = 1  both signed: correct
H  $signed(ea)-$signed(eb) at ea=200 eb=100: -156 (true diff +100)
```

Four findings, in order of likelihood: (1) a submodule's `output signed` declaration means nothing in a parent that redeclares the receiving net as plain `wire` — same bits (A), opposite meaning (B vs D); each end of a connection interprets independently. (2) Even with the parent net correctly signed, `sd[8:0] < 0` — a full-width part-select, visually a no-op — is unsigned and never true (E): chapter 6's "Signedness in Verilog: Silent, All of It" biting at exactly this boundary. (3) One unsigned operand poisons a whole comparison (F): the 5-bit LZC count drags −100 up to 412; the fix is to widen-and-`$signed` the unsigned side (G). (4) `$signed(ea)` on a raw exponent field misreads half the format — every operand with magnitude ≥ 2 has E ≥ 128 and goes negative; the measured "difference" for E = 200, 100 is −156 (H). The conclusion is not a style preference: **an all-unsigned datapath removes four silent bug classes at once, and the swap is what buys it.**

## Unpack and Screen: Chapter 7's Contract, Cashed

Chapter 7 shipped `fp32_fields` and `fp32_class` with a promise attached: it called them a "Seed for chapters 9 and 12", and said "Chapter 9 instantiates both on the adder's operand path". This is that promise coming due. Chapter 8 *inlined* both — its equations were identical but its module was one blob — and the first two modules of the split un-inline them. Neither module below derives anything; each is wiring around a deliverable you already own, which is the entire point of having built the deliverables early.

`fp32_unpack` is chapter 7's field decode, twice, from `src/ch09/fp32_unpack.v`:

```verilog
module fp32_unpack
  (input  wire [31:0] a,
   input  wire [31:0] b,
   output wire        sa,
   output wire        sb,
   output wire [7:0]  ea,      // effective exponent max(E,1)
   output wire [7:0]  eb,
   output wire [23:0] siga,    // {hidden, F}
   output wire [23:0] sigb);

  wire [7:0]  e_raw_a, e_raw_b;
  wire [22:0] fr_a, fr_b;
  wire        hid_a, hid_b;

  fp32_fields u_fa (.w(a), .sign(sa), .e_raw(e_raw_a), .frac(fr_a),
                    .hidden(hid_a), .sig(siga), .e_eff(ea));
  fp32_fields u_fb (.w(b), .sign(sb), .e_raw(e_raw_b), .frac(fr_b),
                    .hidden(hid_b), .sig(sigb), .e_eff(eb));

endmodule
```

The `max(E, 1)` decode and the hidden bit live inside `fp32_fields` and are *tested there* — this module's unit-level obligation is discharged by chapter 7's `tb_fields`, which is why it has no unit testbench of its own here. Note the three unused-output wires (`e_raw_a`, `fr_a`, `hid_a` and their `b` twins): named-port instantiation requires somewhere to land every output you connect, Icarus's `-Wall` says nothing about the ones you never read, and leaving them visible is more honest than the `.port()` empty-connection form, which would hide which fields this design chose not to use.

`fp32_screen` is the classifier cashed the same way. Its decision logic, from `src/ch09/fp32_screen.v`:

```verilog
  wire [31:0] nan_src = a_nan ? a : b;      // propagate a payload, quieted
  assign screen  = a_nan | b_nan | a_inf | b_inf | a_zero | b_zero;
  assign screen_res =
      (a_nan | b_nan)              ? {nan_src[31], 8'd255, 1'b1, nan_src[21:0]} :
      (a_inf & b_inf & (sa ^ sb))  ? 32'h7FC00000 :
      a_inf                        ? a :
      b_inf                        ? b :
      (a_zero & b_zero)            ? ((sa == sb) ? a : 32'h00000000) :
      a_zero                       ? b : a;
  assign invalid = a_snan | b_snan | (a_inf & b_inf & (sa ^ sb));
```

where `a_nan`, `a_snan`, `a_inf`, `a_zero` and the `b` set are the one-hot outputs of two `fp32_class` instances — the classifier's contract supplying exactly the terms `invalid` needs. Chapter 8 taught this table's semantics (NaN beats infinity, `inf + (−inf)` invents `7FC00000` and raises invalid, unlike-signed zeros give +0); nothing here changed, and the ported corner library re-proves every row against this module.

The structural decision worth defending is that **the screen is a sibling of the datapath, not a stage of it**. It takes the raw packed operands — not unpacked fields — and produces `screen`/`screen_res`/`invalid` with no dependence on anything downstream; the top's final mux (`result = screen ? screen_res : dp_res`) is golden step 10 verbatim. This mirrors the golden module's control/arithmetic split, gives chapter 11 a screen path it can pipeline as a thin 34-bit side channel, and creates the "don't care under screen" region that both the masking section and the X-propagation section below turn into measured lessons: when `screen` is high, the datapath computes garbage, and *that is fine* — until you write a testbench or a second driver that assumes otherwise.

## Swap and Align: The First Unit Testbench

`fp32_swap` is golden step 3 with one interface decision worth explaining. Like unpack and screen, it deliberately gets no unit testbench: the corner library and the equivalence sweep kill swap mutants on contact — an inverted magnitude compare dies on the first subnormal corner vector — so its coverage contract is the integration suite, and saying so out loud is the point. Its body, from `src/ch09/fp32_swap.v`:

```verilog
  wire swap = (b[30:0] > a[30:0]);
  assign sign_big = swap ? sb   : sa;
  assign e_big    = swap ? eb   : ea;
  assign e_sml    = swap ? ea   : eb;
  assign sig_big  = swap ? sigb : siga;
  assign sig_sml  = swap ? siga : sigb;
  assign eff_sub  = sa ^ sb;
```

The compare runs on the **packed** magnitude bits, so this module takes both the packed words and the unpacked fields — a wider port list than the "clean" alternative of comparing `{eb, sigb} > {ea, siga}` on unpacked fields alone. Both versions were built, and both are bit-identical to the golden module over the full equivalence sweep (the `{e, sig}` variant re-passed the 92-corner + 100,000-random sweep this session). The equivalence is also provable: python3 checked that the decode map `{E, F} → {max(E,1), |E, F}` is strictly monotone over all 2,048 (E, F) pairs at a 3-bit fraction — `strictly monotone over all 2048 (E,F): True` — and the fraction width cannot disturb the argument, since F enters both comparison keys identically. The chapter ships the packed compare anyway: it matches the golden text line-for-line (chapter 7's encoded-order property, no lemma required), and when two interfaces are provably equal, the one whose correctness is *visible* wins.

`fp32_align` wraps chapter 2's `align_sticky` — the module "Reduction Operators and the Sticky Bit" built before a floating point number existed, which has since carried chapter 8's million-pair sweep. The wrapper, from `src/ch09/fp32_align.v`:

```verilog
  wire [7:0] d     = e_big - e_sml;
  wire [4:0] shamt = (d > 8'd26) ? 5'd26 : d[4:0];

  align_sticky #(.W(24), .SHW(5)) u_core
    (.mant(sig_sml), .shamt(shamt),
     .aligned(aligned), .guard(g), .round(r), .sticky(s));
```

Why is a two-line wrapper a module at all, when chapter 11 will probably register align and addsub as one stage? Two reasons, one of them measured later. First, this boundary is where the 8-bit exponent difference (0..253 — never negative, by the swap invariant) is clamped into the 5-bit shift count: chapter 2's full-width-localparam lesson made into two lines that deserve their own unit tests, because deleting the clamp makes d = 200 *wrap* to a shift of 8. Second, the boundary between align and addsub is where the G/R bit-packing convention lives — and the masking section measures exactly that convention being violated with both unit suites green. A boundary that can host that bug should be a named, documented boundary.

**The unit testbench** — the chapter's first, and the pattern for all three. `tb_align_u.v` follows chapter 4's "The Anatomy of a Testbench" shape (time-based watchdog, task-per-check, self-checking, `$fatal` on any error) plus chapter 5's vacuous-pass guard: it counts checks and fails on the wrong literal. Its nine vectors are chosen *from the contract*, not from convenience: d = 0, 1, 2, 3 walk one live bit through G, then R, then into sticky; d = 25 parks the significand's *leading* bit in R — chapter 8's clamp-boundary lesson, now checkable in one line; d = 26, 27 and 200 pin the saturation (200 is the wrap-killer); and one vector puts the only sticky contributor in bit 0. Every expected constant was computed by an independent python3 model first and pasted in — never read off the DUT. The run prints `PASS tb_align_u (9 directed)`, and the mutation record backs it: five align mutants tried, four killed each by a single directed vector — clamp mis-thresholded (killed at d = 25), clamp deleted (killed at d = 200: `got 00c90f/111`), difference reversed (killed at d = 1), guard/round connections swapped (killed at d = 1: `got 6487ed/010 want 6487ed/100`). The fifth mutant — clamp value 27 instead of 26 — **survived, and the survival is the lesson**: `align_sticky` saturates internally at W + 2 = 26, so the wrapper's clamp and the core's saturation overlap by one, and the mutant is output-equivalent (confirmed over the full 100,092-check sweep, not assumed). The wrapper *believes* it owns the invariant `shamt ≤ 26`; only the core actually enforces the behaviour. A mutant that violates the spec without violating any output is a spec-versus-output gap, and the honest fixes are an assertion or a comment naming which clamp is load-bearing — the shipped file carries the comment.

## The Adder Module: 28 Bits, One Borrow

`fp32_addsub` is the width budget made a module. Its entire body, from `src/ch09/fp32_addsub.v`:

```verilog
  wire [26:0] big27 = {1'b0, sig_big, 2'b00};
  wire [26:0] sml27 = {1'b0, aligned, g, r};
  assign sum27 = eff_sub ? (big27 - sml27 - {26'd0, s})
                         : (big27 + sml27);
  assign exact_zero = eff_sub & (sum27 == 27'd0);
```

Four lines, and every one of them carries a chapter-8 theorem. The 27-bit frames are the budget laid out as bits: the 24-bit significand in bits 25:2, G and R below it, and one bit of carry headroom on top. Sticky passes **around** this module, not through the adder bits — `s` enters only as the borrow (chapter 8's "The Effective Operation and the Sticky Borrow": when sticky is set on a subtract, the truncated difference is one too big and still inexact, so sticky both borrows and survives, unchanged, to the normalizer). And `exact_zero` is gated on `eff_sub`, because a zero *sum* on an effective add means both operands were zero — a screened case whose signed-zero rule the screen owns.

The unit testbench, `tb_addsub_u.v`, is six directed vectors: a plain add, carry-out at maximal operands, a subtract with G live, the sticky borrow (expected `0FFFFFC` — one *less* than the truncated difference, the theorem in a constant), an exact zero, and a zero-operand ADD where `exact_zero` must stay 0. That last vector looks pedantic. Hold the thought: it is the only thing in this chapter standing between you and a latent bug that 100,092 end-to-end checks provably cannot see, and the masking section is built around it. Mutations against this bench: borrow dropped — killed, `got 0fffffd/0 want 0fffffc/0`; small operand packed `{aligned, r, g}` — killed on the G-live subtract; big operand packed `{2'b00, sig_big, 1'b0}` — killed on the plain add; `exact_zero` gate dropped — killed by the zero-ADD vector, `got 0000000/1 want 0000000/0`. Four one-vector kills; each message names the field that lied.

What the bench deliberately does **not** do is drive the full input cross. `eff_sub = 1` with a `sum27` that would carry out of bit 26 violates the upstream swap invariant — on an effective subtract the difference can never set bit 26 — and the contract has no expected output there. This matters because the corresponding normalize mutant (`right1 = carry`, ignoring `eff_sub`) is a **survivor** in the mutation record, and legitimately so: a unit testbench that drove the impossible input to kill it would have to *invent* expected values for inputs the contract excludes, and would then fail correct implementations. Unit tests test the contract, not the type signature. When a survivor's explanation is "equivalent under an upstream invariant," write the invariant down at the port where it holds — the shipped `fp32_normalize.v` header does — and re-verify the equivalence mechanically, which the record did over the full sweep.

Compare the economics with chapter 8's for the same bug class. Its mutation M16 — the right-1 sticky fold dropped, the same class as this chapter's N-M1 — survived chapter 8's entire first-draft directed library and died only under random stimulus, forcing a campaign fix. At module level, the same class dies in **one deliberately chosen vector**: `tb_normround_u` drives `s_in = 0` with `sum27[0] = 1`, making the fold the *only* sticky source, and the mutant cannot hide (`got 800000/000/101 want 800000/001/101`). That asymmetry — end-to-end-expensive bugs becoming unit-cheap when you can drive the boundary directly — is the working argument for unit testbenches, and it recurs one module downstream, where the hardest coverage problem in the design becomes a constant you just write down.

## Normalize, Round, and the Latch Simulation Cannot See

`fp32_normalize` is golden steps 6–7 behind a port: three mutually exclusive outcomes (right-1 on carry, none, left-N after cancellation) and the subnormal stop. The selection logic, from `src/ch09/fp32_normalize.v`:

```verilog
  wire [4:0]  lz     = lzc26(frame);
  wire [7:0]  e_lim  = e_big - 8'd1;
  wire [7:0]  shl8   = ({3'b000, lz} > e_lim) ? e_lim : {3'b000, lz};
  wire [4:0]  shl    = shl8[4:0];
  wire [25:0] framel = frame << shl;

  assign nsig   = right1 ? sum27[26:3]       : framel[25:2];
  assign ng     = right1 ? sum27[2]          : framel[1];
  assign nr     = right1 ? sum27[1]          : framel[0];
  assign ns     = right1 ? (s_in | sum27[0]) : s_in;
  assign e_norm = right1 ? ({1'b0, e_big} + 9'd1)
                         : ({1'b0, e_big} - {4'b0000, shl});
```

The LZC stays inside as the same `lzc26` function — making it a module is defensible for reuse, but adds nothing to a 2-input adder; it is the piece a wider datapath would extract first. `fp32_round_pack` is steps 8–9, from `src/ch09/fp32_round_pack.v`:

```verilog
  wire        round_up     = ng & (nr | ns | lbit);
  wire [24:0] rsig         = {1'b0, nsig} + {24'd0, round_up};
  wire        round_renorm = rsig[24];
  wire [23:0] fsig         = round_renorm ? 24'h800000 : rsig[23:0];
  wire [8:0]  e_rnd        = e_norm + {8'd0, round_renorm};

  assign ovf = (e_rnd > 9'd254);
  assign dp_res =
      exact_zero ? 32'h00000000 :
      ovf        ? {sign, 8'd255, 23'd0} :
      fsig[23]   ? {sign, e_rnd[7:0], fsig[22:0]} :
                   {sign, 8'd0,       fsig[22:0]};
  assign inexact_dp = ovf | ng | nr | ns;
```

Their shared unit testbench, `tb_normround_u.v`, drives both modules independently — their boundary is a chapter 11 register cut, so each side holds its own contract. Its sixteen vectors include the two that repay the whole exercise. First, `nsig = FFFFFF` with G and R set: **the rounding-carry renormalize, as a constant you just write down.** End to end, chapter 8 measured this path firing 0 times in 1,000,000 random pairs; at module level it is one line, checked against `E4800000`. Unit testing turns the design's hardest coverage problem into a trivially reachable input — though not a dischargeable one: the composed design still needs its coverage gate, held by the regression section. Second, `nsig = 800000` exact at `e_norm = 255`: overflow whose sum was exact, where `inexact` must fire anyway because the delivered infinity is never the exact sum. Two of the round mutants die on these vectors *flag-only* — result bits identical, one flag wrong — and one of them (overflow threshold 255 instead of 254) was probed further: at `nsig = C00000` exact, it packs `7FC00000`, a **fabricated qNaN**, with both flags low. A unit testbench that checked result bits only would have shipped it. Flags are ports; test them like ports.

**Now the trap this section exists for.** The shipped normalize is all-`assign`, and so is the whole design — no `always` block anywhere. To measure what that discipline is worth, the module was rewritten procedurally (`always @*`, `reg` outputs, if/else-if) with a realistic missing arm: no branch for `frame == 0`, on the reasoning "exact_zero handles that downstream." Chapter 3's "The Accidental Latch" told you Icarus reports nothing for this; confirmed at this design's exact shape — `-g2012 -Wall`, exit 0, zero output. The simulator will not catch this class. What it *does* at runtime is worse than chapter 3's story:

- **Module level**: the same input produces different outputs depending on history — `frame = 0` gave `nsig=800000 grs=000 e=92` after one predecessor and `nsig=ffffff grs=101 e=99` after another (captured four-step trace).
- **End to end**: the full equivalence sweep fails just **4 checks of 100,092 — all flag-only, all exact-cancellation corners, 0 kills in 100,000 random pairs**: `3fc00000+bfc00000: split 00000000/001 golden 00000000/000`. The result bits are rescued by the `exact_zero` mux; only `inexact` betrays the latch. A results-only regression certifies a latch.
- **The latched value is a delta-cycle glitch, not the previous vector's settled state.** Instrumented this session (an event-count line between the two experiments is elided): every settled evaluation before the failing cancel had `grs=000`, yet the latch held `grs=110` — a snapshot of the combinational cascade mid-settle, corresponding to *no settled input vector*:

```
settled prev : grs=000 e_norm=128
settled mid  : grs=000 e_norm=127  (a=1.5, b=-0)
after cancel : grs=110 e_norm=127  <- latched
b-first mid  : grs=000 e_norm=128  (a=-3.0, b=-1.5)
b-first final: grs=000 e_norm=125  <- latched (different!)
```

Reversing the order of two blocking assignments in the testbench's driver task (`b` before `a`) latched a *different* state — one whose `inexact` happens to be correct, so that stimulus ordering **hides the bug entirely**. A latch turns a combinational vector list into a sequence, and PASS/FAIL can hinge on the order of two assignments inside a driver task. Chapter 3's picture of a latch — the block holds the value it had on the previous row — is the weaker true statement; a latch that remembers whatever the cascade was mid-settle is the measured one. The defence is not tooling — no tool here fires — it is shape: complete if/else or default assignments in every procedural block, or, as this datapath chooses, no procedural blocks at all.

One more sensitivity fact, measured because `lzc26` is a function: Icarus's `always @*` does not see through a function body to non-argument reads. A function with one live argument that also reads a module-level variable compiles with zero diagnostics, then goes *stale* when only the global changes — `shl=4 (want 2)` — and silently catches up on the next argument change. The same probe under `always_comb` re-evaluates correctly (`t2: shl=2 (want 2)`): Icarus 13.0 really implements the IEEE 1800 sensitivity distinction, which sharpens chapter 3's verdict on `always_comb` — that a reader working only in Icarus gets "the documentation and none of the enforcement" — by exactly one dimension. This guide's rule stays simpler than the standardese: **everything a datapath function reads arrives as an argument** — `lzc26(frame)` complies — and the full `always_comb` story belongs to chapter 13's SystemVerilog treatment.

## Composition, and the Golden-Equivalence Sweep

The top module is deliberately boring. `fp32_add2` declares the boundary wires, instantiates the seven modules by name (`u_unpack` through `u_round` — the instance names the coverage bins will need), and owns exactly this much logic, from `src/ch09/fp32_add2.v`:

```verilog
  assign result   = screen ? screen_res : dp_res;
  assign invalid  = inv_scr;
  assign overflow = ~screen & ovf;
  assign inexact  = ~screen & inx_dp;
```

Golden step 10, and nothing else: the screen mux, two flag gates, one pass-through. That is the discipline worth naming — **a top module is wiring**. Every piece of arithmetic that could hide in a top-level expression is arithmetic no unit testbench covers; three auditable assigns is the whole budget this design allows itself.

Wiring is now the product, so the chapter's central verification move targets exactly the wiring: `tb_equiv.v` instantiates chapter 8's `fp32_add_alg` and this chapter's `fp32_add2` side by side, drives both with the same operands, and compares `{result, invalid, overflow, inexact}` — 36 bits, bit-exact, **NaN sign included**. That last clause deserves a beat, because chapter 7 forbade comparing a generated NaN's sign. The prohibition was about *simulator-generated* NaNs, whose sign IEEE 754-2019 leaves unspecified and this project's two hosts have disagreed on. Here both sides are deterministic RTL computing the same NaN-propagation rule, so full equality is not just allowed, it is the point — and checking flags and payloads bit-exactly is precisely what the value-domain reference in the regression section cannot do. Stimulus is chapter 8's 46-pair corner library in both orders (92 checks, guarded by a literal count so a truncated vector list cannot pass vacuously) followed by its five-regime random generator, seeded once with the first draw discarded per chapter 5's rule, seed echoed. The shipped bench runs 100,000 random pairs in 20.6 s on this container; the same bench at 200,000 ran 40.8 s this session —

```
SEED=9090
PASS tb_equiv (92 corner checks + 200000 random, split == golden bit-for-bit incl. flags)
```

**200,092 checks, zero mismatches, result bits and all three flags.** The shipped target stays at 100,092 because 40.8 s pushes against the harness's 60 s simulation timeout, and the corner phase carries the rare paths either way.

The comparison itself is where this chapter pays for a toolchain lesson with a body count. Here is the check task's core, from `src/ch09/tb_equiv.v`:

```verilog
      // X-guard first: a fully-driven combinational design fed defined
      // operands has NO excuse for an x or z anywhere in its outputs.
      if ((^{r_s, inv_s, ovf_s, inx_s}) === 1'bx ||
          (^{r_g, inv_g, ovf_g, inx_g}) === 1'bx) begin
        mismatches = mismatches + 1;
        if (mismatches <= 10)
          $display("FAIL tb_equiv %h+%h: X in outputs: split %h/%b%b%b golden %h/%b%b%b",
                   a, b, r_s, inv_s, ovf_s, inx_s, r_g, inv_g, ovf_g, inx_g);
      end else if ({r_s, inv_s, ovf_s, inx_s} !== {r_g, inv_g, ovf_g, inx_g}) begin
```

Two defences, both load-bearing, both proven by demonstration this session. Recall the interface-hygiene section's one-character typo — `.s_in(s_a1)` — in a top without `` `default_nettype none ``: it compiles silently, the undriven wire reads `z`, and the normalize logic turns it into `x` in `ns` and the flags. Against that broken design, the shipped bench fails immediately and loudly, first kill `3fc00000+bfc00000: X in outputs: split 00000000/00x golden 00000000/000` — 60,980 X-contaminated mismatches in 100,092. Now weaken the comparison to `!=` **and** delete the X-guard, and run the same broken design: **`PASS tb_equiv (92 corner checks + 100000 random, ...)`** — a false pass across every check, captured this session. The mechanism is chapter 4's `!==` rule ("Checking, and Making Failure Impossible to Miss") with measured teeth: every mismatch this bug can produce is X-contaminated, `X != X` evaluates to `x`, an `if` on `x` does not take, and the weak comparison misses *all* of them. The second body: a plausible integration accident — a leftover bring-up driver `assign result = dp_res;` alongside the screen mux — compiles in total silence at `-Wall` (chapter 3's multi-driver table, confirmed at this composition), agrees with the real driver on every unscreened vector, and fails exactly 168 of 100,092 checks, every one a screened vector, every one partially-X (`split 7fX000XX` — per-bit wire resolution keeps agreeing bits defined). The weak-`!=` bench **passes that design too**. Two compile-silent composition bugs, two false green runs from one character. The shipped bench keeps both defences so that no single future edit can reopen the hole: with the X-guard in place, even the `!==`→`!=` weakening is caught (measured — the guard alone kills the typo design). Chapters 2 through 5 taught `!==` as a rule; this chapter can finally teach it as a measurement.

One honesty note about what this sweep proves: **equivalence, not correctness.** It proves the refactoring preserved chapter 8's function — flags, NaN details and all — and it inherits every property chapter 8's own million-pair exact-arithmetic campaign established. What it cannot do is catch a bug present in *both* adders, and the regression section measures exactly that scenario. One sweep is not a verification strategy; it is one leg of a tripod.

## Masking, Measured in Both Directions

If every module has a unit testbench and the composition is proven bit-identical to a million-pair-verified golden model, is either level redundant? This section answers with four measurements, and the answer is the chapter's thesis: **each level kills bugs the other provably cannot see.**

**Direction one: a unit-killed bug the end-to-end sweep can never see.** Take mutant B-M2 — `exact_zero = (sum27 == 27'd0)` with the `eff_sub` gate dropped. `tb_addsub_u` kills it in one vector, the pedantic-looking zero-operand ADD. Then the same mutant was placed in the full design and run through the complete equivalence sweep: **PASS, 100,092 of 100,092** (and in the research prototype, the full 200,092). The mask is structural, not statistical: `eff_sub = 0` with `sum27 = 0` requires both significands zero, which only zero operands produce — and zero operands are screened, so the datapath's wrong `exact_zero` is multiplexed into oblivion before any output can show it. No black-box campaign of *any* size sees this bug. The composed design is provably immune today, and the module is still wrong against its own contract. That combination has a name — a **latent interface bug** — and a schedule: chapter 10 reuses `fp32_addsub` in a wider datapath, which is exactly when latencies surface. The unit testbench is not redundancy; it is the only witness this bug has.

**Direction two: both unit suites green, integration red.** Constructed scenario, fully measured: the author of `fp32_addsub` believes the port named `g` carries the *second* bit shifted out and `r` the first, packs `sml27 = {1'b0, aligned, r, g}` — and writes their own unit testbench under the same reading, drives it, expects accordingly. Captured this session:

```
PASS tb_addsub_wrong (6 directed, author convention)
PASS tb_align_u (9 directed)
FAIL tb_equiv 3f800000+33800001: split 3f800000/001 golden 3f800001/001
```

Each suite is self-consistent; the *composition* is wrong wherever G ≠ R matters — 19,795 mismatches in 100,092, first killed by the corner library's deep-sticky vector. This is the measured answer to "why keep the equivalence sweep when every module is unit-tested": **unit tests verify each author's reading of an interface; only an integration reference verifies that the readings agree.** The cheap insurance is naming the convention in the port list at both ends — the shipped `fp32_align.v` and `fp32_addsub.v` headers both say "g is the FIRST bit shifted out" — and the sweep is the enforcement.

**The two survivors sharpen what "kill" means.** A-M5 (align clamp at 27) and N-M4 (`right1 = carry`) survive their unit benches *and* the full sweep, and both survivals were verified as genuine equivalences rather than assumed: one because ch02's core saturates one step past the wrapper's clamp, one because the swap invariant makes the distinguishing input impossible. A mutant that survives your stimulus is a gap in your stimulus (chapter 4's lesson) *only until you prove it equivalent* — then it is a documentation obligation instead.

The full matrix, every row run this session:

| Bug | Unit TB | 100k end-to-end | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| B-M2 (`exact_zero` gate) | kills | passes | latent — only unit-visible |
| A-M5 (clamp 27) | survives | passes | equivalent; spec-vs-output gap |
| N-M4 (`right1 = carry`) | survives | passes | equivalent under the swap invariant |
| G/R convention (both authors consistent) | passes ×2 | kills | only integration-visible |
| RNE `lbit` dropped in BOTH adders | — | `tb_equiv` passes; `tb_short` kills | only oracle-visible (see "The Regression: Corners Carried Forward, Oracle Kept Independent") |

The last row is the third leg. Nothing above protects against a bug planted in both the golden model and the split — the equivalence sweep would certify it cheerfully — which is why the regression keeps a reference that is independent of chapter 8 entirely. Unit suites, integration sweep, independent oracle: three levels, three distinct blindness patterns, no pair sufficient. That is the verification thesis of this chapter, and from here on in the guide it is assumed rather than argued.

## X-Propagation: The Wrong Answer That Looks Right

The equivalence sweep's X-guard earned its place against wiring bugs; this section asks the complementary question — when an X arrives at the *operands*, what does this datapath do with it? The folklore answer is "X spreads and you see it." The measured answer is more useful and less comforting. One X was injected at chosen bit positions and traced hierarchically at each module boundary, this session:

| Injected X | swap | sum27 | result | flags |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none (clean 2.0 + 1.0000001) | 0 | `3000002` | `40400000` | `001` |
| X in b's fraction LSB | **x** | all-x | `Xxxxxxxx` | `0xx` |
| X in b's exponent MSB | x | all-x | `Xxxxxxxx` | `xxx` |
| X in b's SIGN bit only | 0 | `XxxxxxX` | **`34000000` — fully defined** | **`000`** |
| a = qNaN `7FC00055`, b = all-X | x | all-x | `7fc00055` — perfectly clean | `x00` |
| a = +inf, b = all-X | x | all-x | `XfXxxxxx` | `x00` |

Four findings worth owning. **First, the magnitude compare is the design's X chokepoint**: one X in the fraction LSB makes `b[30:0] > a[30:0]` return `x` even though the comparison's outcome does not depend on that bit, and the swap muxes spread the `x` everywhere — classic X-pessimism, ugly but at least *visible*. **Second — the row this section is named for — an X in just the sign bit produces a fully defined-looking wrong answer with clean flags.** The mechanism: `eff_sub = sa ^ sb` goes `x`, the add/sub ternary evaluates both arms and bit-merges them (bits where add and subtract agree stay defined), and the LZC's `if (v[i] == 1'b1)` treats `x` as false — X-optimism in `if` — so a defined-looking `nsig` emerges and packs into `34000000` with flags `000`. Nothing about that output says "an X went in." **Third, X-immunity of the specials is a mux-priority accident**: the qNaN row comes out clean against an all-X partner because `a_nan` sits in the screen's first mux arm and dominates; +inf, sitting behind two x-valued conditions in the chain, comes out contaminated. If a later chapter ever wants X-robust special handling, priority order is the knob. **Fourth, flags contaminate before results do** — the qNaN row's result is clean while `invalid` is `x` — one more reason the flag ports are first-class citizens of every check in this guide.

The practical consequence for testbench authors is the one the sign-bit row forces: **an X-injection test that greps its outputs for visible `x` is testing the wrong property.** The dangerous propagation mode produces defined wrong values, so an X-injection test must check outputs against *expected values* (or an X-clean reference run), exactly as any other test does — the X-guard catches the advertised failures, and only a value check catches the quiet ones. This design's actual protection for the qNaN/inf/zero rows is not X-robustness at all: it is the screen invariant. `screen_res` is computed from the raw operands by its own logic, so whenever `screen` is high the datapath's X-storm is multiplexed away — "don't care under screen," now visible as the mechanism that kept row five clean. The X never needs to be *stopped*; it needs to be provably ignored. That is an invariant argument, not a simulation argument, which is a preview of this chapter's closing theme: the properties that matter most here are the ones simulation alone cannot establish.

## The Regression: Corners Carried Forward, Oracle Kept Independent

Three more testbenches complete the chapter's kit, and each carries something forward from an earlier chapter with its own measured twist.

**The corner library ports mechanically — and its brittleness is a feature.** `tb_corners_split.v` is chapter 8's `tb_corners.v` with the DUT swapped to `fp32_add2`: the 46 pairs, both orders, the python-exact expected values and the 92-check guard are unchanged. What changed is six of the thirteen hierarchical coverage-bin references, re-aimed into the submodule instances the refactor moved those wires into — `dut.right1` becomes `dut.u_norm.right1`, `dut.round_renorm` becomes `dut.u_round.round_renorm`, `dut.shamt` becomes `dut.u_align.shamt`, and so on; the other seven bins sample wires that are top-level in the split and survive verbatim. The ported bench runs 92/92 with all bins hit, captured this session:

```
bins: right1=12 left>=2=8 round_renorm=8 sticky_sat=8
      tie_up=12 tie_down=4 subnormal_res=6 overflow_res=6
PASS tb_corners_split (46 pairs x both orders, 8/8 coverage bins hit)
```

`round_renorm = 8` is the four directed reachers times both orders — and those four vectors matter enough to name: `4B800000+B3800000`, `4B000000+BDFFFFFF`, `3FFFFFFF+33800000`, `7F7FFFFF+73000000`. Chapter 8's standing measurement is that the rounding-carry renormalize fired **0 times in 1,000,000 random pairs**; random will never replace these vectors, so the regression carries them explicitly and the empty-bin gate proves they still reach. Proof that the gate itself executes, re-measured on the split (chapter 8's T3c discipline): delete exactly those four vectors, dutifully adjust the count guard to 84, and the run fails — `bins: ... round_renorm=0 sticky_sat=4` then `FAIL tb_corners_split: a required coverage bin is empty`. Two more properties of the port are worth a sentence each. A *stale* hierarchical reference cannot fail silently: compiling a bench that still says `dut.right1` against the split top is an elaboration error naming every missing wire, because hierarchical references cannot become implicit nets — so a refactor can break the coverage gate only by *stopping the build*, which is the good direction. And the alternative design — exporting `right1`, `shl`, `round_renorm`, `shamt` as observation ports to make the bins boundary-visible — was deliberately not taken: the hierarchical route works, keeps the synthesizable interface minimal, and chapter 11 will re-time the bins anyway when the boundaries acquire registers. Re-aiming bins is the normal maintenance cost of a decomposition; budget for it.

**The independent oracle, licensed form.** `tb_short.v` is chapter 8's "The Reference Model Verdict for Chapter 9", executed: `$bitstoshortreal` each operand, ONE `+`, one `$shortrealtobits` — never a second operation in the real domain, per chapter 5's double-rounding trap — NaN-vs-NaN compared by class only. 100,000 five-regime pairs, zero mismatches, 10.6 s this session, with the exclusions re-pinned at the new top level:

```
  sNaN+1.0: dut=7fe00055 inv=1 (payload kept, quieted; sign not asserted)
  sNaN+1.0 via shortreal ref = 7fe00055 (host quiets; class NaN)
  sNaN pure round-trip = 7fe00000 (expect 7fe00000: quieted with NO arithmetic)
```

The sNaN operand is built from bits (`32'h7FA00055`), never sent through the value domain — a shortreal round-trip quiets an sNaN with no arithmetic at all (chapter 7, re-measured here and asserted in the bench). On this container the host FPU happens to agree with the DUT's quieted payload and sign; the bench is *not* tightened to that, because NaN sign remains never-comparable against a simulator-generated NaN.

**Why ship two references that check the same adder?** Because they discharge different obligations, and the difference was measured, not argued. The same bug — RNE's `lbit` term dropped, ties-to-even degraded to ties-down — was planted in **both** `fp32_add_alg` and the split's rounder. `tb_equiv` passed all 100,092 checks against the doubly-wrong pair; `tb_short` killed it **6,890 times in 100,000** (6.9 %), first kill `196ad232+98baf931: dut 190d5599 ref 190d559a`. The equivalence sweep proves the *refactoring* preserved the function, including flags and NaN details the shortreal path cannot see (the reference has no flag outputs — `invalid`, `overflow` and `inexact` are simply unverifiable through it); the shortreal bench re-proves the *function* against an oracle that has never read chapter 8. The corner library, whose constants come from exact python arithmetic, is independent of both. Seed policy throughout is chapter 5's, verbatim: `$urandom` seeded **once** per simulation, first draw discarded, seed echoed so any failure reproduces.

The per-module intuition (a 27-bit ripple of full-adder cells; two 5-level 26-bit mux-tree shifters around 130 mux2 each; a 26-input priority encoder) is *consistent* with the sizer's operator inventory, but converting any of it into LUTs or nanoseconds requires a synthesizer this guide does not have. ## Traps Beginners Fall Into

Every entry below was demonstrated this session against this chapter's own design; nothing is folklore.

1. **Trusting the port-width warning to triage itself.** The identical "Padding 1 high bits" diagnostic covered a provably-benign narrowing (`e_norm` to 8 bits: sweep passes) and a catastrophic one (`sum27` to 26 bits: the adder's carry vanishes, 20,573 mismatches). You cannot read severity off the text. The only workable policy is the harness's: any compile output on a `run` target is a failure.

2. **Believing `!=` is close enough to `!==`.** One character made the equivalence sweep print PASS across 100,092 checks against a design with an undriven net, and again against a double-driven design. `X != X` is `x`, and an `if` on `x` does not take. Use `!==` everywhere, and put an X-guard (`^bus === 1'bx`-style) in front of it so no future weakening reopens the hole.

3. **Skipping `` `default_nettype none ``.** The one-bit typo `s_a1` compiles in total silence without `-Wall`, is a mere warning with it, and is an elaboration error naming the instance and port with the directive. At interfaces, one-bit nets are where width warnings cannot save you; the directive is the only compile-time defence measured to work.

4. **Assuming signedness crosses a port.** It does not — ports copy bits. A parent that redeclares a `signed` output as plain `wire` gets `sd < 0` never-true; a full-width part-select kills signedness even on a correctly-signed net; one unsigned operand poisons a whole comparison; `$signed(ea)` misreads every exponent ≥ 128. The all-unsigned, swap-first datapath removes the entire class.

5. **Writing a procedural normalizer with a missing arm.** Icarus reports nothing, the latch captures delta-cycle glitch state matching *no settled input*, the full sweep fails only 4 flag-only checks in 100,092 — and whether it fails at all can depend on the order of two blocking assignments in your driver task. No tool here catches this; the discipline (complete branches, default assignments, or all-`assign`) is the defence.

6. **Letting a datapath function read module-level variables.** `always @*` does not see through a function body: the output goes stale when only the global changes, with zero diagnostics, and silently catches up later. Pass everything as arguments.

7. **Testing result bits and skipping the flags.** Two round-stage mutants die *flag-only* — including one that fabricates a qNaN with clean flags in a case its killing vector doesn't cover — and the inferred latch is flag-only visible end to end. Flags are ports.

8. **Concluding a unit-test survivor is a testbench gap without proof — or with it.** Two survivors here are provable equivalences (clamp overlap; swap invariant). The right response is to *measure* the equivalence (both were re-swept), then document which module owns the invariant — not to invent expected values for impossible inputs, which would fail correct designs.

9. **Believing the equivalence sweep makes the unit suites redundant, or vice versa.** The `exact_zero` bug passes 100,092 end-to-end checks and dies in one unit vector; the G/R convention bug leaves both unit suites green and dies only in the sweep; the common-mode rounding bug passes the sweep and dies only against the independent oracle, 6,890 times in 100,000. Three levels, three blindness patterns.

10. **Expecting random to cover the rounding-carry renormalize.** Zero hits in a million pairs, standing measurement. The four named reachers plus an empty-bin coverage gate — shown to fire when they are removed — are the only guarantee the path runs.

## Bridge to Chapter 10

You now hold a 2-input adder as a *design*: seven contracts, a top that is wiring, and a three-legged verification kit whose blind spots are measured rather than suspected. Three chapters are already leaning on it.

**Chapter 10** builds the four-input adder, and chapter 8's "Order Is Part of the Answer" set its central fact: binary32 addition is not associative, so the design document must fix an association order and the reference must replicate it, add by add. What changes at the interfaces is the question this chapter equips you to ask. A tree of two `fp32_add2` instances is the obvious composition — but each interior edge then packs to binary32 and unpacks again, rounding at every node; whether the design keeps that, or passes wider intermediate state between stages, is a *port-contract* decision, and every technique here — the unit suites, the equivalence discipline, the masking matrix — reapplies to whatever contracts chapter 10 writes. And one specific debt comes due: `fp32_addsub`'s `exact_zero` gate is this chapter's worked example of a latent bug that only module reuse can surface. Chapter 10 reuses the module. Watch it.

**Chapter 11** pipelines this design, and its cut points are these module boundaries — 100, 95, 73 and 72 bits, screen side channel included, counted from the shipped port lists. The cheapest cuts sit just after the deepest logic, a coincidence it will exploit; the coverage bins will need re-timing when the boundaries acquire registers, exactly as they needed re-aiming when the wires moved into submodules here.

**Chapter 13**, on SystemVerilog, inherits a measured aside: Icarus 13.0's `always_comb` really does re-evaluate when a function's non-argument global changes, where `always @*` goes stale — the IEEE 1800 sensitivity distinction, live in this simulator, sharpening chapter 3's verdict by one dimension. Until then, this guide's rule stands: functions take arguments, datapaths take `assign`.

The next adder you build has four operands, three interior sums, and a reference model that must round-trip through the bits functions after every single add. You already own every module it will instantiate — and the testbench that will catch the first bug you compose into it.
